# Toast's $0 Offer and the Price of Leaving: What Will a Federal Court Find?

On October 29, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission, California and seven other states filed a civil lawsuit against Toast and its affiliates in federal court in Northern California. Toast supplies restaurants with the equipment and software used to take orders and payments, along with payment processing, add-ons such as payroll, and financing through Toast Capital. The FTC is the US agency responsible for consumer protection and competition, and it reported a 2–0 commission vote to bring the case. According to the plaintiffs, Toast charged hidden or unauthorized fees, put barriers in the way of cancellation and made misleading promises about support. These claims are allegations awaiting a judge's decision. In the material reviewed for this article, collected on October 30, Toast's response and the eventual ruling are both unknown.

The case begins with a piece of restaurant equipment most diners have seen. A point-of-sale system is the combination of machines and software that staff use to enter an order and take payment. It sits close to the center of a restaurant's working day, because food orders and incoming money both pass through it. Payment processing, the service that handles a customer's card payment, is closely tied to that system. Toast's other offerings extend the relationship further, with payroll among its add-ons and Toast Capital providing financing.

The appeal of having one supplier cover so much is easy to imagine, since an owner has fewer companies to deal with. The arrangement also raises a practical question, and this is this article's interpretation, separate from any measured finding in the case. When a single company touches equipment, payments, software and borrowed money, a decision to leave may involve all of them at once. The plaintiffs focus on particular disclosure and exit practices, and the court will examine those practices specifically. The central question for an owner is broader and simple to state: how clearly can anyone see the cost of joining, the cost of operating and the cost of leaving?

An everyday comparison helps. Imagine, purely as an illustration, a household that gets a phone, its monthly service and a loan for the handset from one provider. That household faces at least four numbers: the advertised price today, the monthly bill for usage, the charge for cancelling early and the balance still owed on the device. Each number has its own trigger, and a low figure for one says little about the others. Toast's contracts differ from phone plans, and a loan balance differs from a penalty. The comparison simply shows how linked obligations can produce several separate prices.

Three timelines also run through this case. The 99-cent online-order fee named in the lawsuit belongs to a period running from September 2021 through late July 2023. The complaint containing the subscription and exit allegations was filed in October 2026, and many of the individual operating dates remain unspecified in the material reviewed here. The third timeline, the court's, has only just begun. Keeping them apart matters, because the lawsuit's date is easy to mistake for the date of the fee, and the filing date alone leaves open which alleged practices were still active.

## The price of getting in

The complaint, a 71-page document filed as Case 3:26-cv-12705, is the plaintiffs' opening account of what they say happened. It tells one side of the dispute, and its publicly posted file name labels it as redacted. Among its most striking details is a Pay-as-you-Go offer, which the complaint describes as $0 per month for hardware and software, paired with higher transaction fees.

A transaction fee is a charge tied to the payments a business takes. A monthly subscription arrives on schedule like rent. A transaction fee behaves more like a toll, collected each time a car passes through. So $0 per month describes one column in the ledger, and the transaction fees fill another. Whether this plan was cheaper or more expensive for any particular restaurant remains an open question, because the fee rates and total costs are still unverified here.

The complaint goes on to allege conditions attached to the offer, each with its own trigger. If a restaurant had yet to start processing payments within 120 days, it could be required to return the hardware or pay its retail value. During a temporary closure, processing could cost $85 per month. Early termination could cost $150 for each month left on the contract.

Those units deserve careful reading. The 120-day provision sets a deadline with two possible outcomes. The $85 applies month by month during a closure. The $150 is a rate multiplied by the time remaining, so the total depends on when a restaurant leaves. As simple arithmetic, a hypothetical contract with 10 months left would produce $1,500 at that rate, and one with 20 months left would produce $3,000. Those totals only illustrate the alleged formula and stand apart from any actual customer's bill.

A pattern is visible here, offered as this article's interpretation. A plan that collects its money through transaction fees depends on a restaurant actively taking payments. A restaurant that has yet to begin processing, or has closed its doors for a while, produces few or zero such fees. The alleged conditions apply to exactly those moments. That reading explains how such an offer might be structured. Whether its terms were fairly and clearly presented is a matter for the court. These conditions are pleaded claims, unproven as terms for every customer, and the contract versions involved and the frequency of each condition remain open questions.

The complaint describes a different pricing arrangement as well. Annual or biennial software subscriptions, meaning contracts lasting one or two years, were billed monthly, at a figure described as roughly $900 per month on average, varying with the number of terminals and add-ons. Terminals are the individual devices used to take orders and payments. This figure belongs to those subscriptions and sits apart from the $0 offer, so the two should stay separate in any reader's mental ledger. What the average counts and how it was calculated remain unverified here.

## Ninety-nine cents at checkout

The smallest number in the case may be the easiest for a diner to picture. The plaintiffs describe a $0.99 fee on certain online orders above $10, charged from September 2021 through late July 2023 and retained by Toast. This is a historical charge covering a defined category of orders. The lawsuit is new, and the fee period ended more than three years before it was filed.

Paragraphs 69 through 73 of the complaint supply the details that give this fee its legal weight. They describe the fee's expansion by June 2023, its placement inside a line labeled "Taxes & Fees," and an additional disclosure through a tooltip, the small box of text that appears when a person hovers over or taps an item on a screen.

Those details explain why a charge of under a dollar can end up in a federal complaint. A checkout screen is a small space with a great deal of influence over what a customer understands. A line labeled "Taxes & Fees" groups several charges under one heading, and a customer glancing at it sees a combined category. A tooltip offers more information to someone who opens it. Both facts bear on what a diner could have seen, and both leave the larger question open. The legal dispute includes whether restaurants and customers knew about the fee and consented to it. "Hidden" is the plaintiffs' word. The court will have to weigh the wording of the disclosures, their timing and the screens as they appeared at each stage.

Small charges also grow with repetition. At 99 cents, a hypothetical 1,000 qualifying orders would come to $990. That figure is arithmetic for scale only. The real count of affected orders and the total Toast kept both remain unknown here, and they will matter in assessing the size of the dispute.

## The price of leaving

The plaintiffs' account of departure involves several separate mechanisms, and it helps to take them in order.

The first is a calendar rule. Plaintiffs allege that customers had to give 30 days' written notice that they would decline to renew. A rule like this turns the decision to leave into a matter of timing. As an illustration, an owner whose contract term ended on December 31 would need to act around the start of December if the notice ran from the end of the term. The owner's choice would have to be made and put in writing a month ahead.

The second is the process of cancelling. Plaintiffs allege cancellation handoffs, in which a customer trying to leave is passed along from one point of contact to another. The third is billing, with plaintiffs alleging that charges continued. Each of these could arise separately, and how often each occurred remains unknown.

The fourth mechanism applies to a narrower group. According to the complaint, restaurants with Toast Capital balances could face accelerated repayment if they cancelled their subscriptions or changed providers. Accelerated repayment means the remaining balance becomes due promptly, in place of the original payment schedule. The money in question is an outstanding financing balance, which makes it a different kind of obligation from a termination fee. In the phone comparison, it resembles the handset balance coming due at once when the household switches carriers. For an owner, the practical effect is about timing and cash: a debt already owed could arrive far sooner because of a decision about software or payments. The loan terms and the frequency of acceleration remain unknown.

Then comes the figure with the cents attached. The complaint cites one termination-fee example of $5,318.87. Its precision makes it memorable, and it shows how high the stakes could run for at least one customer, according to the plaintiffs. It remains a single example, and how it compares with what other owners paid is unknown. Its calculation also remains unverified. Dividing it by $150 gives about 35.5, an uneven result, which is reason enough to keep it separate from the per-month formula.

## What the court has to sort out

Put together, the complaint's numbers form a map of a relationship, from a $0 monthly entry price and transaction fees to monthly subscriptions, a notice deadline, per-month exit charges and a financing balance that could come due. The map is useful because it shows where each cost is said to appear. The plaintiffs' words, such as "hidden," "unauthorized" and difficult to cancel, are claims in a lawsuit, and they are separate from measurements of how common any practice was. The figures reviewed here are selected rates and examples, so total harm, the share of customers affected and the average exit bill all remain unknown.

The allegation about misleading support promises is the thinnest part of this account. The plaintiffs make the claim, and the specific statements, their audience and their timing remain unverified here.

The case's status is plain. The plaintiffs have requested remedies, and the court will decide the allegations. As of the record collected on October 30, the case stood at the allegation stage, with any remedy still undecided. Toast's position is unknown in that record. A focused search of official Toast sites found product and status pages, and a verified response remains to be located. Readers should treat that gap as a limit of the record, separate from any admission or refusal to comment. Later developments on the court's docket would fall outside this dated account.

The evidence to come should show what was actually on the screens, in the contracts and in the bills. It may also show how many restaurants met each condition, how many diners paid the 99-cent fee and what happened to the owners who tried to leave. Any answer Toast gives in court may change how these numbers look.

Until then, the case leaves a question that reaches any owner who runs a business through one connected system. When the company that takes the orders also handles the payments, sells the software and holds the loan, how much of the true price can an owner see on the day of signing, and how much comes into view only on the day of leaving?
