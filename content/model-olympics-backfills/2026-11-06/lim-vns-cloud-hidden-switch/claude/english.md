# The Cloud's Hidden Traffic Cop Works Behind Closed Doors. One Engineer Built a Version Meant to Be Opened.

Much of what people call the cloud runs on physical servers owned by large companies, with many customers sharing each machine. The equipment that steers those customers' data is largely proprietary, so outside researchers have limited access to its inner workings. On November 2, 2026, [MIT News reported](https://news.mit.edu/2026/jeffery-lim-wants-to-open-up-cloud-research-1102) that Jeffery Lim, a computer engineer at MIT Lincoln Laboratory, MIT's federally funded research laboratory, had built and tested a working research prototype that recreates key parts of this layer. He developed the system, called VNS, while pursuing a master's degree at Boston University. MIT News dates its presentation to September 2026, and [the paper](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/176308), written with his adviser Martin Herbordt, appears in the proceedings of an IEEE conference on high-performance computing. VNS reproduces selected functions of a modern cloud network so researchers can investigate them. A commercial cloud contains far more.

To see why such a device matters, start with what happens inside a shared server. A customer of a cloud company usually rents a virtual machine, which is a computer made of software that uses a portion of a real server's processor, memory and other resources. Several virtual machines can run side by side on one server. A program called a hypervisor manages them, much as a building manager oversees a block of rented flats.

These virtual computers talk to the outside world, and to each other, by sending data in small bundles called packets. The physical connection between the server and the network is a piece of hardware called a network interface card, or NIC. Every packet that reaches a shared server raises two practical questions: where should it go, and do the rules allow it to go there? One customer's traffic must reach the right machine and obey that customer's security settings.

Conventionally, a program called a host software virtual switch answers those questions. It runs on the server's own processor, where it directs packets and applies network policies. That processor also has customers' work to do, so packet handling competes for a shared resource. As network demands grew, cloud engineers found a way to shift much of this traffic onto a faster path.

That path relies on a technology with a long name: single-root input/output virtualization, or SR-IOV. It lets one physical network device present itself as several separate virtual devices, called virtual functions, which can be assigned to virtual machines. Traffic then moves through a more direct hardware route. Amazon Web Services [says its enhanced networking uses SR-IOV](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/enhanced-networking.html) on supported EC2 instances, its rentable virtual machines. Microsoft [describes Azure Accelerated Networking](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/virtual-network/accelerated-networking-overview) as sending data past the host software switch while enforcing policies in hardware. Both descriptions come from documentation retrieved on November 4, 2026; the publication dates of those page editions are unknown. Reduced packet handling on the host can free processor capacity for other work. These company pages describe their own systems, and their claims say nothing about how VNS performs.

As an illustration, picture a large shared building where every delivery once passed through a central front desk. The desk checked each parcel and pointed it toward the right tenant. Then the building added dedicated loading lanes that run straight to each tenant's door. Parcels now move faster and the front desk has more time for other duties, yet someone still has to direct each delivery and check where it may go. That checking has moved to the lanes themselves. The comparison is useful for one purpose, showing where routing and admission decisions happen. Computer isolation follows its own rules, and a virtual boundary behaves differently from a brick wall.

## The machinery behind the door

The shift in location carries a consequence. Once routing and rule enforcement sit inside network hardware, the behavior of that hardware becomes important to anyone studying isolation, the separation of one customer's activity from another's, and resilience, a system's ability to keep working under stress. The mature versions of this hardware are largely proprietary. Outside researchers may observe what the machinery does while the mechanism producing those effects remains closed to inspection and change. The sources present this as an obstacle to research. A flaw at any particular provider, a customer breach or a proven fix for a named operator all lie outside what they report.

This was the gap Lim set out to fill. According to MIT News, he found that existing research hardware offered some SR-IOV support but lacked practical functions he needed. VNS, short for Verilog Network Switch, is his answer. Verilog is a language engineers use to describe digital circuits, and the name points to the kind of work involved. VNS is built on a field-programmable gate array, or FPGA, a chip whose internal digital circuitry can be reconfigured after manufacture. Installing an app adds instructions for existing circuits to follow. Reconfiguring an FPGA changes how the circuits themselves are arranged, which is a much deeper kind of change and suits experiments on hardware behavior.

The finished design is a research version of a SmartNIC, a network card that takes on networking jobs that would otherwise fall to a server's main processor. Lim's two central additions handle separate jobs, and together they correspond closely to the two questions every packet raises.

The first addition, a switching fabric, answers the question of where a packet should go. Two virtual machines on one server may need to exchange data, in which case the traffic can stay inside that machine. Other packets must leave for a different server. The paper's abstract describes a flow table, a lookup list held in hardware, that redirects packets accordingly.

The second addition, a network function engine, deals with whether a packet is allowed. It applies firewall rules separately for each tenant, the industry's word for a customer sharing the equipment. Those rules run on what the authors call an eBPF softcore engine. In this design, eBPF is a way of expressing small packet-processing programs, and a softcore is a processor formed from the FPGA's programmable circuitry. Here Lim adapted earlier doctoral research by Zaid Tahir, who also studied under Herbordt at Boston University. The available abstract leaves two larger questions open: how much of the full Linux eBPF feature set the engine matches, and how it would hold up against attack.

Lim's career helps explain why he approached the problem through reconfigurable chips. MIT News reports that he studied electrical and computer engineering at the University of Colorado Boulder and worked with FPGAs in industry before joining Lincoln Laboratory in 2018. His work there included radar for detecting the breathing of people trapped under rubble, radar involving satellites and debris, and later an investigation of chip faults caused by radiation. In 2022 he moved to the laboratory's Secure Resilient Systems and Technology Group, and in 2024 he entered its Lincoln Scholars graduate program. Benjamin Nahill mentored him at the laboratory. This background shows a long familiarity with programmable hardware and resilient systems. The evidence for VNS's network performance comes from the VNS tests alone.

## What the tests show, and where they stop

Lim told MIT News that he built two servers at home to test the design. He also used the Open Cloud Testbed, part of the Mass Open Cloud, to gather measurements and validate it. That account places the work beyond a paper design, in physical systems running real traffic. Boston University's College of Engineering gave him its 2026 Master's Societal Impact Award, which recognizes the work's potential and serves a different purpose from a performance test or security certification.

The reported performance results are specific, and their conditions shape their meaning. In the abstract, the authors report throughput, the amount of data moved over time, that they describe as competitive with a commercial SR-IOV NIC. That comparison used a matched feature configuration at moderate firewall rule depths, explicitly 0 to 64 rules. Rule depth here refers to the number of firewall rules in play, so the test covered a range from an empty rule list up to 64 entries.

The headline figure comes from a particular communication workload called 16-rank all-to-all collectives. In this kind of test, a group of participating processes, each called a rank, exchange data with every other member of the group. Sixteen ranks may differ from 16 customers or 16 physical servers. On that workload, against that commercial NIC, the authors report a 10% to 13% performance advantage. The abstract leaves unstated whether the percentage comes from a measure of duration, throughput or some other formula, so the safest description is the one the authors give: a benchmark performance advantage. An all-to-all exchange creates many simultaneous conversations, which, as general reasoning, would put heavy demands on a system's switching path. That connection is an inference, because the abstract reports the result and stays silent on its cause.

The authors also report a real limit. Beyond moderate rule depths, the latency of the programmable FPGA data path becomes visible. Latency means the delay a packet experiences on its way through. The authors accordingly present VNS as reconfigurable research hardware and draw a clear line between it and a production NIC built for peak throughput. Their own framing therefore points toward flexibility and access as the design's main offering, with the speed results showing that it can run in a comparable range under the tested conditions.

This account also has limits of its own. Its benchmark details come from the manuscript's public abstract and repository record. A normal request for the full PDF returned a server rate-limit error during reporting, so the paper's numerical tables, test settings, variation between runs and exact percentage formula remain unaudited here. The results stand as the authors' reported findings. Whether any outside team has replicated them is unknown.

Taken together, the results support a modest but useful claim: under the stated conditions, a programmable research design kept pace with a commercial card and came out ahead in one reported workload. General cloud acceleration, customer savings and improved security all sit beyond that evidence.

## A door that still has to open

The project's central promise depends on other people being able to use it, and here the sources contain an unresolved tension. The paper's title and abstract call VNS an open-source design. MIT News describes an open-source release as Lim's plan. The two statements may reflect the difference between a project's intended character and code that others can already download, but the sources leave the matter unsettled. As of the November 4 collection of material for this article, the public availability of the code is unknown.

The release question carries real weight because a closed prototype helps mainly the people who built it. VNS could widen research on this layer to the extent that others can obtain the design, understand its documentation, get suitable hardware, reproduce the results and put the system to work in their own experiments. Each of those steps remains ahead of the evidence. Openness also has a precise value. An open design gives researchers the means to examine and alter a system, and any security gains would have to be demonstrated through that examination.

MIT's report identifies possible uses that follow from this access. One is more realistic research on hypervisor hardening, the work of making the virtual-machine manager tougher against attack and failure. On a platform like VNS, researchers could study that manager alongside a network path resembling the architecture used in modern clouds. Another is the study of high-performance datacenter architectures. Lim is also considering whether lessons from the project could apply to constrained embedded systems, such as computers aboard drones or satellites, where space and power are tight. All of these remain prospects, with completed deployments and provider adoption absent from the reported record.

The story so far is a working machine and a set of carefully bounded numbers, built around an architectural layer that outsiders have found hard to reach. As more work depends on shared computing, the routes that carry and filter its traffic deserve realistic study, and the mature versions of those routes belong to the companies that built them. Lim has made a version designed for experiment, tested it on his own servers and on a shared research testbed, and said he plans to release it. The next chapter depends on people outside his project: once they can load VNS onto their own hardware, will their measurements match his, and what will they learn about the hidden layer that decides where the cloud's packets may go?
