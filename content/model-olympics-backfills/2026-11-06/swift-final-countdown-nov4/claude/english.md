# Eleven Hours of Uncertainty: A Falling Space Telescope and the Robot Rescuer That Came Within 15 Kilometres

NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, a space telescope that spent nearly 22 years chasing the most powerful explosions in the universe, was predicted to fall back into Earth's atmosphere on November 4, 2026. Its final chapter involved several groups. NASA ran the mission, flight controllers at Penn State gave the telescope its daily instructions, and the U.S. Space Force produced the re-entry forecast. Katalyst, a company in Flagstaff, Arizona, was hired to build a robotic spacecraft that would catch Swift and lift it to a safer height. The robot reached orbit, but the rescue failed.

The latest forecast available for this account came from NASA's blog on November 2. It reported a Space Force prediction that the roughly 3,200-pound spacecraft, about 1,450 kilograms, would re-enter at approximately 3:30 a.m. EST on November 4. That equals 08:30 UTC, or 16:30 in Hong Kong. The clock time sounds exact, yet NASA attached an uncertainty of 11 hours and left the statistical meaning of that figure undefined. NASA also said that it and the Space Force would keep tracking the spacecraft and updating their predictions. This account stops at the November 2 update, so the re-entry appears here as a forecast.

NASA expected most of Swift to burn up in the atmosphere and some components to survive. Timing shapes where those pieces could land, and a change of hours could move the possible location from one hemisphere to another. Swift's orbit stayed fairly close to the equator, so NASA limited surviving fragments to a belt from 20.6 degrees south latitude to 20.6 degrees north. That belt gives the outer limits for possible surviving debris, and any specific impact location remains unidentified in the forecast. NASA called harm unlikely and put the chance of harm to anyone on Earth at approximately 1 in 2,100. This figure describes the whole event across the planet, and it differs from any one person's odds. The update leaves the calculation behind it unexplained.

## A telescope built for a race

To understand why NASA tried so hard to save an old spacecraft, start with what Swift was built to catch. Gamma-ray bursts are the most powerful explosions known. Gamma rays are the most energetic form of light, and these bursts arise when massive stars die catastrophically and form new black holes, or when neutron stars or black holes in orbiting systems merge. A neutron star is the extremely dense remnant of a collapsed star. The bursts are brief and fade quickly, leaving a dimming glow called an afterglow. An astronomer who arrives late sees only the final moments of the event.

Swift launched on November 20, 2004, so the forecast date fell 16 days short of its 22nd anniversary. NASA described it as a first-of-its-kind observatory at launch, carrying three telescopes that worked as a relay team.

The first was the Burst Alert Telescope, or BAT. It used a D-shaped mask of about 52,000 tiny lead tiles, placed in a random pattern one metre above its detectors. Light from a burst cast the mask's shadow onto those detectors, and the shadow's position revealed where in the sky the light had come from. The onboard computer worked out the location to within about a tenth of a degree, a tiny patch much narrower than the full Moon, and sent it to the ground within 20 seconds. NASA's alert network then passed the position to astronomers around the world, giving other telescopes an immediate place to search.

Swift then turned itself, and this was the feature that gave it its name. Acting on its own, the spacecraft swung around in 20 to 100 seconds and brought two narrower instruments onto the target. The X-ray Telescope measured how the afterglow's brightness and energy changed, often beginning within about 70 seconds of discovery and continuing for days or weeks. Before Swift, NASA says, the few X-ray telescopes in space typically reached a burst hours or days later. The Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope, with a 30-centimetre opening, captured ultraviolet and visible light and pinned down the most precise position. Its ultraviolet view depended on working in space, because that observation is impossible from the ground.

The original plan called for a two-year prime mission. Swift's speed and range soon made it a general-purpose observatory as well. It studied exploding stars, stellar flares, stars torn apart by supermassive black holes, eruptions in active galaxies, and comets and asteroids in our own solar system. It also followed one burst nicknamed the BOAT, for Brightest Of All Time, whose scattered X-rays formed expanding rings across images gathered over 12 days. NASA renamed the observatory in 2018 for Neil Gehrels, who helped develop it and served as its first principal investigator.

NASA's tally at Swift's 20th anniversary stood at 1,800 gamma-ray bursts, 1,400 supernovae and more than 6,600 scientific publications using its data. Those numbers capture the mission at that birthday, two years before its end, and the publication count measures the use of Swift's data across science. Together they show how an instrument designed for one kind of cosmic emergency became a shared resource for many fields.

## The slow fall

A satellite in low Earth orbit is always falling. It also moves sideways so quickly that the planet's surface keeps curving away beneath it, and the fall becomes a loop around the world. The air at that height is extremely thin, yet some particles remain, and each encounter takes a tiny amount of energy from the spacecraft. NASA explains that all satellites in low Earth orbit experience this drag, and that those lacking propulsion gradually lose altitude. Swift's rescue plan depended on an outside craft to do the lifting.

Here is an illustration, separate from the events of the mission. Picture a cyclist who stops pedalling on a flat road. Air pushes against her body, and she slowly loses speed. A stronger headwind slows her faster, and crouching low reduces the push. Swift's case differs in one important way, since its lost energy showed up as lost height. Still, the picture captures the two features that shaped this story: a small force repeated over and over, and a body position that could change how hard that force pressed.

The headwind, in Swift's case, came from the Sun. When solar activity rises, the upper atmosphere swells and places more particles at orbital height. NASA says a period of increased solar activity magnified the drag on Swift and caused it to sink rapidly.

Predictions showed Swift reaching a critical altitude of about 300 kilometres, or 185 miles, in fall 2026. That figure was a planning line for this particular rescue. Below it, NASA said, any boost attempt would become increasingly difficult. The deadline set the shape of everything that followed. NASA's astrophysics director said in the agency's September account that Swift would re-enter by the end of 2026 in the absence of intervention.

## Two clocks

NASA moved quickly once it decided to investigate a boost. In a few months it issued a call for proposals and funded design studies. In September 2025 it contracted Katalyst for a task with a daunting list of steps. In around one year, the company had to design, build, test and launch a satellite called LINK, which would then meet, grab and lift Swift. NASA's account contrasts this with ordinary science missions, which take years to develop before operating for decades. Staff at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center in Maryland worked with Katalyst on faster milestones and approvals, and NASA's Engineering and Safety Center offered feedback during integration and testing.

By April 28, 2026, LINK was at Goddard for testing in a vacuum chamber 27 feet across. There the team practised firing its ion thrusters, a type of engine that pushes a spacecraft with a stream of charged particles, and operated one of its robotic arms through space-like cycles of heat and cold. In June it awaited encapsulation in its rocket at NASA's Wallops facility in Virginia.

While that work raced ahead on the ground, a second team fought for time in the sky. In normal operations, controllers at Penn State in University Park sent Swift a daily plan of cosmic targets. In December 2025 they began replacing around 25% of those targets with points in the sky that would minimize drag when the telescope aimed at them. This was the spacecraft's version of crouching on the bicycle. By February 2026 they had switched to that approach entirely, and pointed science observations stopped from mid-February to late August. The observatory built for quick reactions spent about six months holding low-drag positions to wait for its rescuer.

Even this choice carried hazards. Aiming too close to Earth, the Moon or the Sun could overheat and damage the instruments with their brightness. The most streamlined position tilted Swift too near the atmosphere, where particles could strike the telescopes and affect later observations. The controllers settled on a balance that, according to NASA, kept Swift above the critical altitude for several months. Mission director John Nousek of Penn State said the changes bought valuable time and could carry forward to future NASA missions.

LINK lifted off in July 2026 from Kwajalein Atoll in the Republic of the Marshall Islands, aboard a Northrop Grumman Pegasus XL rocket. Katalyst had chosen that rocket as the best option for the compressed schedule.

At first the flight went well. LINK reached space and completed its initial checkouts. Then it began losing communications intermittently and developed problems controlling its orientation, the direction it faced. For a spacecraft meant to approach and grab a moving telescope, both faults were serious. The teams spent a period troubleshooting around the clock, and then NASA and Katalyst agreed to scale back. LINK would drop the grab and the boost and attempt a series of technology demonstrations.

The reduced objectives included exercising LINK's xenon-powered propulsion system and its three robotic arms, which had been designed to give flexibility in where the craft could safely hold Swift. The action NASA's account documents with a picture is a pass 12 to 15 kilometres from Swift, photographed by LINK, in which the observatory appears as a streak. That picture is the closest moment this article can document between rescuer and patient. It records a distant pass, and the two spacecraft stayed apart.

Several dates mark the end of the rescue, and each one refers to a separate step. NASA says the servicing mission ended in August with the boost unattempted. The agency formally concluded its involvement in LINK's mission on September 3. LINK itself re-entered the atmosphere on September 25, weeks ahead of the telescope it had been sent to save.

## The last science

Swift then went back to work. With the rescue over, its team resumed science observations and collected data on hundreds of targets, including the remains of stellar explosions, active supermassive black holes in distant galaxies, and comets. Those observations carried a price. Aiming at science targets raised the drag again, and Swift resumed its rapid descent. One way to read NASA's sequence, and this reading is this article's own, is that the telescope spent the last of its altitude on astronomy.

On October 20, Swift reached 275 kilometres, or 171 miles. By then the spacecraft had lost the ability to compensate for the growing drag, and it entered safe mode, a protective state that spacecraft enter when something threatens normal operation. The Penn State team then sent a command that effectively ended Swift's operations and prepared it for re-entry. NASA reported the end of the mission on October 23, saying re-entry would come within weeks. The November 2 update then supplied the sharper forecast.

The record that remains is mixed. Swift outlasted its two-year prime mission by about two decades. Its controllers found a way to slow its descent using pointing alone. The rescue itself ended with the orbit unraised. NASA says it and Katalyst gained valuable experience for future servicing, and it presents the effort as a test of faster project management and new ways to operate satellites. Katalyst's chief executive, Ghonhee Lee, acknowledged that the company missed some objectives and described the work as a foundation to build on. Those are the views of the organizations involved. What this mission demonstrated in orbit is more modest: LINK reached space and photographed Swift during a distant pass, while the planned grasp and boost were abandoned. Whether a commercial rescue service can work reliably remains an open question.

Those accounts also leave open whether an earlier start would have changed the ending, and they assign blame to nobody. They do show the shape of the problem. A satellite can serve for decades, and its final decline can then outrun the year or so needed to build and test a rescuer, while the Sun's activity helps set the pace. So the question Swift leaves behind is a practical one. When a valuable spacecraft is still healthy and high above the danger line, how early should someone begin preparing the mission that might one day have to catch it?
