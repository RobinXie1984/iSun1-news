# The Robot Remembered a Squirrel. Was It Looking at a Chimney?

Robots that move through the world for long stretches collect a flood of pictures, and many research systems turn those pictures into written notes that the machine can search later. On November 5, 2026, MIT News reported on a method for finding the notes most likely to need checking. The method is called UQ-DAAAM, and it comes from MIT researchers Harry Zhang, Nicolas Gorlo and Luca Carlone, who shared their paper as a preprint, a publicly posted research report, on June 6, 2026. The MIT report, by Adam Zewe, says the work will be presented at NeurIPS, a major artificial intelligence conference. One example from the research makes the problem easy to see: a distant dark chimney was described as a squirrel.

That small mistake points to a larger question for robots that are meant to share homes, factories, hospitals and other busy places with people. Such a machine needs some record of what it has seen, where it saw it and when. A person might ask where a tool was left or when a package appeared in a corridor. The robot can answer only from what it stored, so its usefulness depends on the quality of its memory as well as the strength of its motors and cameras.

The study deals with one part of that larger challenge: object memory used to answer questions. It tested how well a system could describe objects and recall their positions and times. Safe handling of objects, collision avoidance and general independence are separate abilities that require their own tests. Household help, factory collaboration and medical or emergency uses are future possibilities described in the MIT report. MIT also offers a hypothetical involving a defibrillator, the device used to shock a heart back toward a normal rhythm. That scenario shows why a wrong memory could matter, and it remains an illustration. The research tested benchmark questions, and its evidence stops short of any medical deployment or rescue.

The memory begins with a camera. As a mobile robot travels, it can see the same object at different moments and from different angles. A vision-language model, which is software that turns images into words, writes descriptions of what appears in those views. A structure called a scene graph then attaches the descriptions to objects with fixed places in three-dimensional space and links them to time. A scene graph works like a map of identified things. Each thing on the map has a label, a location and a place in the timeline.

This arrangement has an appealing feature. Because the memory is written in ordinary language, a human can read it, and a language model can reason over it. It also has a weakness. A caption made from a poor view can state something false with complete assurance. Distance, difficult lighting or a partly blocked angle can all produce that kind of error. The authors say existing memory systems treat these captions as dependable truth. In DAAAM, the earlier system their work extends, each object gets a small fixed set of representative views. If those views happen to be bad, the wrong entry sits in memory looking just like a correct one.

So the squirrel in the notes could become a squirrel in the answers. Ask the system about that object later, and it may retrieve the mistaken description as though it were a settled fact. That leads to the central puzzle. If a robot has stored a wrong description, how can it tell which of its thousands of memories deserve another look, and how much would that second look improve what it can answer later?

Here is an everyday comparison, offered purely as an illustration. Picture a notebook that records each item's description, address and date, with several witnesses writing about the same item. One witness writes "small animal on a roof," another writes "brick chimney," and a third writes "dark post." Those conflicting notes give the reader a reason to stop and check the entry. Re-reading every page and studying every photograph would take far too long, so the reader flags the most conflicted entries, pulls the clearest available photographs for those items and rewrites their summaries.

UQ-DAAAM follows a similar sequence with numbers and software. It first gathers captions from several views of each object; in the reported runs, each object received about 3.44 captions on average. Each caption is then converted into an embedding, a list of numbers that represents meaning so that similar descriptions land close together. The system measures how widely those points scatter. The authors call this semantic scatter, and it serves as a measure of how much the descriptions disagree. The final uncertainty score also weighs in a second signal, called coherence, drawn from the individual views.

That second signal appears to matter. When the authors tested different ways of choosing objects for attention, raw disagreement alone gave the weakest question accuracy among the four rules, 73.7%. The full score reached 76.1%, and the authors concluded that disagreement among captions works poorly as a stand-alone warning sign when it is detached from the visual evidence.

The next steps spend a strictly limited allowance. Each extra caption is a query to the vision-language model and carries a computing cost, so the system assigns a fixed budget of extra queries to its uncertain objects. For those objects, it chooses further views using an existing quality score based on how visible the object is and how many image pixels it covers. Those candidates can come from images already gathered along the robot's path. Routinely driving back for a fresh look at a doubtful object is future work, so the current result should be pictured as a careful review of an album of existing images and queries about them, with the robot's return trip still ahead.

The last step merges the descriptions into one summary. This turned out to be a delicate job. When later captions conflicted with earlier ones, the authors' rules favored the refined captions, and they kept alternatives on record when the initial captions stayed in dispute. This careful approach reached 76.1% accuracy. A simple instruction to a language model to merge all captions reached 74.7%, which was lower than using just the single best caption, at 75.4%. More evidence helped most when the system had a sensible way of weighing it.

The paper also makes a mathematical promise, with an important condition. The authors argue that better-scored views are more likely to reduce the measured uncertainty if an assumption called first-order stochastic dominance holds. In everyday terms, the assumption says that, across the range of possible outcomes, a higher-quality view has better odds of shrinking uncertainty than a lower-quality view. The promise applies to the uncertainty score and depends on that assumption. It stops well short of proving that every revised description is true or that the robot is safe. The authors also derive a shortcut formula for the change in uncertainty when one view is added, which saves some repeated calculation, while each extra caption still requires a model query.

## What the tests found

The authors evaluated the system on a benchmark called OC-NaVQA, built from a collection of sequences known as CODa. The benchmark asks questions about objects, positions and times, and its contexts extend up to 35.8 minutes and 1.64 kilometres of travel. Those figures describe the longest settings, and they give a sense of how far back the memory may need to reach. All methods in the main comparison used the same reasoning model, GPT-5-mini, so the differences point toward how each memory was built and refined.

Overall accuracy on descriptive questions was 76.1% for UQ-DAAAM and 71.1% for the original DAAAM, a gain of 5.0 percentage points. The strongest alternative on that measure, a variant called DAAAM-ES, scored 74.3%, which puts the new method 1.8 points ahead. Those named comparisons give the fairest reading of any broad statement about a five percent improvement. The gain is measured in percentage points on this benchmark, and its size depends on the competitor.

Recall of place and time also improved overall. Average position error fell from 41.75 metres to 37.84 metres, and average time error fell from 1.792 minutes to 1.589 minutes, or from roughly 108 seconds to 95. These are real gains, and the remaining errors are still large. An answer that lands about 38 metres away, on average, may describe an object well and still leave a robot far from it.

The table also holds a stubborn counterexample. On long-horizon questions, position error worsened, from 53.47 metres for the original DAAAM to 58.94 metres for UQ-DAAAM, the largest error of the eight methods in that column. Accuracy on the same long-horizon group rose substantially, from 69.0% to 78.6%. The paper's statement that the method wins across all three measures therefore fits the overall figures, while individual rows tell a more mixed story. A better description of an object and a better memory of its position over a long journey can move in different directions.

The most striking number in the paper is 92.3%, and it requires the most careful reading. That figure is the uncertainty reduction rate at a budget setting of two extra caption queries, compared with 34.2% for a method guided only by view quality and 5.6% for random refinement. The rate looks only at objects first classed as uncertain. It reports the share whose score then fell below a chosen cutoff, one set using a separate calibration dataset. So the 92.3% means that most flagged objects moved into the lower-uncertainty group under that test. It measures calmer scores, and it differs from a count of corrected memories, completed tasks or safe outcomes.

A classroom offers another illustration. A test score records how many answers were right, while a marker's doubt about those answers is a separate quantity. Doubt can fall while an answer remains wrong. The paper's own results show both quantities moving in a helpful direction: raising the budget from one extra query to two to three lifted the reduction rate from 68.9% to 92.3% to 94.4%, while overall accuracy climbed from 74.2% to 76.1% to 77.4%. The largest accuracy gain came with the second query, and the reduction rate had nearly levelled off by that point. The authors read this as a sign that a few extra views can settle most flagged objects, with smaller benefits afterward.

The review also stayed selective in practice. Across seven CODa sequences, 18,298 objects were annotated, and 6,324 of them, or 34.6%, were chosen for refinement. Those selected objects received an average of 1.32 additional views each, give or take 0.47. A budget of two therefore should be read as a ceiling on extra queries for selected objects, and most objects in the dataset received none.

Speed looked promising in this particular setup. The authors report 13.5 frames per second across 98,866 frames on one RTX 4090, a powerful graphics card, while the robot's sensors supplied 10 frames per second. Graphics memory use stayed within the card's 24 gigabytes. These numbers describe one implementation on one strong piece of hardware. Product cost, performance on cheaper consumer hardware, delays from calling remote models and behavior in general deployment all remain unknown.

## When the witnesses agree

The sharpest limit comes from the authors themselves, in an appendix on the system's weaknesses. Their score depends on the vision-language model that wrote the captions. If that model systematically invents details, misses small features or gives badly calibrated coherence signals, the score can misrepresent how ambiguous an object really is. Several views can also agree on the same mistaken description. In the notebook illustration, three witnesses standing in the same bad light might all write "squirrel," and the entry would look perfectly calm.

The method also carries costs. Repeated queries and merging may be too slow for fast-moving machines or for tasks with strict time limits. Storing descriptions, scores and refinement histories for many objects can also make memory demands grow over long operation. The authors propose stronger ways of acquiring new views and summaries that keep memory use within fixed bounds. All of the reported results come from the authors, and independent replication has yet to appear in the available evidence.

Even with those limits, the work suggests a useful change in how a machine's memory might be designed. This interpretation is an editorial reading of the evidence, and the experiments themselves stayed within a benchmark. A stored description can travel with some evidence about how far it should be trusted, and a robot's limited checking effort can go where that evidence looks weakest. A blanket warning that any memory might be wrong gives a robot little to act on. A flag attached to one object tells it where to look next.

Moving from better benchmark answers to dependable help in a home, a factory or a hospital would demand far more. It would require tests built around specific tasks, measurements of what each kind of error costs, checks on calibration and hardware, and replication by other researchers. A robot looking for a chimney and a robot looking for a defibrillator face very different consequences when a memory goes wrong.

UQ-DAAAM gives a robot a way to notice when its notes are arguing with one another, and on this benchmark that habit produced better answers overall. The next test is harder and remains open. When every note finally agrees, how will the machine find out whether they all agree on a squirrel that was a chimney all along?
