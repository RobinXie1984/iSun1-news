# The Gallium Sits in the Ore. Which Handoff Decides Who Gets It?

On November 3, 2026, MIT News published an interview with Elsa Olivetti, a materials researcher and professor who co-directs MIT's Minerals Stewardship Consortium, about why supplies of important minerals are hard to secure. Her argument concerns a chain of jobs: taking material from the ground, separating the useful part, refining it and manufacturing it into something people can use. A secure supply, she says, depends on all of those jobs working together. The interview presents her analysis and research priorities, offering a way to understand supply risk. One small metal offers a good test of her argument.

That metal is gallium. Inside many electronic devices, it works as part of compounds such as gallium arsenide, called GaAs, and gallium nitride, called GaN. These materials form the basis of semiconductor devices, the electronic parts that steer electricity inside machines. According to the U.S. Geological Survey, or USGS, integrated circuits, the tiny electronic systems often called chips, accounted for 73% of U.S. gallium consumption. Optoelectronic devices, which turn electricity into light or light into electricity, accounted for 26%. They include LEDs, laser diodes, photodetectors and solar cells. Olivetti points to defense radar as one technology that relies on small amounts of gallium, and USGS says GaAs and GaN chips lack effective substitutes in many defense uses.

The USGS gallium chapter, published in February 2026 with figures that mostly describe 2025, contains a puzzle. It says China accounted for 99% of worldwide primary low-purity gallium production. It also reports that a company in New York recovered and refined high-purity gallium. And it lists the direct sources of U.S. gallium metal imports from 2021 to 2024 as Canada at 28%, Japan at 22%, China at 18%, Germany at 16% and other countries at 16%. A reader might wonder how one country can hold 99% of something while supplying 18% of American imports, and how a country with zero primary production can host gallium refining.

All of these statements can stand together because each one describes a different link in the chain, and in one case a different span of years. That distinction matters well beyond gallium. Governments and companies often describe mineral security in terms of mines, trading partners or new factories. Olivetti's framing asks a more demanding question: where does the material go next, and who can do the next job?

Minerals also differ from one another. Olivetti contrasts the huge quantities of steel, aluminum and copper that electrical grids need with the tiny quantities of gallium used in technologies such as radar. Each material has its own geology, market and processing route, so a plan built for copper may do little for gallium. Rare earths form another separate case. They are a group of elements with such similar chemistry that separating one from another is notoriously difficult, and gallium sits outside that group.

Gallium's particular difficulty starts with how it is made. USGS says primary gallium, the first low-purity metal recovered from ore, comes mostly as a byproduct of processing bauxite, the main ore used to make aluminum. A smaller share comes from zinc-processing residues. Gallium therefore rides along with a far larger industry, as Olivetti explains. Strong demand for gallium alone may be insufficient to bring more of it out, because increasing output can require changes at a plant organized around aluminum.

## A pinch from someone else's soup

Picture, purely as an illustration, a kitchen that needs a rare spice. The spice exists only in tiny traces in vegetables grown for an enormous pot of soup. Someone at the soup kitchen has to install a special strainer and skim the pinch out as a rough powder. A second specialist then purifies the powder, and a third cook uses it in a delicate dish. Owning the final kitchen secures the last step. Owning the vegetable farm secures the first. The dish reaches the table only when every handoff works, and the soup maker's priorities shape how much rough spice exists in the first place. The comparison explains handoffs alone. Real mineral chains also demand exact chemical purity, specialized equipment, enough volume at a workable cost, and acceptable conditions for nearby land and communities.

Set against that picture, the USGS figures fall into place. The 99% share belongs to the skimming stage: primary low-purity gallium. The chapter's table, drawing on estimates from Asian Metal Ltd., puts China's 2025 production at about 900,000 kilograms, Japan's at about 3,000 and Russia's at about 6,000. The rounded world total also appears as 900,000 kilograms. That rounding makes the table look as though China produced everything, while the chapter's own figure is 99%, with Japan and Russia as other producers.

The import list measures a later moment. It records where metal directly arrived from over four earlier years. A shipment from Canada or Germany left that country on its way to the United States, and the trade figure stays silent about where the gallium inside it was first recovered. These trade shares therefore leave the upstream route of each shipment untraced. An 18% direct share for China and a 99% production share for China answer different questions, and each one needs its own date and definition.

The American position splits in the same way. USGS reports zero domestic primary gallium recovery since 1987. Its February 2026 chapter places the New York company one step later, reporting that it recovered and refined high-purity gallium from imported low-purity metal and new scrap, meaning waste left over from manufacturing. USGS says substantial quantities of new scrap from GaAs device production were reprocessed at the New York facility. It lists recycling of old scrap, the material in products that have already been used, as none. So the USGS account shows the United States with a refining step and a partial manufacturing loop, both resting on material arriving from the stage before.

The USGS reliance figure reflects that position. It reports 2025 U.S. net import reliance of 100% of reported consumption, using a measure defined as imports minus exports and excluding GaAs wafers. Readers should keep that definition close. It describes the country's overall balance on that measure, and it leaves room for shipments from many countries. The chapter estimates 2025 consumption at 19,000 kilograms and metal imports at 25,000 kilograms, with export figures listed as unavailable. Those published numbers form an incomplete physical balance, so the gap between them is best left unexplained, and calling it a surplus or a disappearance would go beyond the evidence.

## What the numbers and the ore can show

The annual trade figures show a market in motion, with careful limits on what they prove. USGS estimates that U.S. gallium metal imports rose from 11,000 kilograms in 2024 to 25,000 kilograms in 2025. The average customs value rose from $439 to $580 per kilogram over the same years, about 30%. Imports of GaAs wafers, measured by gross weight, were estimated to be 24% lower than in 2024. Their estimated 2025 total import value was $120 million, compared with an estimated $15 million for gallium metal. Those totals cover different products and different quantities, so they work best as separate facts. The customs averages are annual records, and they differ from a spot price or from the price a particular buyer pays this month.

Trade policy adds the most time-sensitive part of the story. USGS records Chinese export controls on gallium in August 2023, a ban on gallium exports to the United States in December 2024, and a decision in November 2025 to lift that ban for one year. That one-year suspension began in November 2025, and its status on November 6, 2026 requires fresh confirmation. The chapter also places the rise in 2025 imports beside these trade events, and any causal link between them remains an inference.

The supply map has also narrowed over time. USGS says Kazakhstan, Hungary and Germany ceased primary production in 2013, 2015 and 2016, respectively, and that Ukraine most likely ceased in 2022. Yet the same table estimates 2025 world production capacity at 1.7 million kilograms, nearly double the rounded output of 900,000. Capacity describes what plants could potentially produce under stated assumptions, and it differs from metal that actually exists or reaches a buyer. About 1.6 million kilograms of that estimated capacity is in China. The estimated 100,000 kilograms elsewhere in the "other countries" line belongs to places including Germany, Hungary, Kazakhstan, the Republic of Korea and Ukraine.

The ore itself seems to promise plenty. USGS says bauxite averages 50 parts per million of gallium, which works out to about 50 grams in a metric tonne of ore, roughly the weight of a hen's egg in a load about as heavy as a small car. It estimates that the world's bauxite resources contain more than 1 million metric tons of gallium. That impressive number describes what is present in the rock. It differs from an economically proven reserve, and any estimate of years of supply drawn from it would be unreliable. Recovery depends on chemistry, plant design, cost and which ore stream a plant processes. Geography adds a further limit: USGS describes U.S. bauxite deposits as mainly subeconomic, generally unsuitable for alumina production because of their high silica content. Some domestic zinc ores contain up to 50 parts per million of gallium and could be a significant resource, USGS says, although it reports recovery from domestic ores at zero.

Olivetti widens the list of pressures beyond chemistry. She cites deeper or lower-grade deposits, sensitive locations, water availability, extreme weather, community concerns and shifting costs. Her proposed response combines science with market incentives, governance, workforce development and continuing engagement with communities. These are her diagnosis and priorities, stated in general terms; measuring how strongly each factor restricts gallium output is a separate task.

Her research examples show how varied the attempts are. They include engineered microbes or proteins that latch onto selected metals, a route aimed at problems such as rare-earth separation, along with electrochemical methods that use electricity to extract and separate metals. Others involve remote sensing of land-use change, meaning observation of the land from a distance, along with tighter temperature control in separation, mechanical and fluid studies relevant to deep-sea mining, and community knowledge in planning. She also names startups: Lithios, working on lithium from brines; RockZero, on hard rock; and SiTration, NthCycle and FoundIndustries, on recovery from mining or industrial waste. These companies start from different materials, and their output, cost and environmental performance await independent verification.

The consortium Olivetti helps lead is itself a research arrangement with mining companies at the table. MIT says it launched in September 2025 with BHP, Vale and Rio Tinto as inaugural members, under Olivetti and economist Christopher Knittel. MIT's December 16, 2025 announcement described nine faculty members across four schools and the computing college. It also named four research areas: digital mining and decision support; social, ecological and economic integration; environmental monitoring and remote sensing; and market and policy innovation. Those descriptions come from MIT itself and set out a program of work. Membership is separate from any certification of a company's mining practices.

## Which link would a new project secure?

Several projects now aim at gallium's narrow points, and they sit at different stages. USGS reports that in September 2025 the U.S. Department of Energy announced as much as $6 million for research on recovering gallium from alumina refining or primary zinc smelting, with the goal of restarting domestic primary recovery for the first time in almost 40 years. In November 2025, the Department of War granted $29.9 million under the Defense Production Act for a demonstration facility in Louisiana to recover gallium and scandium from industrial waste, with initial development expected in Texas. USGS also notes announced production projects in Australia, Canada, Greece, Kazakhstan and the Republic of Korea. Olivetti separately describes projects moving toward pilot testing, including support associated with the Department of Energy's Genesis Mission.

Each of those milestones means something different. A research grant funds a question. A pilot tests a process at small scale, and a demonstration plant tests it at a larger one. Commercial supply means reliable output of the right quality, delivered year after year at a price buyers can pay. The sources here establish the funding announcements and plans as of their dates, and the status of these facilities in November 2026 remains to be checked.

The stage-by-stage view also clarifies what success would change, as an editorial reading of this evidence. New American primary recovery would fill the empty first link, and its metal would still need a refiner able to meet exacting purity demands. More refining capacity in New York or elsewhere would strengthen the middle while remaining tied to a supply of low-purity metal or scrap. A new chip factory would add capacity at the end and could still draw on the same narrow upstream stage. Rare earths offer a parallel illustration: a domestic magnet factory that depends on imported separated rare-earth oxides has moved the final step and kept its reliance on an earlier one.

So the gallium in the ground is only the start of the story. The next announcement of a mine, plant, award or breakthrough will invite one natural question, and Olivetti's chain of jobs helps a reader ask it precisely. Which stage has changed, how much material of what verified quality can it deliver and by when, and which supplier remains indispensable after the ribbon is cut?
