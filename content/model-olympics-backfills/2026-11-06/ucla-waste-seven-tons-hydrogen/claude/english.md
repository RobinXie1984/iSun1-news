# Seven Tons of Waste a Day and a Clock Running to 2027: Can UCLA's Pilot Pull Hydrogen From Its Scraps?

The University of California, Los Angeles, is a research university that also behaves like a small city: people eat there, gardeners trim its plants, and its buildings draw electricity around the clock. UCLA estimates that its campus produces approximately 7 tons of biomass waste a day, with food scraps and plant trimmings among the examples. Since April 2026, engineers from UCLA's Samueli School of Engineering and Mote, a Los Angeles climate-technology company, have been testing a pilot system that explores an ambitious use for this kind of material. The aim is to turn it into high-purity hydrogen, a gas that can serve as fuel, while capturing and storing its carbon. Associate professor Dante Simonetti leads the UCLA team. The 18-month pilot is scheduled to run through September 2027, with a possible extension.

The project joins two jobs that a campus usually handles separately. One job is dealing with organic leftovers, the stuff that piles up after lunches and landscaping. The other is supplying energy to classrooms, laboratories and vehicles. The partnership asks whether the first job can help with the second, and whether the climate can come out ahead along the way.

Hydrogen sits at the center of that hope for reasons that reach far beyond one university. In general terms, a device called a fuel cell can combine hydrogen with oxygen to make electricity, and its main exhaust is water. That makes hydrogen an appealing energy carrier for machines and power systems. The catch is that hydrogen on Earth is almost always bound to other elements, in water, in natural gas and in the molecules of living things, so someone has to spend energy and effort pulling it free. Most hydrogen made today comes from fossil fuels, a process that releases carbon dioxide into the air.

This general background, separate from the UCLA reports, explains the attraction of starting with plants and food. Biomass simply means material that came from living things. A hedge clipping and a discarded orange peel both qualify. Both also contain carbon that plants drew out of the air as they grew. If engineers could extract hydrogen from such material and keep the carbon locked away, a pile of campus trash would become a source of useful fuel and a possible climate tool. That "if" is the reason for the pilot.

## Inside the recipe

The conversion route under test is called gasification. As engineers generally use the term, gasification means heating material intensely while tightly limiting the oxygen that reaches it. With plenty of oxygen, hot material would burn. With little oxygen, large molecules break apart into smaller ones and form a mixture of gases, typically including hydrogen, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide. This point matters because scraps hold their hydrogen locked inside larger molecules. The hydrogen has to be released through chemistry and then separated from everything else in the mixture.

According to UCLA's accounts, the system's goal is to convert biomass into a gas stream, then to obtain high-purity hydrogen and capture and store carbon. High purity means the hydrogen has been separated well enough from other gases to suit its intended job. Fuel cells, for instance, generally demand very clean hydrogen because stray gases can damage them. The most interesting scientific trouble begins with the material going in, which engineers call feedstock. Factories usually enjoy uniform ingredients. A campus offers the opposite. Food scraps tend to be wet, and water soaks up heat that a gasifier would otherwise put to work. Plant trimmings tend to be drier and woodier. It also seems reasonable to expect the mix to shift with the seasons, the academic calendar and the habits of the people on campus, although that expectation is this article's inference. The reported research includes feedstock behavior, along with optimization and system performance. In plain words, the team wants to learn how these materials act inside the system, how to tune the process and how well the whole machine runs.

Picture, purely as an illustration, a small test kitchen handed a changing basket of leftovers. One day the basket holds mostly hedge clippings. Another day it holds soggy salad and fruit peels. The cooks want to know whether their recipe survives the changes, how much finished dish comes out, and what cleanup remains afterward. The size of the pile waiting outside the kitchen door tells them how much food might someday be available. Only the tasting notes tell them what the recipe delivers. The comparison stops at that measurement problem, since real gasification involves fierce heat and gas chemistry far from any home stove. Still, it gives a useful way to read the numbers in this story: the 7-ton figure describes the pile.

UCLA's engineering school reported the project on October 22, 2026, and the campus newsroom followed on October 29. By then, testing and evaluation had been underway for about six months, roughly a third of the planned schedule. One image from the reports shows wood chips at Mote's Los Angeles pilot plant. The photo places real plant material at a real facility, which gives the partnership a physical presence. Its evidence stops there. Whether UCLA's own waste has gone through the system, in what amounts and with what results, are questions the picture leaves for later reporting.

## Three futures for one stream

The boldest numbers in the story are projections from the team. Hydrogen from the estimated campus stream, they say, could supply about 2% of UCLA's electricity. Alternatively, it could provide peak-period energy for several hundred hours a year. Or it could fuel approximately 100 hydrogen vehicles.

The word "or" carries a lot of weight. These are three alternative destinations for hydrogen, so the benefits should be read one at a time. A family with one paycheck can spend it on rent or a vacation, and the same holds for a single stream of fuel. Adding the three projections together would count one supply three times. All three are also forecasts of what could happen, and the pilot is still gathering the measurements that would test them.

Each option raises its own question, and each question exposes an assumption that matters. Start with electricity. A share of 2% means the other 98% would keep coming from other sources, which gives a useful sense of scale at the outset. The figure's meaning depends on the size of the campus electricity demand used in the calculation and on how much energy survives the trip from scraps to hydrogen to electricity. Every conversion step loses some energy. The gasifier, the purification equipment and the collection of waste also consume energy of their own. A figure that accounts for those demands would describe something quite different from a figure that describes gross output, and the details available here leave that method unspecified.

The peak-period option has a different logic. Electricity use rises and falls through the day and the year, and the moments of highest demand can put particular strain on a power system. Stored hydrogen could, in principle, be saved for those moments. A year contains 8,760 hours, so by this article's arithmetic several hundred hours would amount to a few percent of the calendar, possibly at the times when extra supply matters most. The projection gives a span of time. The size of the load it would meet remains to be stated in the information available here, and that size decides whether the option means a modest assist or a meaningful cushion.

The vehicle option seems the easiest to picture, and it may hide the most. Approximately 100 hydrogen vehicles sounds concrete, but a compact car, a shuttle bus and a heavy truck drink fuel at very different rates, and a vehicle driven twice as far needs about twice the hydrogen. The vehicle type and distance behind the estimate remain open in the information available here. So does the matter of a place to fill those tanks, since hydrogen transport is among the details still open.

Then comes the gap at the center of the whole story. Simple multiplication turns approximately 7 tons a day into roughly 2,500 tons a year, if the estimate held on every day of the calendar. That is a large heap of material, and even one day's worth weighs about as much as several family cars. The source says "tons" and leaves the exact definition of the unit unstated, so these comparisons serve only to convey rough scale. In the information available for this article, the estimate stops short of saying how much of the pile can be collected, how much is chemically suitable and how much reaches a gasifier. The underlying tables and calculation methods were unavailable for review, so the steps leading from a mass of waste to a mass of hydrogen, along with any measured yield from the pilot so far, remain open here. For now, the distance between the heap and the projected fuel remains the great unanswered question of this story.

## The carbon ledger

The climate side of the project rests on a simple sequence. Plants absorbed carbon from the air as they grew. The carbon then sat in their leaves, stems and fruit. The project aims to capture that carbon during processing and store it, keeping it out of the atmosphere while the hydrogen does useful work. Two terms help here. Capture means separating carbon-bearing material for handling. Sequestration adds the commitment to store it away for the long term.

The idea carries real appeal because of what it could add up to. If carbon pulled from the air by plants ended up in durable storage, the overall process might remove more carbon than it released, a result known as net carbon removal. That phrase sets a high bar. Reaching it depends on the full chain: collecting waste, moving it, preparing it, running the gasifier, purifying the hydrogen and delivering it for use, along with the emissions from each step. Storage has to last, too. Carbon can be stored in several forms in general, such as gas injected deep underground or carbon locked into solid material, and each carries its own questions of monitoring and durability.

For this project, capture and sequestration stand as stated aims. The storage site, the form of the stored carbon and the amount captured are all unsettled in the information available for this article. Independently audited totals across the whole process remain unknown, and so does any verified storage chain at campus scale. The climate payoff therefore sits in the same category as the hydrogen projections: a plausible goal awaiting measurement.

## Who does the measuring

The most firmly documented number in the project concerns people. Mote is supporting the pilot with $100,000 toward stipends, tuition and supplies for two chemical-engineering graduate students. A stipend is the living allowance that graduate researchers receive while they study. The arrangement gives the work a human shape: two students attached to a real system, studying how materials behave, how the process can be improved and how the equipment performs, under a faculty lead and alongside a company with a stake in the technology.

The sum should stay in its own lane. It describes research support for students. The pilot's full budget, the cost of building a larger plant, the price of the hydrogen and the conditions that would justify expansion all remain open. Those costs will weigh heavily on any future decision, because a process can succeed in chemistry and still struggle against cheaper ways of handling waste or buying energy. Mote's involvement also deserves plain notice. A company working in this field has an interest in promising results, which makes published data and outside review especially valuable once results arrive.

The reasons to follow this pilot are concrete. Testing has been underway since April 2026, Simonetti leads the UCLA team, Mote is a working partner, two graduate students are funded to do the research, and the schedule points to September 2027. The project also has a useful form of honesty built into its stage: a pilot exists to find out what happens when tidy plans meet messy material. The outcomes could include disappointing yields, stubborn feedstocks or costs that climb too high, and those results would still teach engineers something real about whether campus waste belongs in a hydrogen plant.

The clock now runs toward September 2027, or later if the extension comes. By then, the team should be far better placed to report on the questions its research covers, including how the feedstock behaves and how the system performs. The figures that could settle the bigger argument are easy to name: hydrogen produced per ton of waste, energy spent along the way, carbon stored and for how long, and the price of it all. When the pilot reaches the end of its schedule, how much of that estimated daily heap will have become measured hydrogen, and will the numbers be strong enough to send a campus's scraps down a new path?
