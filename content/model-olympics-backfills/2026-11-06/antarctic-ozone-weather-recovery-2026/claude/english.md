# The Antarctic Ozone Hole Swelled to a Size Last Seen About a Decade Ago. Is the Ozone Layer's Recovery Still on Course?

On November 4, 2026, two United States government science agencies reported that this year's Antarctic ozone hole was among the largest ever measured. NOAA, the agency that studies weather, oceans and the atmosphere, and NASA, the space agency, track the hole together each year using balloons, ground instruments and satellites. The hole is a severe seasonal thinning of the ozone layer, a widely spread band of gas high above Earth that absorbs harmful ultraviolet light from the Sun. The agencies reported that from September 7 to October 13, the peak of the depletion season, the hole averaged about 25 million square kilometers (9.6 million square miles), more than twice the area of the United States. That seasonal average ranks seventh largest in 48 years of monitoring.

The same announcement carried a second message that seems to pull against the first. The scientists said the ozone layer remains on course to recover later this century, and that this year's hole would have been wider under the heavier chemical burden of earlier years. Paul Newman, a senior scientist at the University of Maryland and longtime leader of NASA's ozone research team, said the hole had grown to a size last seen about a decade ago. He also said the layer is healing. Both statements come from the same monitoring effort, and the story of 2026 lies in how they fit together.

The answer begins with where ozone sits. Most of the protective layer lies in the stratosphere, the region of the atmosphere above the ordinary weather of the lower air. Ozone at that height shields living things from damaging solar radiation. Ozone near the ground is a harmful air pollutant, commonly produced by human activity. The same gas therefore plays opposite roles depending on its altitude, and the concern in Antarctica is the loss of the high, protective kind. This problem is also separate from greenhouse warming, although the two often get blended in conversation.

The damage traces back to useful products. Chlorofluorocarbons, known as CFCs, were once widely used in refrigerators, air conditioners and building insulation. These and related compounds contain chlorine or bromine, and they last a very long time. Air currents carry them up into the stratosphere, where they can do harm far from the kitchens and buildings that released them. In 1987, under an agreement called the Montreal Protocol, the international community committed to replacing ozone-depleting substances with less harmful alternatives.

A treaty can change what factories make within years, but the atmosphere responds on a much longer schedule. The old compounds can linger in the air for many decades, and they can keep leaching from aging products and landfills. That slow release explains why a commitment made in 1987 is still working its way through the sky in 2026. It also explains why the current recovery forecast reaches all the way to the 2070s.

## What the numbers measure

The 2026 report offers two figures for the hole's size, and each answers a different question. The first is the average of about 25 million square kilometers across the September 7 to October 13 window, the figure that earned seventh place. The second is the largest single day, September 18, when the hole reached 27.1 million square kilometers (10.5 million square miles). The agencies describe that one-day peak as about 90 percent of the largest ozone hole ever observed, on September 9, 2000. The seventh-place ranking belongs to the seasonal average, so the September 18 figure tells a separate story about one day at the height of the spread.

Both figures describe area, the way a map shows how far a lake extends across a valley. They tell us how widely the thinning spread. How much ozone remains at any given spot requires a different measurement.

That second measurement uses a unit named the Dobson unit, and it helps to picture how it works. Imagine gathering all the ozone in the column of air above one spot, from the lowest air to the top of the atmosphere. Then imagine squeezing that ozone into a pure layer at 0 degrees Celsius and the air pressure found at sea level. One Dobson unit equals 0.01 millimeters of that squeezed layer, so 100 units make 1 millimeter, which the NOAA release compares to the thickness of a dime. In the real sky, the ozone stays spread out through the air, so this tidy layer is a compressed-column comparison, a way of counting the gas. It describes the amount of ozone, and the actual ozone layer is far deeper and more diffuse.

NASA Ozone Watch uses this unit to define the hole itself. The area mapped as the hole is tied to a threshold of 220 Dobson units in the Antarctic region, equal to 2.2 millimeters under the squeezing comparison. The word "hole" can suggest an empty gap in the sky, but ozone remains inside it. The name marks a region of severe depletion, set by a threshold that scientists use for mapping.

Now the South Pole reading makes sense. NOAA scientists found that ozone directly overhead reached its lowest value of the year on October 17: 105 Dobson units, or 1.05 millimeters in squeezed terms. That is a little under half of the 220-unit line. It also sits above the lowest value ever recorded over the South Pole, 92 units in October 2006. NOAA says depletion within this year's hole was less severe than in the record-breaking holes of 10 or 20 years ago.

That comparison has clear boundaries. The 105-unit reading describes the air above one station, while the hole covers a vast region. NASA's Annual Records page lists a Southern Hemisphere minimum gathered by satellites, which covers a far larger footprint than a single station. When inspected on November 5, that table ended in 2025. The seventh-place rank therefore rests on the analysis the two agencies released this week, and the NASA and NOAA reports describe one joint monitoring effort, so they count as one body of evidence.

As an illustration only, picture a worn blanket held up to a window. The width of the worn patch resembles the mapped area of the hole. The amount of thread left at any one point resembles the Dobson reading. A patch can spread across more of the blanket while its thinnest point holds a little more thread than the worst spot on record. Roughly speaking, 2026 had that pattern: a wide patch, with a South Pole reading above the station's lowest mark. The comparison ends there, because a blanket sits still, while the atmosphere moves, mixes and reacts.

The monitoring system is built around that same difference in scale. Balloon-borne instruments released from the South Pole Baseline Atmospheric Observatory sample ozone at different heights as they rise. A ground instrument called the Dobson Ozone Spectrophotometer uses light to measure the ozone overhead. Satellites, which have been central to this work since 1979, supply the wide view. This year's contributors included instruments on NASA's Aura, on NOAA-20 and NOAA-21, and on the jointly operated Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership satellite. Each tool has its own footprint, and together they cover the fine detail and the broad map.

## Why this year's hole spread so far

The agencies attribute this season's unusual size to atypical weather high above Antarctica. The key feature is the polar vortex, a seasonal stream of wind that circles the continent in the stratosphere. It corrals very cold air over the polar region, and that frigid air allows polar stratospheric clouds to form. This high Antarctic circulation is a separate subject from the northern winter cold snaps that people may associate with the same phrase.

Amy Butler, a research scientist at NOAA's Chemical Sciences Laboratory, offered the main explanation for 2026. Huge weather systems, spanning a large part of the planet, normally rise from the lower atmosphere and help break up the vortex. In August and September this year, those waves were especially weak. Butler said that weakness was part of the reason the vortex grew unusually large. The strongest vortex winds stayed several degrees of latitude farther from the South Pole, enclosing a larger area with ideal conditions for ozone loss. Newman added that the powerful vortex extended depletion this season. Their account names the cause in physical terms, and the exact share of the hole's area due to each weather factor remains unquantified in the release.

Cold air sets the scene for a chain of chemistry. Human-made compounds containing chlorine and bromine drift up to the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation breaks them apart and frees the reactive atoms. Those atoms interact with the particles in polar stratospheric clouds, and this prepares the destructive chemistry that takes off when sunlight returns to the polar region. Chlorine is especially troublesome because it can take part in ozone destruction again and again, remaining available after each round. A small amount of it can therefore do outsized harm.

This chain shows how weather and chemicals divide the work. Weather helped determine how large the cold region became in 2026. The chemicals supply the capacity to destroy ozone within it. A season with an unusually broad vortex can therefore produce an alarming map even as the stock of destructive chemicals falls. The weather pattern and the chemical trend run on separate schedules, and this year's map shows the two overlapping.

## The evidence for a slow recovery

The agencies base their long-term view on several kinds of evidence, each with its own limits.

The first is chemical. Stephen Montzka, a senior scientist at NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory, said chlorine in the Antarctic stratosphere derived from ozone-depleting substances has fallen by about a third from its peak, with the release framing that decline relative to levels reached in the 1980s. This is the most direct sign of a shrinking burden, because chlorine is the fuel of the destruction described above. It is a measure of ingredients in the air, and any one season's map also depends on that season's weather.

The second is an estimate. Newman said the 2026 hole could have covered roughly 3.5 million square kilometers more if ozone-depleting substances were as common today as at their peak in the year 2000. That figure describes an estimated scenario, a comparison with a different chemical history. The observed hole is the 2026 one, and the 3.5 million figure is an estimated difference in area, separate from any measured patch of repaired sky. It is one scientist's estimate, presented by the agencies, and it uses a year-2000 reference point that differs from the 1980s comparison attached to the chlorine figure. The two belong in separate columns. A full judgment of the estimate's strength would require its technical method and range of uncertainty, along with an analysis of how much of the difference belongs to the Montreal Protocol specifically. The estimate does offer a useful way to think about this year: a large hole can still be smaller than the one the same weather might have produced under a heavier chemical load.

The third is timing. Newman said the hole has been forming later in the season in recent years, and he reads that as a sign of healing. The logic is that a hole's start date records something different from its peak size. The release states this trend in words, and measuring its strength would call for the long series of formation dates.

The fourth is a forecast. Scientists estimate that the ozone hole will recover by the 2070s as the older compounds break down. That date is a projection from the 2026 update, about a half-century away, and it carries the uncertainty of any long forecast. It describes a destination for the chemistry, and the size of next year's hole remains an open question that will also depend on that season's conditions.

Taken together, these strands suggest how to read a season like this one, and what follows is this article's interpretation of the agencies' evidence. Wind patterns can shift within weeks. Ozone loss plays out across a season. Chemical decline and international policy unfold over decades. When a slow downward trend in chemicals meets weather that swings from year to year, the record of hole sizes can rise and fall along the way. A seventh-place finish in 2026 means six seasons averaged larger, and it also means the sky over Antarctica still holds enough chlorine and bromine to do serious damage when conditions line up.

The report supports a limited conclusion, and it has real gaps. It documents continuing damage on a very large scale, and it documents a reduced chemical burden at the same time. The update focuses on ozone measurements and their causes. Any estimate of ultraviolet exposure, health effects or harm to ecosystems from the 2026 hole would need separate evidence, and so would any judgment of how well individual countries are keeping to the treaty. The figures describe the atmosphere. Their meaning for people and living things remains a further question.

That leaves the puzzle that this year's map poses to anyone following a long repair. Under the forecast, the Antarctic hole will return each southern spring for decades, and some of those years could bring a strong vortex and another frightening picture. The chlorine figures change slowly and quietly, while a hole more than twice the area of the United States arrives with immediate force. When the next wide hole opens, which measurement should the rest of us look at first, and how long would we have to watch before we could tell a bad year from a recovery that has stalled?
