# Most of the Oxygen Made in 37 Small Galaxies Is Missing From Their Disks. Where Did It Go?

Galaxies run on a slow cycle. Gas collapses into stars, stars forge heavier elements such as oxygen, and the biggest stars scatter those elements into space as they age and explode. In a study NASA reported on November 2, 2026, astronomers on a project called GLOW, short for Galaxies Losing Oxygen via Winds, audited that cycle in 37 small galaxies near our own. Kristen Wingfield McQuinn of Rutgers University and the Space Telescope Science Institute leads the project, and University of Utah astronomer Grace Telford is among its investigators. Their team estimated how much oxygen each galaxy's stars have made over its history, then compared that total with the oxygen now held in the galaxy's main disk of stars, gas and dust. On average, the disks hold 10.7 percent.

As an illustration of that average, imagine the stars of a galaxy making 100 units of oxygen. About 11 units would remain in the disk, and about 89 would lie outside the tally. This arithmetic describes the sample's mean, and individual galaxies differ from it. The result is also an inferred budget, built from many observations and calculations. Astronomers have yet to map the absent oxygen, so its present location remains an open question.

The chronology matters here. The authors' numerical analysis appeared as a preprint, a publicly shared research paper, dated August 13, 2026, and the figures in this article come from that version. NASA says the paper was published in *The Astrophysical Journal* on November 2. A comparison between the final journal text and the preprint remains to be done for this account.

## Why oxygen makes a useful ledger

Astronomers use the word "metals" in a way that surprises most people. In their vocabulary, a metal is nearly any element heavier than hydrogen and helium, so oxygen counts. The team chose oxygen for practical reasons. NASA describes it as the most abundant element after hydrogen and helium, relatively easy to detect, and made through a well-understood process. That makes it a good tracer for the wider family of heavy elements, although each element has its own history and oxygen stands in for the others imperfectly. This story concerns oxygen atoms spread through cosmic material. The breathable oxygen in Earth's air is a separate subject.

The 37 galaxies are dwarf galaxies, far smaller than the Milky Way. All are forming stars, and all are rich in gas and poor in heavy elements. Those two traits fit together comfortably, because the gas is overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium. The sample lies roughly 1 to 6 megaparsecs away. A parsec is about 3.26 light-years, so 6 megaparsecs comes to about 19.6 million light-years. The galaxies' stars weigh in at about 3 million to 3 billion solar masses. A solar mass uses the Sun as a yardstick for mass, so this range measures the total weight of a galaxy's stars and leaves their actual number as a separate question.

These galaxies were selected on purpose. According to NASA, their masses fall in the range where theory expects supernovas to expel a growing share of oxygen, and they sit close enough for Hubble to pick out individual stars. Most known dwarf galaxies lie too far away for this kind of study, so the 37 are a carefully chosen local group. They make a strong test and an incomplete portrait of dwarf galaxies everywhere.

Three regions matter in the search. The disk is the galaxy's main body, where its stars and densest gas live. Around it spreads the circumgalactic medium, a thin halo of gas. Beyond that lies the intergalactic medium, the very thin material between galaxies. Oxygen pushed out of a disk could linger in the halo, fall back into the galaxy later, or travel into intergalactic space. "Missing" in this study therefore has a narrow meaning: absent from the disk inventory.

## How to weigh what a galaxy has made

The census relied largely on existing archives, and each observatory contributed a different page of the ledger. Hubble's optical images resolve individual stars. Their colors and brightnesses reveal when a galaxy formed its stars and roughly what those stars are made of. NASA's retired Spitzer Space Telescope supplied infrared images at a wavelength of 3.6 microns, about one three-hundredth of a millimeter, and that light helps estimate the total mass of a galaxy's stars. Two radio observatories, the Very Large Array and the Australian Telescope Compact Array, traced atomic hydrogen gas. Measurements of oxygen abundance in the gas and estimates for dust completed the inventory.

NASA's headline credits Hubble with uncovering the missing oxygen. The paper shows a broader effort, with Hubble's images feeding a calculation that draws on several observatories and many assumptions. Hubble contributed crucial evidence about stars, while the missing oxygen itself emerged as the gap between two calculated totals.

The first total, oxygen produced, starts from the mass of stars a galaxy has formed. The team adopted a constant yield of 0.010, meaning one hundredth of a unit of new oxygen for each unit of gas turned into stars. It also adopted a return fraction of 0.43, the share of a stellar generation's mass that goes back into the gas. Together these numbers convert today's stars into a lifetime oxygen output. The authors assign a 33 percent uncertainty to stellar mass, based on the range of ways infrared light can translate into mass. They report that plausible changes in the return fraction shift the derived masses by less than 5 percent. The yield remains a chosen value applied across the whole sample, so production rises in lockstep with stellar mass by design.

The second total, oxygen retained, adds three reservoirs. Gas holds the largest share by far, stars hold a smaller one, and dust the smallest. Star composition is the least direct measurement. Individual stars in these galaxies are too distant for current facilities to split their light into detailed spectra, so the team inferred composition from color and brightness and added an extra margin of uncertainty for the assumed ratio of oxygen to iron. The team also ran its stellar analysis with two separate sets of stellar models, called PARSEC and MIST, and the choice made minimal difference to the overall result. That agreement is reassuring for the stars, the smaller reservoir. The yield and gas assumptions stay in place either way.

Gas is the ledger's sensitive spot, because the biggest reservoir also contributes the biggest uncertainty. Hydrogen often stretches far past a galaxy's visible stars, and oxygen may thin out with distance, so treating all that hydrogen as equally enriched would overstate the oxygen. The authors counted gas within 4.4 stellar scale lengths, a yardstick based on how quickly a disk's starlight fades outward, to balance coverage against what abundance measurements can support. The tally also leaves out molecular gas, which is seldom detected in such oxygen-poor galaxies and hard to weigh.

The sample's spread adds texture to the 10.7 percent average. Individual estimates run from about 3.8 percent to 24.8 percent, and 29 of the 37 galaxies sit at or below 15 percent. The standard deviation of 5.1 percentage points describes how much galaxies differ from one another, which makes it a separate quantity from the measurement error on the average. The highest retention estimates also carry the largest uncertainties. Retention rises modestly in galaxies with more stellar mass and deeper gravity, the expected direction, since stronger gravity should hold material more firmly. Within this sample, however, the trend is weak and heavily scattered.

## A model can pass one test and fail another

The sharpest turn in the story involves a familiar graph. Astronomers have long known about the mass–metallicity relation: galaxies with more stellar mass tend to have higher oxygen concentrations in their gas. A galaxy simulation that reproduces this pattern appears to understand galactic chemistry. In the GLOW sample, though, a galaxy's position relative to the relation shows no clear connection to the share of its oxygen that stayed in its disk.

A kitchen comparison helps with the bookkeeping, and it is purely an illustration. Picture two pots of soup with identical saltiness. One may hold far more salt, simply because it holds far more soup. A taste reveals concentration at this moment. Learning how much of all the salt ever added remains in the pot requires the cook's full record. Gas-phase oxygen abundance resembles the taste, and GLOW's retention fraction resembles the full record. The comparison explains concentration and total inventory; galactic winds govern the physical movement of oxygen.

The authors compared their results with three sets of hydrodynamical simulations, computer models that follow gas as it flows, forms stars and gets blown outward. All three reproduce the observed mass–metallicity relations and broad trends in how metals divide between stars and gas. All three also generally keep more heavy elements in their galaxies than GLOW finds. One set, from Muratov and colleagues, runs roughly 2 to 4 times higher. A second, from Christensen and colleagues, sits mostly within a factor of two and still on the high side. In the third, from Piacitelli and colleagues, most simulated galaxies run a factor of a few higher.

The comparison has real limits. Yields, return fractions and feedback recipes differ among the studies, and two of the three simulation sets report all metals where GLOW counts oxygen. Simulations may also define a galaxy's interstellar gas across a wider region than telescopes can sample. Christensen's team found that a tighter definition shrinks the gap, while some gap remains. The authors therefore treat overall trends as more trustworthy than exact values. They read the offsets as evidence that simulated feedback misses details of real chemical evolution, and they point out how little a scaling relation alone can distinguish between models. The disk-retention estimates give modelers a new constraint, subject to those differences in yields and disk definitions. A model can get a galaxy's oxygen concentration right and still overstate how much oxygen its disk has kept.

## The search outside the disk

Telford, speaking in NASA's report, reasons that oxygen made inside galaxies must once have been inside them, so its absence points to ejection powered by massive stars and supernova explosions. NASA adds that supernova-driven winds appear to remove oxygen from low-mass galaxies more effectively than current models predict. That is a strong physical interpretation. Where the oxygen stopped is a harder problem.

A 2014 study of Milky Way-like galaxies, described by NASA, found that they too lost much of their oxygen, with some of it in their surrounding halos. For dwarfs, the halo is far harder to examine. Its gas is thin and faint, so astronomers use a clever trick: they watch a bright, distant quasar through the halo and look for the fingerprints that the intervening atoms leave in its light. Hubble's Cosmic Origins Spectrograph does this work, but suitable background quasars are few, and detections are often weak.

Oxygen adds a further complication. The most useful form is O VI, oxygen atoms stripped of five electrons, and for the nearest galaxies its signal falls outside the wavelengths the spectrograph's high-resolution mode can record. Studies of nearby halos therefore lean on carbon and silicon, which stars make and scatter through different routes. O VI comes into range for more distant galaxies, whose light left them billions of years ago, when their halos may have looked different.

Those conditions shape the numbers. Drawing on earlier studies, the authors estimate that O VI holds 1 to 7 percent of produced oxygen. That literature-based range combines O VI observations with O VI inferred by modeling other ions, and direct measurements for every GLOW halo remain out of reach. An earlier comparison in the paper, based on a particular set of more distant galaxies, gives roughly 3 to 10 percent. If O VI holds about 20 percent of a halo's oxygen, the total halo share may reach 5 to 35 percent, and that "if" carries a lot of weight, because the correction may fail under real halo conditions. Set the generous 35 beside the sample's average of 11, purely as a rough illustration, and close to half the oxygen still has no address. The authors reach a similar conclusion: most of the oxygen remains unaccounted for.

Individual galaxies pull in different directions. A study of Sextans B, a GLOW galaxy, found about 0.5 percent of its metals in the cool inner halo. Work in preparation on Sextans A suggests that 80 to 100 percent of its missing carbon may remain in its halo, under a model that assumes the gas fills a large volume. Carbon is an imperfect stand-in for oxygen, so these results sharpen the puzzle and leave it open. One other study hints that some oxygen may sit just past the halo, within one to two virial radii, a measure of a galaxy's gravitational reach.

A galaxy's neighborhood may also play a part. GLOW galaxies more than about 1 megaparsec from a large neighbor consistently keep under 15 percent of their oxygen, while those closer in show more variety, and some in crowded regions tend to keep more. The authors suggest that denser surroundings may push expelled material back or let galaxies collect oxygen thrown out by others. They call the trend tentative, since it rests on a small sample.

The team also tuned a simple regulator model, a bookkeeping model that tracks gas flowing into and out of a galaxy. A good match came from winds that weaken with stellar mass in a specific proportion, expelled material with twice the metallicity, or heavy-element concentration, of the interstellar gas, and accretion, the inflow of new material, suppressed by 40 percent relative to the universal baryon fraction, the cosmic average share of ordinary matter. This recipe is a possible explanation, and direct measurement of those settings in these 37 galaxies remains ahead.

More observing is planned. NASA reports that McQuinn, who also heads the science operations center for the newly launched Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, has an approved program to use Roman to find still fainter nearby galaxies, and she hopes to study their stars with Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope. Telford is using Hubble to examine the massive stars in nearby GLOW targets, the very objects that make oxygen and help drive it away. Those projects are still to come, and better halo measurements around these same galaxies would test the budget most directly.

For now, the census leaves astronomers with a firmer set of books and a larger mystery. The stars of 37 small galaxies appear to have made far more oxygen than their disks now hold, and leading simulations predict fuller disks than these estimates show. If that oxygen is drifting between galaxies, small galaxies may be seeding intergalactic space with heavy elements. If it lingers in thin halos just beyond the reach of today's instruments, it could someday fall back into the galaxies that made it. Which of those fates claimed most of the oxygen?
