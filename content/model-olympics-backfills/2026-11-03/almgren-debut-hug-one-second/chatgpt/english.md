# One Mile Left: How Almgren Turned His First Marathon Into a New York Victory

Sweden’s Andreas Almgren won his first marathon in New York on November 1, 2026, making the decisive move with one mile remaining, according to race organizer New York Road Runners. He had stayed with the leading runners through 25 miles before securing the men’s open title. In a separate competition at the same event, Switzerland’s Marcel Hug won the men’s wheelchair race by a single second, earning his eighth New York title.

Almgren arrived with considerable experience of winning. New York Road Runners, known as NYRR, identifies him as the 2026 World Athletics Half Marathon champion and European 10,000-metre champion. His first attempt at the marathon distance brought an accomplished athlete into a new competitive test. The question was how far his established strengths would carry him, and what he would still be able to do when the finish finally drew near.

A marathon covers 42.195 kilometres, approximately 26.2 miles. A half marathon covers exactly half that distance, while a 10,000-metre race is 10 kilometres long. Those differences give Almgren’s transition its scale. He had demonstrated championship ability over shorter distances; New York asked him to sustain a winning performance over a substantially longer journey.

The setting carried its own history. The New York City Marathon began in 1970, and its course through the city’s five boroughs followed in 1976. The 2026 race therefore marked the fiftieth anniversary of that course. NYRR, a nonprofit race organizer, held this year’s event, bringing together separate men’s and women’s running and wheelchair competitions alongside participation on a much larger scale.

For Almgren, the interest lay in the meeting of experience and novelty. Championship success gave substance to his prospects. The longer distance introduced a fresh endurance test. Speed over a shorter course is valuable, and a marathon asks how effectively a runner can keep using that ability as the kilometres accumulate. Reaching the closing stages in contention is itself part of the work of winning.

An everyday comparison helps explain the change. Imagine a musician accustomed to giving an excellent 45-minute performance taking on a 90-minute concert. The skill and experience come along; the longer programme asks the performer to sustain quality across a larger span. The comparison illustrates the challenge of extending a performance, while marathon running brings its own physical demands. Proven ability must hold up over new ground.

NYRR’s account places Almgren with the lead pack through 25 miles. That pack is the group of runners competing together near the front. By then, he had covered more than 40 kilometres and remained among the contenders. His shorter-distance credentials had brought an intriguing question to the start; his position late in the race gave that question an immediate competitive form. Who could turn the remaining stretch into a winning advantage?

Being among the leaders and finishing first are distinct achievements. A runner can stay close enough to contend while the final order remains open. Almgren crossed that divide with one mile remaining, making the move the organizer describes as decisive. The significance lies in the sequence: he sustained his place in the contest over the long distance, then acted successfully near the end.

A mile is about 1.6 kilometres. Within an ordinary run, it is a meaningful stretch in its own right; at this point in a marathon, it follows roughly 40 kilometres of racing. That gives the timing of Almgren’s move its weight. His first experience of the distance had already carried him deep into the race when he produced the action that secured first place.

Italy’s Yeman Crippa finished second, with Lesotho’s Kamohelo Mofolo third, according to NYRR. Mofolo turned 21 on race day. The organizer also identifies him as the first athlete from Lesotho to reach this race’s podium, meaning its top three finishing places. His birthday thus coincided with a national milestone at the New York event.

Mofolo’s placing gives the men’s result a significance beyond the winner. A podium records three performances, each with its own place in an athlete’s career and a country’s sporting history. On November 1, the third-place result joined a personal anniversary to a first for Lesotho. Crippa’s second place completed a leading trio representing three countries, with two organizer-reported national milestones among them.

NYRR places Almgren’s success within two historical boundaries. It calls him the first male open-division winner at New York on a marathon-distance debut since Gebre Gebremariam in 2010. It also calls him the first Swede to win any division at the event. The debut distinction belongs specifically to the men’s open competition; the Swedish first extends across the event’s divisions. Together, these organizer-attributed claims locate his achievement within the race’s longer history.

The women’s open race offered another relationship between experience and achievement. Kenya’s Sheila Chepkirui won in 2:24:36—two hours, 24 minutes and 36 seconds—according to NYRR. This was her second New York title, following her victory in 2024. Her result added another winning performance at an event where she had already reached the top.

Almgren and Chepkirui therefore arrived at victory along different paths. He was testing himself over the full marathon distance for the first time. She was returning to a race she had already won. Their results give experience two roles in the day’s story: shorter-distance accomplishment carried into a longer event, and previous success at New York followed by another title there.

American Jess McClain took second place ahead of Hellen Obiri. NYRR’s account confirms their finishing order; the precise margin awaits verification. Hug won the men’s wheelchair race in 1:28:14, finishing one second ahead of American Daniel Romanchuk, according to NYRR. The victory extended Hug’s event record to eight titles. That pairing of facts gives the result its force: a long record of success at New York, with the latest title secured by a one-second margin.

Repeated victories can make a champion’s career seem orderly when viewed through a list of titles. The latest margin brings the individual contest back into focus. After a winning time of 88 minutes and 14 seconds, Hug and Romanchuk were separated by one second.
