One Company, Many Stories: Inside the Deal That Put HBO, CNN, CBS News and Two Film Studios Under One Roof

On Tuesday, October 6, 2026, a stock symbol stopped trading.

Warner Bros. Discovery, the company behind HBO, CNN and Warner Bros. Pictures, came off Nasdaq. On the New York Stock Exchange, a new ticker appeared: SKYD.

No single dramatic moment marked the change. It happened through a press release, a cash payment, and the quiet machinery of a closing. But what changed hands is one of the largest collections of storytelling infrastructure in the world.

Paramount announced that day that it had completed its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery. The combined company is called Skydance. David Ellison is chairman and CEO. Ynon Kreiz is co-CEO.

And under that one roof now sit CNN, CBS News, HBO, and both film studios.

Read that list again slowly. Two major American news organizations. One of the most influential premium television brands ever built. Two Hollywood studios.

One parent.

The Facts First

Before any opinion, here is what the company's October 6 closing announcement establishes. Everything in this section comes from that announcement or the company's March 2 acquisition presentation. My own analysis comes later, and I'll label it as such.

The deal closed on October 6, 2026. Paramount announced completion of its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery.

The new name is Skydance. David Ellison serves as chairman and CEO; Ynon Kreiz serves as co-CEO.

The combined group includes CNN, CBS News, HBO and both film studios.

Shareholders got cash. WBD shareholders received $31.01666668 in cash per share. WBD stopped trading on Nasdaq. Skydance began trading on the NYSE as SKYD.

Control is shared, not singular. The Ellison family and RedBird together hold all voting shares of the company.

The company has made promises about the future. It targets at least $6 billion in annual run-rate synergies within three years, and it promises at least 30 theatrical films per year.

That's the verified core. Short list. Huge consequences.

The Number Everyone Is Going to Get Wrong

When a deal this large closes, the headlines reach for the biggest number available. Expect to see "$110 billion" all week. You may also see "$81 billion." Some writers will treat these as competing estimates, as if analysts can't agree on the price.

They aren't competing. They measure different things.

In the March 2 acquisition presentation, the company described the deal at $81 billion in equity value and $110 billion in enterprise value.

Here is the plain-English difference, which is standard finance rather than anything specific in the company's materials:

Equity value is roughly what the shares are worth: what owners of the company's stock are being paid for.
Enterprise value is a broader measure of the whole business. It typically adds the company's debt and other claims on top of the equity, minus cash.

A concrete way to picture it: buy a house for $500,000 that still carries a $300,000 mortgage you agree to take over, and you hand the seller far less than the full cost of the house. Equity is the seller's slice. Enterprise value is the whole house.

So the $29 billion gap between those two figures is not a disagreement. It is two different lenses on one transaction.

And neither number is a personal cheque. David Ellison did not personally pay $110 billion. He did not personally pay $81 billion. The voting control sits with the Ellison family and RedBird together. Any story that frames this as one man writing one check for HBO is telling you a fairy tale with a spreadsheet attached.

Then there is the third number: $31.01666668 per share. That is what WBD shareholders actually received at closing. It is slightly above the announced $31, by about 1.67 cents per share. The materials I'm working from don't explain the difference, so I won't guess at it.

Takeaway: In mega-deals, the biggest number is rarely the most honest one. Ask what each figure is measuring before you repeat it.

Who Actually Holds the Wheel

It is tempting to make this a story about one person. Media coverage loves a single face, and David Ellison, as chairman and CEO, will be that face.

But the structure matters.

Voting control belongs jointly to the Ellison family and RedBird. The day-to-day leadership has two titles at the top: Ellison as chairman and CEO, Kreiz as co-CEO.

That's a narrower circle than a typical widely held public company, where voting power is scattered among thousands of shareholders. Here, the shares that carry votes sit with one family and one investment firm, together.

Whether that concentrated control turns out to be a strength (faster decisions, a clear long-term vision) or a vulnerability (fewer checks on those decisions) is not something a closing announcement can tell us. Only the next few years can.

The Two Promises, and Why They Are Only Promises

The company put two specific numbers on the table.

Promise one: at least $6 billion in annual run-rate synergies within three years.

"Synergies" is one of the most used and least examined words in corporate life. In mergers, it generally refers to the financial benefit expected from combining two businesses, through some mix of lower costs and new revenue.

Here's what matters: this is a target. As of October 7, 2026, it is not achieved savings. It is not a measured result. It is a goal with a three-year deadline.

And the brief I'm working from does not establish where those billions will come from. It does not establish job cuts. It does not establish changes to what you pay for streaming or cable. If you read elsewhere that those things are confirmed, ask for the source. On the record available today, they are not.

Promise two: at least 30 theatrical films per year.

This is the one I find most interesting, and it's why I put it in the counter-intuitive headline.

Thirty is a commitment to output, specifically to movies made for theaters. In an era when many observers have worried about the future of the cinema release, a company publicly pinning a minimum number of theatrical films to its name is a statement of intent.

But notice what it is not. It is not a promise about quality. It is not a measure of creative improvement. A commitment to 30 films tells you how many doors the studio plans to open. It cannot tell you who gets to walk through them, or what they'll be allowed to make once inside.

Takeaway: A target is a bet placed in public. It becomes a result only when someone checks.

What We Don't Know (and Shouldn't Pretend To)

This is the section most deal coverage skips, so I'll make it explicit.

Based on the verified material available as of October 7, 2026, none of the following is established:

Job cuts. No confirmed numbers, no confirmed plans in this brief.
Editorial intervention at CNN or CBS News. No evidence in the record of changes to how either newsroom operates.
Consumer prices. No confirmed changes to what audiences pay.
Creative outcomes. No measured improvement or decline in the work itself.

You will see confident predictions about all four. Some may eventually prove right. But a prediction dressed up as a fact does real damage, especially in a story about who controls information.

The honest position today is uncomfortable but simple: we know the shape of the new company. We don't yet know its behaviour.

My Analysis: The Machinery Behind the Stories

Everything from here on is my interpretation, not a reported fact.

Most people think of media companies in terms of what they make: a show, a film, a news broadcast. I'd suggest thinking about them in terms of three verbs.

Choose. Fund. Distribute.

Every story that reaches you went through all three. Someone decided it was worth making. Someone paid for it. Someone delivered it to a screen in front of you.

What happened on October 6 is that more of that three-step machinery now runs through one corporate owner. The same group now sits above two newsrooms, a premium TV brand, and two film studios. The decisions about which projects get green-lit, how much money they receive, and where they appear are now, at the top, made inside one structure.

That isn't an accusation. It's a description of where the levers are.

The question was never whether one company can tell good stories. It's how many different kinds of stories survive when the same company decides which ones get told.

The case that scale helps creators

There's a serious argument on this side, and it deserves to be stated fairly.

A bigger company can afford bigger risks. A promise of 30 theatrical films a year, if kept, means more slots on the slate, not fewer. A combined library and distribution network could give a project more ways to find an audience. For a filmmaker, a studio with deep pockets and long-term stability may be a better partner than a studio fighting for survival.

Scale can be a shield. A diversified company can absorb a flop without panicking.

The case that scale reduces choices

There's also a serious argument on the other side.

When two major buyers become one, a creator pitching a project has one fewer door to knock on. A writer whose pitch is rejected by one studio used to be able to walk it across the street. Now, in some cases, "across the street" is the same building.

The same logic applies to talent negotiating pay and terms, to independent producers selling finished work, and potentially to journalists considering where they can do their work. Fewer buyers usually means less leverage for sellers.

Where I land

Honestly? Undecided, and I think that's the right place to be on October 7.

Scale is a tool. It amplifies whatever decisions are made with it. A combined company with a commitment to range could fund more ambitious, more varied work than either predecessor could alone. A combined company optimizing hard for that $6 billion target could narrow the field.

The structure doesn't decide the outcome. The choices made inside it do. And those choices haven't been made in public yet.

How to Judge This Deal: A Scorecard You Can Actually Use

Instead of waiting for pundits to declare victory or disaster, here are concrete things anyone can track over the next three years. This list is my suggestion, not something the company has published.

Count the films. The company promised at least 30 theatrical releases a year. Are they hitting it? Annually, not as an average buried in a footnote.
Check the range of those films. Are they mostly sequels and franchise extensions, or is there room for original, mid-budget, and riskier work? Thirty films of the same type is a very different promise kept than thirty films of different types.
Follow the synergy reporting. The $6 billion run-rate target has a three-year window. When the company reports progress, look at where the savings come from.
Watch the newsrooms on their own terms. Judge CNN and CBS News by their journalism: what they investigate, what they publish, how they handle stories involving their own parent company. Demand evidence before believing claims of interference, and demand transparency to rule it out.
Watch what creators say on the record. Not anonymous rumours. Named people, describing their actual experience pitching, negotiating and working inside the new structure.
Track your own bill. If prices change, that will be visible. Until then, it's speculation.

Takeaway: Don't judge a merger on the day it closes. Judge it on the promises it made, the day they come due.

The Bottom Line

On October 6, 2026, Warner Bros. Discovery stopped trading, Skydance started, and WBD shareholders were paid just over $31 a share in cash. HBO, CNN, CBS News and two film studios now share one parent, led by David Ellison as chairman and CEO and Ynon Kreiz as co-CEO, with voting control held jointly by the Ellison family and RedBird.

The company has made two big promises: $6 billion in run-rate synergies within three years, and at least 30 theatrical films a year. Both are targets. Neither is a result.

Everything else (jobs, newsroom independence, prices, the quality of what gets made) is still unwritten.

A ticker change takes a second. Finding out what it means takes years.

Ownership is decided in a day. Character is revealed over a decade.
