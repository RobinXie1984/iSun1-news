The Hardest Thing in Mathematics Isn't Being Right — It's Admitting You Weren't, in Public, on a GitHub Log.
On October 6, 2026, OpenAI announced a trove of mathematical results generated by an unreleased internal model. Twenty-four hours later, a revision log told a different story — and that second story might matter more.
Let's start with the number that stops you cold.
Seven hundred and nineteen.
That's how many manuscripts OpenAI's current GitHub repository lists, spread across 372 mathematical families, sitting at various stages of verification. Not abstracts. Not blog posts. Not press releases dressed up in LaTeX. Manuscripts — dense, technical documents wrestling with Weil classes, Kuga–Satake correspondences, products of K3 surfaces. The kind of objects that make non-specialists' eyes glaze over and make specialists lean forward.
On October 6, 2026, OpenAI announced these results publicly. The framing was ambitious: an unreleased internal model had produced mathematical output at a scale that would have taken a human research group years to generate. Sam Altman is the company's CEO. The public record supplies no direct quotation from him about these specific proofs, and there is no evidence in the brief that he personally worked on any of them. But his name is attached to the company, and the company's name is attached to the repository, and so the announcement landed with the weight of institutional promise.
Here's what the promise looked like: a machine that doesn't just calculate but conjectures. That doesn't just verify but discovers. That steps into the territory once reserved for people who spend decades building intuition, and produces 719 manuscripts.
It sounded, to put it mildly, like a lot.
And then came October 7.
The Log
If you go to the OpenAI math repository on GitHub — the link is public, the history file is public, the whole apparatus is public — you will find a revision log dated October 7, 2026. It is not a press release. It is not a triumphant thread on social media. It is a changelog, the kind of thing software engineers write when they push a commit and want the next person to understand what changed.
And what changed was this: a sign error had invalidated a construction. Not a typo in a comment. Not a formatting glitch. A sign error — a plus where there should have been a minus, or vice versa — that collapsed a mathematical construction and, with it, two dependent papers.
Three manuscripts were withdrawn. The log names them explicitly. They concern Weil classes, Kuga–Satake correspondences, and products of K3 surfaces. The withdrawn originals remain linked in the repository. They have not been scrubbed. They have not been quietly deleted at 2 a.m. They sit there, visible, part of the record.
Read that again, because it is easy to skip over in the rush to the next headline. The withdrawn work is still there. You can go look at what was wrong. You can trace the error through the dependency chain. The architecture of the correction is, by design, transparent.
But the log doesn't stop at three withdrawals. It also records 14 revised manuscripts and 13 dependency-reference updates. And here is where precision matters, because it would be very easy — and very wrong — to lump those numbers together and say "30 manuscripts had problems." They are not interchangeable failure counts. A revision is not a withdrawal. A dependency-reference update is not an admission that the underlying mathematics is false. These are different categories, describing different interventions, and collapsing them would misrepresent what actually happened.
Fourteen manuscripts were revised. Thirteen had their dependency references updated. Three were withdrawn because a sign error broke the chain of logic they relied on. The total picture is one of a living document being maintained, not a monolith cracking.
What the Numbers Don't Say
Now, let's talk about what the repository does and does not claim, because this is where the story gets genuinely interesting and genuinely easy to misread.
The current README — the front page of the GitHub repository, the first thing you see — lists those 719 manuscripts across 372 families at "different verification stages." Approximately 42 percent of top-line results have been formalized.
Forty-two percent.
That number is doing a lot of work, and it deserves careful handling. Formalization, in this context, means that a result has been encoded in a way that a computer proof assistant can check. It is a rigorous process. It is not, however, the same thing as blanket independent validation of every manuscript. It does not mean that every result has been peer-reviewed by human mathematicians in the traditional journal sense. It does not mean that every result constitutes a novel discovery. It does not mean that every result has demonstrated applicability beyond the theoretical.
And — this is the other side of the coin, the side that gets lost when people want a simple narrative — formalization's absence does not make the unformalized work false. Fifty-eight percent of top-line results have not yet been formalized. That is not a verdict of incorrectness. It is a statement about the queue. It means the work is pending, not that it is wrong.
The brief is explicit about this, and I want to honor that explicitness: the numbers describe the dated repository as it existed at the time of collection, October 9, 2026, in Hong Kong. They do not represent an independent mathematics audit. Nobody outside OpenAI has compiled or adjudicated these proofs. To call all 719 manuscripts "established discoveries" would be an overstatement. To call them "peer-reviewed papers" would be inaccurate. To call the unformalized majority "false" would be equally wrong, just in the opposite direction.
The truth is messier than either extreme. The truth is: this is a large, active, partially verified body of mathematical output, maintained in public, subject to correction in public, and still very much in motion.
The Real Story Isn't the Error
Here is my analysis, clearly labeled as such, separate from the sourced facts above.
The tempting narrative is scandal. AI math machine caught making mistakes! Three papers withdrawn! Altman's grand project stumbles! It writes itself. It has a villain, a fall, a public reckoning. It is the story that gets shared because it confirms a suspicion many people already hold: that machine-generated mathematics is a house of cards, that speed without understanding is just sophisticated guesswork, that you cannot automate the thing that makes mathematics mathematics — the careful, human, years-long process of being sure.
And there is a kernel of truth in that suspicion. A sign error is not a small thing. In mathematics, a sign error can be the difference between a theorem and a counterexample. It can invalidate not just the result where it appears but every result that depends on it, cascading through a dependency graph like a crack through glass. Three manuscripts gone because of one wrong sign is a vivid reminder that mathematical truth is fragile, that it requires vigilance, that no amount of computational power eliminates the need for checking.
But here is the counter-narrative, and I think it is the more important one: the correction was public, immediate, and structurally embedded.
Think about how mathematical errors are normally handled. A paper is published in a journal. Years later, someone notices a gap. A correction is issued, maybe. Or maybe it isn't. Maybe the error propagates silently through a dozen citing papers before anyone catches it. Maybe it never gets caught. The history of mathematics is littered with results that were "known" to be true for decades before someone realized the proof had a hole in it. The correction, when it came, was slow, quiet, and often buried in a footnote.
OpenAI's model inverts that. The revision log is dated. It is specific. It names the withdrawn manuscripts. It keeps the originals linked. It distinguishes between revisions, dependency updates, and withdrawals. It does not pretend the error didn't happen. It does not rebrand it as a "learning opportunity" in a corporate blog post. It logs it, the way an engineer logs a bug fix, and moves on.
That is not a scandal. That is a mechanism. And mechanisms, unlike reputations, can be improved.
The editorial tension here is real, and I don't want to paper over it: output can outrun checking. A system that produces 719 manuscripts will, almost by definition, produce errors faster than any human team can catch them. The sign error in the Kuga–Satake construction is proof of that. The machine moved fast, and the machine broke something, and the breaking was only visible because the checking infrastructure existed and was public enough to register the break.
But the alternative is not a world without errors. The alternative is a world where errors are invisible. Where 719 manuscripts are published and no one checks, no one logs, no one withdraws, and the sign error sits there forever, quietly poisoning everything downstream. The fact that the correction happened — publicly, specifically, within 24 hours of the announcement — is not evidence of failure. It is evidence that the correction infrastructure works.
Or, more carefully: it is evidence that the correction infrastructure exists and was activated once. Whether it will continue to work at scale, whether the 42 percent formalization rate will climb, whether the remaining 58 percent will survive contact with formal verification — those are open questions. The brief does not answer them. I cannot answer them. Nobody can, as of October 9, 2026.
The Altman Question
Sam Altman's name is on the company. The company's name is on the repository. But the brief supplies no direct quotation from him about these proofs. There is no evidence that he personally worked on the Weil class construction or the K3 surface products. He is the CEO, not the author, and the distinction matters.
In the public imagination, though, the name does the work. "OpenAI's math release" becomes "Altman's math release" becomes "Altman's math error," and the compression is lossy in ways that matter. The people who built the model, who wrote the manuscripts, who caught the sign error and updated the log — their names are not in the brief. They are not in the headlines. They are in the commit history, if you go look.
This is a small point, but it is not a trivial one. The story of AI-generated mathematics is not the story of one CEO's ambition. It is the story of a system — models, verify, formalize, correct, repeat — and the system is bigger and more distributed than any single name. Giving one person credit for the output and blame for the error distorts both.
What This Actually Means
Let me try to land the plane.
On October 6, 2026, OpenAI announced a large body of mathematical results from an internal model. On October 7, a revision log recorded a sign error that invalidated one construction and led to the withdrawal of three manuscripts, the revision of 14 others, and 13 dependency-reference updates. As of October 9, the repository lists 719 manuscripts in 372 families, with roughly 42 percent of top-line results formalized. The withdrawn manuscripts remain publicly linked. The correction categories are distinct and should not be conflated. Formalization is not the same as peer review, and the absence of formalization is not the same as falsehood.
That is the factual skeleton. Everything I have said about mechanisms, narratives, and implications is analysis, layered on top, and you are free to disagree with any of it.
But here is the line I want to leave you with, the one I think captures what actually happened in those 48 hours:
The most radical thing OpenAI's math project did this week was not produce 719 manuscripts. It was admit, in writing, in public, within one day, that one of them was wrong.
Mathematics, as a culture, has struggled with that admission for centuries. Journals retract, but slowly. Authors correct, but quietly. Errors propagate because the cost of admitting them is high and the infrastructure for correcting them is low. What OpenAI built — intentionally or not — is a correction layer. A changelog for mathematical truth. And the first entry in that changelog was not a triumph. It was a sign error.
That is not a reason to dismiss the project. It is a reason to watch it closely. Because if the correction layer holds — if the log keeps getting updated, if the formalization rate keeps climbing, if the withdrawn manuscripts stay linked and the dependencies stay honest — then what we are looking at is not just a math engine. It is a new kind of mathematical record. One where being wrong is not a scandal but a data point. One where the history file is as important as the theorem.
And if it doesn't hold — if the corrections slow down, if the withdrawn manuscripts quietly disappear, if the 42 percent stalls and the sign errors multiply — then the log will tell us that, too. That's the thing about public records. They cut both ways.
The sign error is in the history. The correction is in the history. The 716 manuscripts that weren't withdrawn are in the repository, waiting for the next round of verification, the next formalization pass, the next pair of eyes — human or otherwise — to look at them and say yes or not yet or no.
The math is not done. The math is never done. But for the first time, the process of getting it less wrong is happening in the open, on a public GitHub log, timestamped and linked and impossible to ignore.
That, more than any theorem about K3 surfaces, is the result worth watching.
