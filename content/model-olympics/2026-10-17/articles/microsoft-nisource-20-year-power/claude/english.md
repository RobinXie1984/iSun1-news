# Three Headline Options

**Suspenseful:** Microsoft Just Signed for 1,100 Megawatts in Indiana. The Household Savings Haven't Arrived Yet.

**Emotional Resonance:** Who Pays for the AI Boom's Electricity? One Indiana Utility Says It Won't Be You.

**Counter-intuitive:** A Data Center Could Lower Your Power Bill. Here's the Fine Print.

---

# The Promise Is Signed. The Savings Are Still a Forecast.

*Inside NiSource's Microsoft deal, and the question every household near an AI campus should be asking*

---

Picture a kitchen table in northwest Indiana.

A power bill sits on it. It's an ordinary bill, the kind that arrives every month whether anyone asks for it or not.

A few miles away, ground has been set aside for something that will one day use more electricity than many towns. It's a data-center campus, and it will run the computing that the AI economy depends on.

The question connecting the bill and the campus is simple to state and hard to answer:

**When a tech giant moves in next door and needs a huge amount of power, who pays for it?**

On October 16, 2026, NiSource gave its answer. It announced a long-term energy agreement with Microsoft. It said Microsoft will pay its own way. It said existing customers are meant to be shielded. It also said customers could end up *saving* money.

That story deserves a careful read, because there are two stories in it:

**What has been signed.** And **what has been projected.**

Those are not the same thing.

---

## Part One: What Actually Happened

Here are the facts as NiSource disclosed them. These come from the company's own announcement. They are not an independent audit, and nobody has yet checked them against a single real bill.

**The deal.** NiSource announced an energy agreement with Microsoft for its previously announced data-center campus in northwest Indiana.

**The timeline.** Microsoft is *expected* to begin electric service in **2028**, under a **20-year term**.

**The scale.** The pooled generation portfolio of GenCo, NiSource's generation vehicle, is set to grow by approximately **1,100 megawatts**.

**The structure.** NiSource says Microsoft **fully funds the generation and transmission** needed to serve its load. According to the company, the GenCo structure is designed to **protect existing NIPSCO electric customers** from incremental data-center costs.

**The savings claim.** NiSource raised its estimate of **total expected NIPSCO customer savings over the life of its data-center contracts from $1.4 billion to approximately $1.75 billion.** It also cited savings of **as much as $140 per customer annually.**

**The batteries.** GenCo contracted **600 MW of battery storage** through a long-term tolling agreement. The facility is to be developed by an affiliate of AGP Group, and it serves **multiple large-load customers**, not only Microsoft.

**What comes next.** NiSource promised more financial and operational detail on its **third-quarter earnings call**.

**The risks.** The release lists forward-looking risks, including regulatory approvals, construction, financing, customer plans, and the possibility of **termination or reduced capacity**.

NiSource CEO **Lloyd Yates** and Microsoft energy executive **Jeff Riles** both framed the agreement around affordability. That is the extent of what they said in the material. Anything beyond it would be invention.

So much for the facts. Now the reading.

---

## Part Two: The Word Doing the Heavy Lifting Is "Expected"

*(Analysis begins here. What follows is my interpretation, not company disclosure.)*

Read the announcement a second time and look at the verbs.

Microsoft is **expected** to begin service.
The portfolio is **to grow**.
The battery facility is **to be developed**.
The savings are **expected**.

None of those verbs describes the present. All of them describe the future.

**Nothing in this deal is delivering electricity to anyone today.** The campus has no service yet, and a household has nothing yet on its bill. The 2028 start date is more than a year away, and the 20-year term runs into the late 2040s.

That doesn't make the deal hollow. Large energy contracts are built this way: commitments come first, then construction, then power. It does change what the deal *is*.

> **The deal is a blueprint, not a building.**

A blueprint can be excellent and still never become a building. Permits get denied. Financing gets expensive. Customers change their plans. NiSource names every one of those risks in its own release.

---

## Part Three: Megawatts Are Not What You Pay For

Here's a trap that catches even sharp readers.

**1,100 MW sounds like a quantity of electricity. It isn't.**

A megawatt measures **power**, meaning how fast energy can flow at a given moment. Your bill charges for **energy**, measured in kilowatt-hours, or megawatt-hours at utility scale.

The car analogy works well here:

- **Megawatts are the speedometer.** They tell you how fast the car can go.
- **Megawatt-hours are the odometer.** They tell you how far it actually went.

A car with a top speed of 200 mph tells you nothing about how many miles it was driven last year.

To make it concrete, here is a purely illustrative calculation, not a disclosed figure. If 1,100 MW ran at full output for every hour of a year, that would be about 9.6 million megawatt-hours. Real generation portfolios don't run that way. Output depends on fuel, weather, maintenance, and demand. The disclosure doesn't tell us the real number.

**The same caution applies to the batteries.**

A 600 MW battery tells you how fast it can discharge, not how long it can keep going. A 600 MW battery with two hours of duration holds about half the energy of a 600 MW battery with four hours. The release gives no duration. **The battery's actual energy capacity is not established.**

It also gives no total contract price, no generation fuel mix, and no carbon emissions estimate.

> **Read every megawatt figure as the size of the door, not the volume of what walks through it.**

## Part Four: The $140 Question

This is the number most likely to spread, and most likely to be misread.

**"As much as $140 per customer annually."**

Look at the first three words: **as much as.**

That is a ceiling, not an average, and certainly not a promise. It doesn't mean every household gets $140 back. It doesn't mean a check arrives. It doesn't mean any particular bill will go down by any particular amount.

It describes the upper end of a projection.

The headline total needs the same scrutiny. **$1.75 billion** sounds like a windfall from Microsoft. Here's what the number actually is:

1. **It's a portfolio total.** It covers expected savings across NiSource's data-center contracts, not this deal alone.
2. **It's a lifetime figure.** It is spread across the life of those contracts, not one year.
3. **It's an increase, not a new creation.** The estimate rose from **$1.4 billion** to about **$1.75 billion**. The change is roughly **$350 million**, and even that increase reflects the whole updated portfolio rather than a sum Microsoft alone produces.

So the accurate summary is not "Microsoft brings $1.75 billion in savings to Indiana households."

It's closer to this: **NiSource now projects about $1.75 billion in total customer savings over the life of its data-center contracts, up from $1.4 billion, with individual savings of up to $140 a year.**

That's less exciting, and it's also accurate.

> **A forecast is not a refund. A ceiling is not an average.**

## Part Five: How Could a Data Center Lower Anyone's Bill?

This is the counter-intuitive part, and it's worth taking seriously. The usual fear runs in the other direction.

The common worry about data centers goes like this. A huge new customer arrives and the utility builds new plants and power lines to serve it. Those costs go into the rate base, the rate base is shared, and ordinary households end up covering part of a tech company's infrastructure.

NiSource's stated design is meant to break that chain in two ways.

**First, cost isolation.** If Microsoft fully funds the generation and transmission its load requires, as NiSource says, then the new infrastructure isn't supposed to be charged to households.

**Second, shared fixed costs.** *(This is my interpretation of a general mechanism. The release doesn't spell out its savings method.)* Utilities carry large fixed costs that exist no matter how many customers they serve. When a big, creditworthy customer pays for its own new assets and also contributes to the shared system, the fixed costs can be spread across a larger base. Each existing customer's share can shrink as a result.

That is the logic that makes "a data center could lower your bill" plausible instead of absurd.

**The gap between plausible and proven is exactly what the current material doesn't close.** The release describes a structure *designed* to protect customers. It doesn't, and can't yet, show that bills will never rise, because rates are set over years in regulatory proceedings that haven't happened.

> **"Designed to protect" is an engineering goal. "Protected" is a measurement. We only have the first one.**

## Part Six: What We Still Don't Know

Here is the honest ledger of open questions. Each one matters for whether the household story holds up.

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| Will Microsoft's service actually begin in 2028? | Expected, not certain |
| What will regulators approve? | Pending |
| What's the battery's duration and energy capacity? | Not disclosed |
| What's the total contract price? | Not disclosed |
| What fuels will power the 1,100 MW? | Not disclosed |
| What are the carbon emissions? | Not established |
| What will households actually save? | Projected, not measured |
| What happens if Microsoft reduces capacity or terminates? | Flagged as a risk; consequences not detailed |

The last row deserves extra attention.

NiSource itself lists **possible termination or reduced capacity** as a risk. That's standard disclosure language, but it points at a real scenario: a 20-year contract that doesn't last 20 years. If that happened, the savings forecast would have to be redone. The structure's real test would come in the downside case, not the base case.

The third-quarter earnings call is the next checkpoint. That's where the company says more detail will come.

---

## Part Seven: Why This Deal Matters Beyond Indiana

*(Analysis.)*

Zoom out and this is one of the defining questions of the AI build-out:

**Can AI infrastructure pay its own way?**

Every large model, every chatbot answer, every training run ends at a power plant somewhere. Demand from data centers is reshaping utility planning. Communities near those campuses are entitled to ask whether they're paying for someone else's expansion.

The NiSource–Microsoft structure is one attempted answer. It runs the costs through a separate generation vehicle, makes the large customer fund its own infrastructure, and pitches the arrangement to households as a potential benefit instead of a burden.

If it works, it becomes a template other utilities and tech companies will copy.

If it doesn't, it becomes a cautionary tale about how far a press release can run ahead of a bill.

At this moment, on October 17, 2026, **it's neither.** It's a signed commitment wrapped around a forecast.

---

## Five Lines to Keep

1. **A blueprint isn't a building.** Signed contracts for 2028 service deliver nothing in 2026.
2. **Megawatts are the speedometer, not the odometer.** Capacity says nothing about the energy you'll actually pay for.
3. **"As much as" is a ceiling.** $140 is the top of a projection, not a check in the mail.
4. **$1.75 billion is a lifetime portfolio total.** It isn't a Microsoft gift and it isn't annual.
5. **"Designed to protect" isn't "protected."** One is intent and the other is a measurement taken years from now.

---

## The Bottom Line

Back to that kitchen table.

The bill on it will look the same next month as it did last month. Nothing in this announcement changes it yet.

What has changed is that a utility has made a public, specific claim: a tech giant will cover its own power costs, and households may come out ahead. That claim can now be checked against regulatory filings, earnings calls, and eventually real bills.

That's the right way to treat a story like this. Not with cynicism and not with celebration, but with a calendar. The claims were dated October 16, 2026, and the evidence will come in over the next two decades.

**Microsoft has signed for power. The household savings remain a forecast.**
