Suspenseful: Microsoft’s Indiana Power Pact: The 1,100 MW Question Hanging Over Household Bills Until 2028

Emotional Resonance: The Deal That Could Mean Up to $140 a Year Back—If the Projections Hold and the Wires Actually Get Built

Counter-intuitive: Why Microsoft Fully Funding Its Own Data-Center Power Still Doesn’t Guarantee Your Electric Bill Never Rises

On October 16, 2026, NiSource announced a long-term energy agreement with Microsoft tied to a previously announced data-center campus in northwest Indiana. Microsoft is expected to begin electric service in 2028 under a 20-year term. NiSource’s GenCo pooled portfolio is to grow by approximately 1,100 MW of planned resources. These are commitments for future capacity and service, not electricity already flowing today. A megawatt measures the rate of power the system must be ready to deliver; it is not the same as a megawatt-hour of energy actually consumed and billed.

NiSource states that Microsoft fully funds the generation and transmission needed for its load. The GenCo structure is designed to protect existing NIPSCO electric customers from incremental data-center costs. That is the disclosed structure and the stated intent. It is not an audit proving that no household bill can ever rise for any other reason, nor is it a guarantee that every future cost item outside this arrangement remains frozen.

The company also raised its total expected NIPSCO customer savings over the life of data-center contracts from $1.4 billion to approximately $1.75 billion. This figure is a portfolio total spanning multiple contracts and many years. It is not $1.75 billion newly created by the Microsoft agreement alone, and it is not an annual figure. The companion claim of “as much as $140 per customer annually” is described as an upper projection, not a universal cheque or a guaranteed reduction on every bill.

Separately, GenCo contracted 600 MW of battery storage through a long-term tolling agreement with a facility to be developed by an AGP Group affiliate. The batteries are intended to serve multiple large-load customers, not Microsoft exclusively. Battery duration in megawatt-hours, total contract price, the generation fuel mix behind the broader portfolio, actual carbon emissions, and measured household savings are not established in the announcement. Further financial and operational detail is promised on the third-quarter earnings call. The release itself flags regulatory approvals, construction, financing, customer plans, and possible termination or reduced capacity among forward-looking risks.

NiSource CEO Lloyd Yates and Microsoft energy executive Jeff Riles have described the arrangement in terms of affordability goals. No independent rate audit accompanies the release. The central open question the announcement leaves on the table is whether AI infrastructure can pay its own way without shifting costs onto households.

The distinction between capacity and energy is not semantic. An 1,100 MW addition is a large block of planned power-plant and transmission capability. Whether that capacity is used at a high capacity factor, a moderate one, or only during peaks determines how many megawatt-hours actually move and how the fixed costs of building it are recovered. A 20-year contract that begins in 2028 means the first years of operation, if they occur on schedule, will still be years from now. Construction, interconnection queues, equipment lead times, and regulatory timelines sit between the signature and the meter.

NiSource’s description of the GenCo structure is that the large-load customer pays for the resources required to serve it, and that existing customers are shielded from those incremental costs. In regulatory practice, such structures are common attempts to address the classic problem of cost allocation: when a new, very large user appears, who pays for the new poles, wires, transformers, and generation that would not have been built otherwise? The company’s statement is that Microsoft funds its portion. The protection is structural and intentional. It does not erase other drivers of bills—fuel prices, storm recovery, ordinary capital programs, inflation in labor and materials, or changes in the broader generation mix that apply to the whole system.

The savings projection has also been revised upward. Moving from $1.4 billion to approximately $1.75 billion over the life of the contracts is a material increase in the company’s expected total. Because it is a life-of-contract portfolio number, its translation into any single year’s bill depends on how the savings are allocated across customers, how many customers exist in each year, the timing of when the new load and new resources come online, and what else is happening to rates. An upper-bound figure of as much as $140 per customer annually is therefore a ceiling under a set of assumptions, not a floor that every household can count on. Households that use less electricity, or that fall into different rate classes, or that live in years when other cost pressures are larger, may see different outcomes even if the overall portfolio math holds.

The 600 MW battery contract adds another layer. Storage can shift energy from times of surplus to times of need, provide capacity value, and help manage the variable output of other resources. Because the batteries are contracted to serve multiple large-load customers, their costs and benefits are not attributable solely to the Microsoft campus. Duration—how many hours the batteries can discharge at full power—is not stated. Without duration and without a disclosed price, it is not possible from the announcement alone to calculate the energy-shifting capability or the unit cost of the storage. Those details, the company indicates, will come later.

The forward-looking risks listed in the release are ordinary for infrastructure of this scale, but they are still risks. Regulatory approvals can be delayed or conditioned. Construction can run long or over budget. Financing markets can change. Customer plans can be revised. Contracts can be terminated or capacity reduced under their terms. Each of those outcomes would alter the timing or the magnitude of both the new load and the projected savings. The announcement does not claim that every risk has already been eliminated; it identifies them as items that remain.

Lloyd Yates and Jeff Riles have spoken to the affordability objective. Their statements, as summarized in the company’s materials, frame the agreement as supporting both economic growth in Indiana and protection for existing customers. Those are the stated goals. They are not measurements of bills already reduced, because the service has not yet begun. They are also not projections of what every other cost category on a NIPSCO bill will do between now and 2048.

The broader tension the announcement sits inside is familiar. Data centers supporting AI training and inference have become among the largest new sources of electricity demand in several regions. Their power needs are not only large; they can also be relatively constant, which is different from many traditional industrial loads that vary with shifts or seasons. When a utility or a generation affiliate signs a long-term agreement that isolates the costs of serving that load, the theory is that the rest of the customer base does not subsidize the new infrastructure. Whether the theory survives contact with actual construction costs, actual utilization, actual fuel or purchased-power prices, and actual regulatory treatment is what the next several years of filings and rate cases will test.

A portfolio savings number measured in the low billions over decades is large enough to matter if it materializes. It is also small enough, when spread across hundreds of thousands of customers and twenty-plus years, that other factors can dominate any single monthly bill. An upper projection of $140 per year is roughly the cost of a few tanks of gasoline or a couple of months of streaming services; it is not, by itself, a transformation of household budgets. Its value as a signal is that the company expects the arrangement to produce a net credit to customers rather than a net charge. The size of that credit, its distribution, and its durability remain projections until the resources are built, the load arrives, and the accounting is reviewed.

The October 16 announcement does not resolve the central question. It describes a structure intended to let the data-center load pay for the resources it requires, and it updates an aggregate savings forecast. It does not deliver electricity in 2026, does not audit future bills, and does not eliminate other cost pressures. The 1,100 MW figure is planned capacity. The $1.75 billion figure is a life-of-contract total. The $140 figure is an upper annual projection. The batteries serve more than one customer. Additional numbers are promised on the earnings call. Regulatory, construction, and commercial risks are flagged.

What households can observe in the meantime is the difference between a signed agreement and energized infrastructure, between a capacity commitment and metered energy, and between a company projection and a line item on a bill. The GenCo design is an attempt to keep those two columns—large-load costs and existing-customer costs—from mixing. Whether the attempt succeeds at the scale of a multi-hundred-megawatt campus and a multi-decade contract is the practical test the announcement sets up but does not complete.

The story is therefore still early. Service is expected to begin in 2028. The portfolio is expected to grow by roughly 1,100 MW. Savings are expected to total approximately $1.75 billion over the life of the contracts. Protection for existing customers is the stated design of the GenCo structure. Each of those expectations carries the ordinary uncertainties of large energy infrastructure. None of them has yet been converted into a lower bill in a mailbox.

If the structure works as described, the data-center load will have funded its own generation and transmission, the batteries will have provided multi-customer flexibility, and the aggregate savings projection will have translated into some measurable credit for NIPSCO customers. If construction slips, if utilization differs from plan, if other system costs rise faster, or if regulatory treatment allocates costs differently than the company anticipates, the outcome for household bills will diverge from the upper-bound projection. The announcement itself does not claim otherwise.

That leaves the practical question the numbers raise but do not answer: once the campus is online and the first full year of operation has been recorded, will the separation between Microsoft’s funded resources and everyone else’s rates still look as clean as the October 16 structure describes, or will other pressures on the system have moved the bill anyway?

The earnings call will supply more of the missing operational and financial detail. Rate cases and construction updates in the years that follow will supply the rest. Until then, the deal remains a set of planned megawatts, a revised multi-year savings total, an upper annual projection, and a structural promise that the new load will pay its own way. Households will be able to test that promise against the numbers that eventually appear on their statements. The gap between the announcement and those statements is measured in years, in megawatts still to be built, and in the ordinary risks that accompany both.
