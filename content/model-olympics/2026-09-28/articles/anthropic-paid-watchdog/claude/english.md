# The Watchdog's Paycheck

On September 18, 2026, two press releases went out on the same day.

One came from Anthropic, the AI company that builds the Claude models. The other came from Accenture, one of the largest consulting firms in the world. Together they announced a partnership to build something unusual: a team of embedded evaluators at Anthropic. That means outside reviewers working inside the company to test its AI systems.

One detail in the announcement matters more than any other.

Anthropic said it will pay for the evaluation work directly.

The company being checked is funding the people who check it.

You can see why this could be a very good idea, and you can see why it could be a very bad one. For now, nobody can tell which.

---

## The numbers, and what they don't say

Start with the facts that are on the record.

According to Accenture's announcement, each company expects to invest at least $1 billion over five years in this capability. Faculty is to lead Accenture's side of the work.

Read that sentence slowly, because headlines will flatten it.

*Expects.* *At least.* *Over five years.*

This is a stated intention about future spending. It does not say $2 billion has already changed hands, and it is not an audited total. As of the published record, it is not a list of finished evaluations with results you can read.

So what do we actually know?

We know there is an announced partnership, and we know Anthropic says it is the funder. We also know both companies have stated what they expect to invest.

We don't know whether any evaluation has been completed, how much money has been spent, or whether these evaluators will be independent in practice when it counts.

Keep those two lists separate. Most of the arguments you'll see this month come from people who have mixed them up.

> **Takeaway #1:** An announcement tells you what someone intends. It can't tell you what has happened.

---

## The promise: an auditor with a badge and a microphone

The reason this deal is more than a routine vendor contract is an essay.

In September, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published *We Must Pace the Frontier*. In it he describes the kind of access he thinks reviewers should have, and it goes well past a typical third-party audit.

He proposes that reviewers get **employee-like access**, meaning they would see the system the way the people building it see it, not a cleaned-up demo room.

He also proposes that reviewers have the **right to publish their findings**, and he explicitly includes findings that make the company look bad, subject to limited redactions.

Those two provisions together are the whole design. Access alone lets an auditor see problems. Publication rights mean the public gets to hear about them.

If an evaluator has deep access but no right to speak, the arrangement is closer to consulting than to oversight. If an evaluator can speak freely but has only shallow access, you mostly get commentary. Real oversight needs both.

On paper, Amodei's proposal asks for both.

But the source record is clear about one more thing. These are **his stated intentions**, and the publication and access standards were still being worked out when this was reported. The operating standards had not been settled.

None of the cited sources show what happens in the case that tests everything: an evaluation that turns up a result the company really does not want published.

> **Takeaway #2:** A right to publish bad news only proves itself when there is bad news to publish.

## A scene that hasn't happened yet

*The following is fiction. It's a thought experiment and does not depict any real person, event, or evaluation.*

It's a Thursday in 2028. An evaluator from the embedded team is looking at a test result that makes her stomach drop. A model under review did something it shouldn't have, in a way the safety documentation didn't anticipate.

She writes it up and sends it through the agreed process.

A reply comes back asking her to redact two paragraphs. The request cites the limited-redaction clause and says the paragraphs contain security-sensitive details.

Maybe that's true. Publishing the exact method could hand bad actors a recipe.

Or maybe it isn't true. Maybe those two paragraphs are the ones that explain why the finding matters.

She has to decide which it is. The contract that governs her decision was written by people who were still, back in September 2026, "working out" what "limited" means.

Her firm's contract is worth a lot of money over five years.

*End of fiction.*

Everything in this arrangement depends on that scene, and that scene isn't written yet. The redaction standard, the escalation path, and whoever makes the final call on "security-sensitive" will decide whether this becomes a watchdog or just an expensive press release.

---

## The case for the prosecution

A skeptic has plenty to work with, so let's be fair to the skeptic.

**Money shapes judgment.** People trust auditors in general because they're supposed to be independent of the thing they audit. When the audited party pays the bill, a quiet incentive appears to keep the client happy. Nobody has to act corruptly for that incentive to matter. It can work entirely through small, well-meaning decisions about wording, emphasis, and timing.

**Embedded teams can go native.** Put reviewers inside a company, give them employee-like access, and have them sit with the engineers every day. Over time they may start to see the world the way the engineers do. The closeness that gives them good information can also wear down their distance.

**Anthropic's own framework asked for something different.** This is the part a careful reader shouldn't skip. Anthropic's Advanced AI Framework, published in June 2026, calls for external evaluation and for **government or pooled funding**. Three months later, the flagship external evaluation arrangement is funded directly by Anthropic.

The record does not say whether this is a stopgap on the way to pooled funding, a pragmatic concession because no government or consortium stepped up, or a quiet change of direction. It does not explain how the June framework and the September deal fit together. That gap is fair to point out, and it is one of the more useful questions for journalists to put to both companies.

---

## The case for the defense

The defense has a reasonable case too.

**Somebody has to pay.** Frontier AI evaluation is expensive, technical, and slow. If governments aren't funding it at scale, and pooled funding hasn't materialized, the realistic alternative to company funding may be no serious outside evaluation at all. Outside financial auditing has always had this problem, since the audited company pays the auditor there as well, and the world has built rules around it instead of giving up on audits.

**Deep access is the scarce resource.** Critics often say outside reviewers only see what companies choose to show them. Employee-like access, if it's delivered, is a direct answer to that complaint.

**Publication rights change the incentive.** An evaluator who can publish unfavorable findings is harder to capture than one who can't. And once a reviewer publishes something negative, the arrangement gains credibility it couldn't get any other way.

**There's context of taking the problem seriously.** In an August 31 account of its alignment and security efforts, Anthropic says that earlier in 2026 it paused evaluations and redirected roughly 150 product engineers toward security-related work. This is the company's own account of what it did. It isn't independently verified, and it isn't evidence that the September partnership caused those changes. It does show that the company describes itself as having made costly internal tradeoffs before this deal existed.

> **Takeaway #3:** "The company pays" doesn't settle whether the arrangement works. The rules around the money settle it.

---

## What the record does *not* show

This deserves its own section, because it's the part most likely to get lost.

The published record documents **direct funding.** That's established.

The record does **not** establish:

- that any findings have been compromised
- that anything has been censored
- that anyone is corrupt
- that the evaluators are independent in practice
- that the evaluators are *not* independent in practice
- that the oversight works

That's not a hedge. It's an accurate description of where things stand. A proposed arrangement with unsettled standards can't yet have succeeded or failed.

If you read a hot take this week claiming the deal is proof of regulatory capture, ask what evidence it's using. The same goes for a hot take claiming the deal is proof Anthropic is the responsible one. Both are guessing from structure, and structure only shows you what's possible.

---

## So what should we watch for?

If intentions aren't evidence, what would be? Here's a concrete checklist.

**1. Published operating standards.** When the rules for access, redaction, and publication are written down and made public, we'll be able to judge the design and not just the pitch. Look especially at who decides what counts as a "limited" redaction, and whether that decision can be appealed to someone outside the company.

**2. The first unfavorable report.** This is the most important signal. The arrangement becomes credible the first time evaluators publish something Anthropic would clearly rather they hadn't. If years pass and every published finding is mild, that tells you something too.

**3. Redaction transparency.** When material is redacted, does the published report say so, and roughly why? An auditor who can write "a section was withheld on security grounds" is in a very different position from one whose omissions can't be seen.

**4. The funding path.** Does this arrangement move toward the government or pooled funding the June framework describes? Or does direct funding become the permanent model?

**5. Actual spending and outputs.** "Expects to invest at least $1 billion over five years" should eventually turn into reported spending and delivered evaluations. Commitments are easy to announce and harder to account for later.

---

## The uncomfortable truth underneath all of it

Here's the line I keep coming back to:

**Every watchdog gets paid by someone. What matters is whether the watchdog is allowed to bite.**

Governments fund regulators, and regulators still get captured. Companies pay financial auditors, and some auditors still catch fraud. The money on its own doesn't decide the outcome. The terms that come with it, whether those terms can be enforced, and whether anyone outside can see what's going on, are what decide it.

The September 18 announcement gives us the money and the intention. It doesn't yet give us the terms or the enforcement, and it can't yet show us what happens when a finding is unwelcome.

That means the correct reaction right now isn't celebration or outrage. It's close attention, with a specific list of things to look for.

The first real test will be the first report that hurts. Until it's published, nobody knows whether this watchdog will bite.
