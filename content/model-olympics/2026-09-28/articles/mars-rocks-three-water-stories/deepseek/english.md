# Mars Wrote Three Water Stories in the Same Rocks. Perseverance Read Them All.

*What a rover found when it looked for a lake shoreline and found a volcano instead—and why that might matter more.*

## The Wrong Rocks

In September 2023, NASA's Perseverance rover rolled up to the inner edge of Jezero Crater and did what it has done since it landed in February 2021: it looked at the rocks and told us what they were made of.

The science team had a hypothesis. The Margin Unit—a ribbon of carbonate-rich terrain tracing the ancient shoreline of a lake that once filled Jezero Crater—should have been sedimentary rock. Layers of sand and silt piled up over millennia, the kind of soft, clay-rich stone that on Earth does a beautiful job of entombing microbial life. Orbital data had shown strong carbonate signals. Carbonates form in shallow water. Shallow water can support life. The logic was clean.

The rocks disagreed.

Instead of sedimentary layers, Perseverance found igneous rock—rock that formed from molten material, deep underground, cooling slowly enough for crystals to grow large. Not a lakebed. A magma chamber.

"Candice Bedford, a research scientist at Purdue University and the study's lead author, put it bluntly: 'If there is one thing I have learned after 10 years working with Mars rovers, it is that Mars constantly throws surprises at you'."

The findings, published September 21, 2026, in *Communications Earth & Environment*, describe not a simple shoreline but a geological crossroads—a place where at least three separate water systems passed through the same volume of rock at different times, each leaving its own chemical signature behind.

## What Perseverance Actually Sees

To understand how the rover reads this story, you have to understand its instrument. SuperCam sits on Perseverance's mast and fires a laser at rock targets from up to 21 feet (6.5 meters) away. The laser vaporizes a tiny amount of material, creating a plasma, and the spectrum of that plasma reveals the target's chemistry.

Across the Margin Unit, Perseverance used this method on more than 185 bedrock targets. It explored approximately 870 feet (265 meters) of elevation, from the high inner crater rim down to the lakebed. At the highest elevations, the rocks were coarse-grained, crystalline, rich in olivine—a magnesium-iron mineral—and showed almost no sign that water had ever touched them. The olivine unit formed in a body of magma deep underground and only reached the surface after the ground above it eroded away.

Lower down, closer to the ancient lakebed, the story changes. The olivine grains are fractured. Silica sits between them. Carbonate minerals appear in the fractures, left standing as ridges after the softer rock around them wore away.

Same unit. Same original rock. Three different watery fingerprints.

## Episode One: The Carbon Dioxide Flood

The first water to reach the Margin Unit's rocks was groundwater rich in carbon dioxide. It reacted with the olivine, and carbonate precipitated inside the bedrock fractures. This wasn't lake water splashing on a shore. This was fluid moving through rock underground, a chemical reaction that changed the stone from the inside out.

When the softer rock around those carbonate-filled fractures eventually eroded away, the fractures remained—ridges of carbonate running through the landscape like the veins of a leaf. Bedford's team describes these as "carbonate-rich ridges upon erosion". They're not sedimentary layers. They're chemical scars.

## Episode Two: The Lake (Or Something Like It)

The second episode is where the story gets less certain, and the scientists say so directly.

Some of the Margin Unit rocks contain silica. Silica is a byproduct of the reaction that turns olivine into carbonate. Eleni Ravanis, a planetary scientist at the University of Hawaii at Manoa and a coauthor of the study, explained: "Turning olivine into carbonate can leave silica behind, and we see more of that silica in rocks that sat below the water line".

Those rocks—the ones below the water line—tell us that at some point, the Jezero paleolake's waters reached them. Or, alternatively, that groundwater chemistry shifted in a way that remobilized carbonate and precipitated silica into the pore spaces. The paper is careful here: "exposure to the Jezero paleolake waters or changes in groundwater fluids". The evidence supports water. It doesn't yet prove which water.

What it does prove is sequence. The silica came after the carbonate. The second episode remobilized what the first episode had deposited. The rock's chemistry changed twice.

## Episode Three: The Hot Water

The third and most recent episode—again, "recent" in relative terms only—left the most dramatic calling card: mineral veins containing calcium sulfate and fluorite, found in the eastern part of the Margin Unit, about 10 inches (25 centimeters) thick.

Fluorite is the key. It typically forms when hot water circulates through volcanic rocks. Not lake water. Not shallow groundwater. Heated fluids, moving through fractures, depositing minerals that require elevated temperatures to precipitate.

This is a hydrothermal system—the kind of environment that, on Earth, can support microbial life. When water interacts with olivine, the reaction releases hydrogen, which some microbes use as food. It also leaves behind carbonate and silica, two minerals that can lock in traces of past biological activity.

The Margin Unit wasn't just wet. At least once, it was hot and wet.

## What the Rocks Don't Say

Here is where the story requires a deliberate pause, because the gap between what the evidence shows and what we might want it to mean is where science gets honest.

The team can establish the order of the three episodes. Carbonate first. Silica second. Fluorite-bearing veins third. But they cannot put dates on any of them. The paper says the absolute ages "remain unknown." The evidence supports an ordered history without supplying calendar dates or durations for each episode.

"At least three" is exactly that—a minimum inferred from the preserved evidence. The record does not establish precisely three lakes, three floods, or three planet-wide wet eras. It establishes that at least three distinct water-rock interactions left detectable mineral signatures in the same volume of rock. There could have been more. The older evidence may simply have been overwritten.

And the study reports neither a discovery of life nor present-day liquid water. The environments are of astrobiological interest—that is the precise, careful phrase. Interest. Not proof. Not even a claim of habitability, only that the conditions preserved in these rocks are the kind that, on Earth, can support microbial life.

The formation of the igneous rock and its subsequent alteration by water are separate chapters. The volcano came first. The water came later. The life—if there was any—came somewhere in between, or after, or not at all. The rocks don't say.

## The Crossroads

Bedford called the Margin Unit "a sort of crossroads for aqueous systems". The phrase is precise. A crossroads is not a destination. It is a place where different paths intersect, and the evidence of those intersections is what remains.

Jezero Crater sits inside one of the largest exposures of carbonate on Mars. What the rover learns here, Bedford said, "reaches well beyond this crater". The implications scale: if the Margin Unit's history—igneous rock, then groundwater carbonate, then lake or groundwater silica, then hydrothermal fluorite—is representative of the broader Nili Fossae region, then the story of water on early Mars is not a single wet period followed by a long dry decline. It is a sequence of distinct aqueous events, each with its own chemistry, its own temperature, its own duration.

That matters for how we search for life. A single lake provides one window of habitability. A crossroads provides several. The rocks at the Margin Unit preserve evidence of at least three windows, one of which was warm enough to precipitate fluorite. If life ever took hold in Jezero, it had multiple chances to do so—and multiple chemical environments to exploit.

## The Rocks Remember

There's a line in the NASA release that stayed with me: "Igneous rocks are excellent record-keepers". The phrase is almost poetic, but it's also technically true. Sedimentary rocks record deposition. Igneous rocks record crystallization—the moment minerals locked into place, preserving the conditions of their formation in their crystal structure. When water later moves through them, it leaves chemical traces in the fractures and pore spaces, and those traces persist.

The Margin Unit was never supposed to be a story about igneous rocks. It was supposed to be a shoreline. The rover went looking for layers of sand and silt and found instead a body of magma that cooled underground, eroded to the surface, and then spent an unknown span of time being chemically rewritten by at least three separate aqueous systems.

Mars, as Bedford said, constantly throws surprises. But the surprises are not random. They are recorded in the rocks, waiting for an instrument with a laser and a team with the patience to read them.

The three water stories are not the end of the Margin Unit's story. They are the first three chapters. The rocks are still there. The rover is still rolling. And the next surprise is somewhere up ahead, in a fracture or a vein or a layer of stone that looks ordinary until you fire a laser at it and read what it has to say.
