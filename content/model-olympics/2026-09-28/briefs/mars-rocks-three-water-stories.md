NASA and the research paper published their findings on September 21, 2026. Perseverance had reached the relevant area, the Margin Unit in Jezero Crater, in September 2023. The publication date is distinct from the rover's arrival at this site. Researchers expected sedimentary rocks near an ancient lake shoreline. Instead, the study interprets the unit as originally crystalline igneous rock, while retaining evidence of sedimentary reworking in some exposures. Higher-elevation rocks are consistent with magma cooling slowly underground. [c1–c2, c10; s1–s2]

The rocks preserve evidence of at least three separate episodes of water–rock interaction. In the first, groundwater rich in carbon dioxide reacted with the rock and carbonate precipitated in bedrock fractures. A second episode moved carbonate and deposited silica. The explanation for that second episode remains open: exposure to lake water or changes in groundwater could account for it. A later episode involved heated underground fluids depositing fluorite-bearing calcium-sulfate veins in fractures. [c3–c6; s1–s2]

The team can establish the relative sequence of these interactions, but their absolute ages remain unknown. The evidence therefore supports an ordered history without supplying calendar dates or durations for each episode. “At least three” is a minimum inferred from the preserved evidence; the record does not establish precisely three lakes, floods, or planet-wide wet eras. [c3, c7; s1–s2]

SuperCam measured more than 185 bedrock targets across the unit. Its laser can operate at distances up to 6.5 metres. The analysis concerns ancient mineral alteration observed by rover instruments. These environments are of astrobiological interest, but the study reports neither a discovery of life nor present-day liquid water. The formation of igneous rock and its subsequent alteration by water are separate parts of the reconstructed geological history. [c2, c8–c9; s1–s2]
