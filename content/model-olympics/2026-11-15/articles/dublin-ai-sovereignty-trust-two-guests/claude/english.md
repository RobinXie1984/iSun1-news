# In Dublin, Europe’s Leaders Opened Their AI Debate. The Real Test Arrives Later

On November 13, 2026, the heads of state or government of the European Union’s member countries met in Dublin for an informal meeting of the European Council. Among the items on that day’s agenda was a discussion of artificial intelligence, which the European Council, the body that brings these national leaders together, and its president, António Costa, described as the leaders’ first in-depth, strategic discussion of AI. That description covers this particular kind of conversation at the very top of European politics. Europe already has years of AI policy behind it, including the AI Act, an existing law that sets rules for how AI systems are built and used inside the EU. The new element in Dublin was the level of the conversation: AI became a subject of in-depth strategic discussion among presidents and prime ministers, who considered what kind of AI future they want for the continent.

To see why that matters, it helps to know who does what in Brussels. The European Council is the summit of national leaders. Its job is to set broad political direction, a little like a family deciding together where to go on holiday before anyone books tickets. The detailed work happens elsewhere. The European Commission is the EU’s executive arm, and it proposes laws. The Council of the EU, a separate body despite the similar name, is where national ministers meet; together with the European Parliament, it adopts those laws. An informal European Council meeting therefore produces direction and priorities. It places an issue on the shared agenda of the people with the most political power, and it signals what they expect the other institutions to work on next.

That is the backdrop for the question this story keeps returning to. Europe’s leaders have agreed that AI deserves their sustained attention. What would show, over the coming months and years, that this attention turns into AI tools people can actually use, and into protections people can actually rely on?

## Two strands in one discussion

The European Council’s published account of the meeting, and Costa’s remarks to reporters afterwards, describe an AI discussion with two main strands. The first concerns Europe’s ability to build and use AI: its competitiveness and what officials call sovereignty. The second concerns AI’s effects on society: safety, rights, work, democracy and public trust.

A third document shows what leaders were asked to think about before they arrived. The European Council prepared a four-page steering note, dated November 3, ten days before the meeting. A steering note is a set of background points and questions circulated in advance to help guide a discussion. It records the questions on the table going into the room. The answers leaders gave, and any shared conclusions, belong to the later accounts of the meeting itself.

Together, these three European Council documents describe a political discussion, the future instruments mentioned in it and its planned next steps. Their scope is that conversation; claims about what any measure later achieves call for their own evidence.

Two invited guests joined the discussion, according to the meeting account and the steering note. One was Arthur Mensch, cofounder and chief executive of Mistral AI, a Paris-based AI company founded in 2023. The other was Geoffrey Hinton, a British-Canadian computer scientist who shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on neural networks and who had earlier received the Turing Prize, often described as computing’s top award. The steering note says Hinton worked at Google until 2023.

A neural network, the technology behind much of Hinton’s recognition, is a kind of software loosely inspired by the brain. It contains a huge number of adjustable connections. During training, the software sees many examples, and those connections are nudged, bit by bit, until the system becomes good at spotting patterns or producing useful output, such as recognising a cat in a photo or writing a sentence. Neural networks are one important approach within the larger field of AI, and many researchers built them over decades.

The guests’ backgrounds hint at the breadth of the agenda. Mensch runs a European company that builds AI models and sells access to them. Hinton is a pioneering researcher in neural networks, and the meeting account places his participation in the strand on AI’s effects on society and safety. The records establish the guests’ presence and the subjects under discussion; their individual remarks lie beyond the scope of this account.

## What it takes to build and use AI

The first strand, on competitiveness, comes with a list of ambitions in the meeting account and in Costa’s remarks: mobilise public and private investment, develop AI capabilities and infrastructure, reduce critical dependencies on outside suppliers, secure the chains of companies and materials that AI relies on, obtain affordable clean energy, and speed up the use of AI by businesses and public administrations. The steering note names the layers involved: computing capacity, cloud services, models and chips.

Each of those words describes a different piece of the machine. Chips are the small electronic components that perform the calculations. Computing capacity means access to large numbers of those chips, housed in data centres, along with the power and cooling to keep them running. Cloud services let a company or government agency rent computing and software over the internet, much as a household pays for electricity from the grid. Models are the trained AI systems themselves, the neural networks that produce predictions, answers or text. Adoption is the step where an organisation actually weaves an AI tool into its daily work. It calls for suitable data and systems, staff who know how to put the tool to use, and a dependable way to run and check it.

**An analogy, offered purely as illustration:** imagine a town that wants to serve good meals to its residents. It can build a gleaming kitchen with the best ovens. Yet the meals depend on a steady supply of ingredients, on cooks who know how to use the equipment, on a reliable gas or electricity bill the town can afford, and on someone who checks the food before it reaches the table. In this picture, the ovens resemble computing capacity, the recipes resemble models, the ingredients and gas resemble chips and energy, the cooks resemble the staff who put tools to work, and the food inspector resembles oversight. A town with only a great kitchen has potential. A town with all the parts working together serves dinner. The analogy simply shows why the pieces depend on one another; the real AI supply chain has its own, far more tangled shape.

This is also the best way to read the word sovereignty in these documents. In the Dublin discussion, it refers to Europe’s ability to make its own choices about capability, supply and control in the places where depending on others carries real risk. The published materials state the goal as reducing critical dependencies and securing value chains. As an illustration, a European AI model could give a hospital or a ministry more choice about whom to buy from, even if that model ran on chips made elsewhere. Sovereignty in this sense is about having options and some leverage at the points that matter most.

Energy appears on the list for a practical reason. Training and running AI models uses electricity, and the amount varies widely with the size of the model and how it is deployed; some large systems draw a great deal. The summit records name affordable clean energy as a priority. Electricity is both a running cost and a condition of access for data centres: higher power prices raise operating costs, and in the kitchen picture, a town facing a steep gas bill might choose to cook less often. Affordable clean energy is one of the quieter conditions behind the louder headlines about models and chips.

## The money question

Investment runs through the whole first strand, and here the status of the record matters a great deal. Costa said Europe needs to make a greater effort on funding. He connected that effort to the EU’s next long-term budget, the multi-year spending plan the union agrees for itself, and to a proposed European Competitiveness Fund. The steering note asked leaders a related question: what role public demand might play.

Public demand means governments using their own buying power. When public bodies decide what tools they want for schools, hospitals, tax offices or courts, their purchases can shape what suppliers build and give young companies early customers. The steering note presents this as an open question for leaders to consider.

Costa discussed the next EU budget and the proposed Competitiveness Fund as future funding instruments, and the summit records document that discussion. Anyone reporting on this meeting can describe a direction of travel; the size and shape of any spending belong to later decisions and the records that accompany them.

## Living with AI: the second strand

The second strand turns from building AI to living with it. The meeting materials list a wide range of concerns: public trust, safety and security, fundamental rights, the protection of children, democratic processes, the labour market and Europe’s social model, the concentration of market power in a few companies, and cybersecurity. The steering note adds privacy, discrimination, AI-generated disinformation and foreign interference in politics, the reliability of essential services, and keeping humans in control of important decisions.

These are areas leaders identified for discussion. The records present them as risks to weigh and ask how Europe should respond. Claims about how often each harm occurs and how large it is rest on their own evidence, existing or future; these summit records set out the themes.

A **hypothetical** example, invented here for explanation, shows how abstract worries become concrete. Picture a city office that uses an AI system to help sort applications for housing support. Several questions follow at once. Does the system treat two applicants with similar circumstances in the same way, or does some hidden pattern in its training favour one group? Is applicants’ personal information kept safe? Can the staff member reviewing a file see why the system ranked it low, and override that ranking when it looks wrong? If an applicant is refused, can they ask for an explanation and a correction? Each of those questions maps onto an item on the leaders’ list: discrimination, privacy, human control, accountability.

The example also shows why trust works best as something you can observe. A policy document can declare that a system should be trustworthy. Trust becomes real when an affected person can actually find out what happened in their case, and someone is clearly responsible for fixing an error.

Market concentration connects the two strands. If a handful of firms control the most useful models and computing, organisations may have fewer real choices, even when the market appears crowded with products. More European suppliers could widen those choices. To count as a real gain, though, users would need to be able to switch providers, receive dependable service, and know who answers for mistakes.

## Oversight and the wider world

In his remarks after the meeting, Costa argued that clear oversight is a condition for public trust in AI. He pointed to the AI Act as the framework Europe already has, and he said the EU should take an active part in shaping global AI rules through the G7, the G20 and the United Nations. The G7 and G20 are regular gatherings of major economies, and the United Nations brings together nearly every country in the world. Costa also presented the EU as an early leader in AI governance; that is his political assessment.

The AI Act is an existing framework, and Costa cited it in his remarks. The meeting’s AI discussion concerned priorities and future meetings; the Act is the law already in place alongside that discussion. The Act’s current legal text and the progress of its implementation are matters to check in the law itself.

Costa’s link between oversight and trust is a reasonable starting idea, and it is also a claim that future evidence can test. A rule on paper defines who should be answerable. Trust grows when people experience that answerability in practice: when a wrongly refused applicant gets a correction, or when a public body can show how its systems were checked.

## The calendar ahead

The records give a timeline for following the story. Costa said leaders will return to AI at a meeting in Alden Biesen, in Belgium, on February 5, 2027. He also described a Social Summit planned for 2027, with AI’s effects on society and jobs as its central theme. That summit is set to mark the tenth anniversary of the European Pillar of Social Rights, a set of principles on fair work and social protection, and to include the social partners, the trade unions and employer organisations that negotiate over working conditions across Europe. As of mid-November 2026, both events sit on the calendar as plans.

Those dates matter for a simple reason. Strategic discussions tend to produce shared vocabulary first: competitiveness, sovereignty, trust, adoption. Later meetings show whether that vocabulary has picked up concrete content, such as a budget line, a programme, or a measurable change in how a service runs.

## What would count as proof

Return to the town and its kitchen. A meeting in which the council agrees that good meals matter is a useful start. The evidence that meals are being served comes later, in plain things people can check.

For Europe and AI, that evidence would come in a few forms. The first is funded capability: which computing facilities, models or research programmes actually received money, how much, and on what timetable, once the budget talks and the Competitiveness Fund move forward. The second is adoption: which companies, especially smaller ones, and which public administrations began using those tools in real work. The third is benefit: what task changed, for whom, and how the result compares with the old way of doing it, measured against a clear starting point. The fourth is oversight in action: what happened when a system made a consequential mistake, whether the person affected could challenge it, and whether someone took responsibility.

The two strands from Dublin meet in those tests. The services that reach people combine both: they are useful and affordable, people can get access to them, and someone answers for their results. Europe’s leaders have now put both concerns into one discussion, and Costa has announced a return to the subject in Alden Biesen on February 5. The question the discussion leaves is whether a shared strategic conversation can become machines that run, tools that work in real offices and clinics, and protections that ordinary people can use when it counts.
