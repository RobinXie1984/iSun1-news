The Moon With No Birth Certificate

Japan’s MMX mission is set to launch for Phobos, and NASA has planned for about 15% of whatever comes back. Here’s what that means and what it doesn’t.

Mars has two moons, and neither one fits the usual pattern.

They aren’t round and stately like our Moon. They’re small, lumpy, and close to their planet. For decades scientists have argued over a simple question: where did they come from?

Japan’s space agency wants to settle that argument by going to one of these moons, scooping up some of its surface, and bringing it back to Earth.

If the schedule holds, that journey starts tonight.

The Countdown

According to a NASA report published October 15, 2026 (page updated October 16), JAXA’s Martian Moons eXploration mission, MMX, is targeted to launch from the Tanegashima Space Center in Japan at:

3:41 p.m. EDT, Monday, October 19
4:41 a.m. JST, Tuesday, October 20
3:41 a.m. HKT, Tuesday, October 20

One caveat matters more than any other in this story: this is a target, not a result. At the time of writing, the launch has not happened. Launch windows slip. Weather turns. Hardware sometimes refuses to cooperate. Everything below describes a plan, not an achievement.

There is no sample yet and no discovery yet. There is a spacecraft, a schedule, and a question that has gone unanswered for a very long time.

The most honest sentence in space exploration is “no earlier than.”

Two Moons, Two Competing Origin Stories

MMX is designed to study both Martian moons, Phobos and Deimos, and to return material from Phobos to Earth.

Why go to all that trouble for two odd-shaped rocks? Because nobody agrees on how they formed, and the competing explanations lead to very different pictures of the early solar system.

The NASA report outlines two main hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: They’re captured asteroids.
On this view, Phobos and Deimos are ancient asteroids that wandered too close to Mars and were pulled into orbit.

Hypothesis 2: They’re debris from a giant impact.
On this view, something large struck Mars long ago, threw material into orbit, and that material eventually came together to form the moons.

According to NASA, samples and close-up measurements may help tell these possibilities apart. “May” is doing real work in that sentence. The origin question is not settled, and nothing in NASA’s report claims otherwise. The report also makes no claim about life, past or present. This mission is about origins, not biology.

My read: the two hypotheses lead to two different stories.

If the moons are captured asteroids, Phobos is effectively a visitor that got caught, and a sample would tell us about material from elsewhere in the solar system.
If the moons are impact debris, Phobos is effectively a piece of Mars itself, and a sample from it could be a way of studying Martian material without landing on Mars and launching back off it.

Either result would teach us something. That’s one of the strongest arguments for a mission like this: the question is worth asking whichever way the answer goes.

Sometimes you don’t need the right answer to justify the trip. You need a question where both answers are worth having.

What NASA Is Bringing

MMX is a JAXA mission. Japan leads it. NASA is a partner, and according to the report its contributions are specific.

1. MEGANE: reading what the surface is made of

NASA supplies MEGANE, a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer. Its job is to determine the elemental composition of the Martian moons.

The useful thing about this kind of instrument is that it measures composition from a distance, before anything is collected. If the sample is the close-up reading, MEGANE provides the wider context around it.

2. A sampler that uses a burst of nitrogen

NASA also provides a pneumatic surface sampler that uses a burst of nitrogen gas to collect material. According to the report, it complements JAXA’s own coring sampler, which is designed to reach deeper.

So the plan includes two collection methods:

JAXA’s coring sampler goes deeper.
NASA’s pneumatic sampler gathers surface material.

My read: carrying two different methods to the same place looks like good engineering. Surface material and subsurface material can carry different histories, since surfaces get exposed and altered over time. Having both gives scientists more to compare and reduces the risk that one method fails to collect anything.

3. The Deep Space Network

NASA will also provide Deep Space Network support, the communications infrastructure used to track and talk to distant spacecraft. It gets less attention than the instruments, but a spacecraft near Mars needs a dependable link home.

Instruments get the headlines. Communications keep the mission alive.

The 15%: What It Is and What It Isn’t

This is the detail most likely to be misread.

NASA says it will obtain and curate about 15% of the total returned sample at Johnson Space Center in Houston. The report presents this as part of a sample-curation partnership, which NASA science chief Nicky Fox describes.

Here’s what that 15% is not:

❌ It is not 15% of Phobos.
❌ It is not NASA ownership of a moon or any part of Mars.
❌ It is not a delivered sample. Nothing has been collected yet.
❌ It is not a known quantity. The report does not give the total sample mass, so 15% of an unknown amount is also unknown.

Here’s what it is: a planned share of whatever material MMX brings back, if it brings any back, to be held and curated at Johnson Space Center.

The NASA report also does not establish:

the total mission cost,
the total returned sample mass, or
any commercial payoff.

If you see a post claiming “NASA gets 15% of a Mars moon” or putting a dollar value on these samples, that claim goes beyond what the source says.

My read: the 15% figure is ordinary in the best sense. Partners contribute instruments, a sampler, and communications support, and in return they get a share of the scientific material. It’s how international science missions commonly divide the work and the results. It isn’t a land grab, and it has nothing to do with mining. It’s closer to a library-sharing arrangement.

The real return on this mission isn’t measured in grams. It’s measured in arguments that finally get settled.

The Timeline: A Long Wait

Here is NASA’s stated schedule. Every date below is a forward-looking plan:

Milestone	Planned timing
Launch from Tanegashima	Targeted October 19, 2026 (EDT) / October 20 (JST/HKT)
Arrival at Mars	August 2027
Phobos sampling	No earlier than December 2028
Sample delivery to Earth	2031

Do the arithmetic. If everything goes to plan, the sample arrives roughly five years after launch.

A child starting first grade this autumn would be in their final year of elementary school by the time Phobos material reaches a lab. And that’s the planned schedule. The phrase “no earlier than” on the sampling date is a reminder that these milestones can move.

My read: this is what serious planetary science looks like. Results take years and the payoff is delayed. In a culture that expects immediate results, it’s notable when two space agencies agree on a plan whose main return won’t arrive until the 2030s.

Some questions take five years to answer. Some of the people asking them have been waiting much longer than that.

Why This Matters Beyond Mars

What follows is analysis, not source fact.

1. Phobos could be a cheaper route to understanding Mars.
If the impact-debris hypothesis is correct, Phobos material could carry information about early Mars. Collecting from a small moon with very weak gravity is a different engineering problem than landing on Mars and launching back off it. That depends on which hypothesis wins, which is the point of going.

2. It shows how space science is shared.
This isn’t one country acting alone. Japan leads, NASA contributes hardware and support, and the scientific material is divided through an agreed partnership. Whatever you think of space policy, that’s a working model for expensive, high-risk science.

3. Samples last longer than any single study.
A curated sample at Johnson Space Center isn’t analyzed once and discarded. Curation means future scientists, using instruments that don’t exist yet, can come back to it and ask questions nobody has thought of. Part of the 15% is being saved for researchers who may not have been born yet.

A returned sample is a message to future scientists: we went there so you could keep asking.

What Could Go Wrong

Since the source is careful about uncertainty, this article should be too.

The launch could slip. At the time of writing, it is a target.
The cruise could fail. Arrival in August 2027 is planned, not guaranteed.
Sampling could be harder than expected. Small bodies with weak gravity are difficult to operate on. The “no earlier than December 2028” wording builds in that caution.
The return could be delayed. 2031 is the plan.
The samples might not settle the question. NASA says they may discriminate between origin hypotheses. That’s a reasonable hope, not a promise.

My read: none of this is a reason for pessimism. It’s a reason for patience. Exploration missions are worth following because the outcome isn’t known in advance.
