Suspenseful: The Brand That Opened the Door—and Quietly Closed the Trust Gap Behind It
Emotional Resonance: When a Familiar Name Draws You Closer to Public Services, but Leaves the Government Further Away
Counter-intuitive: Partnering with a Trusted Company Increased Interest in a Government Portal—and Lowered Trust in the Government

In places where citizens already view public institutions with skepticism, a digital service portal is rarely just a website. It is a test of whether the state can still earn everyday engagement. Kenya’s MyCountyHub, a county-level portal offering permits, parking services, emergency requests, and information, became the setting for exactly that kind of test. Researchers at MIT’s Governance Lab, working with Busara, examined what happens when a government platform is presented with, without, or alongside the name of a widely recognized private partner—Safaricom. The findings, reported by MIT on October 15, 2026, and drawn from a study published in the September issue of Government Information Quarterly, challenge a common assumption: that a trusted brand will automatically lend its credibility to the public institution standing next to it.

The experiment reached more than 11,000 online survey respondents in and around Nairobi. Participants were shown information about MyCountyHub under different attribution conditions: the government alone, Safaricom alone, or both together. Trust was measured before and after exposure. The outcomes of interest were stated willingness to use the portal and interest in learning more—not observed long-term behavior, tax compliance, or voting. Exact effect sizes are not detailed in the MIT account, and the underlying journal paper was not retrieved for this summary; the reported patterns should be read as the institutional account of the work by authors Lily L. Tsai and Lula Chen, among others.

What emerged was not a simple story of borrowed trust. Highlighting Safaricom alone increased respondents’ willingness to use or learn about the portal relative to the government-alone condition. At the same time, that same company-only attribution was associated with lower subsequent trust in government compared with the government-alone version. Presenting both the government and Safaricom together did not, on average, raise government trust above the level achieved by attributing the portal to the government alone. In short, the private brand could pull people toward the service without handing measurable legitimacy back to the public institution.

This pattern matters because baseline trust shaped the response. Among respondents who already trusted both the government and Safaricom, partnership messaging increased trust. The reduction in government trust, by contrast, was driven primarily by those who trusted Safaricom but distrusted the government. For that group, seeing the familiar company name attached to a public service appears to have sharpened rather than softened their existing skepticism toward the state. The MIT account notes that general information about the portal was associated with increased trust overall, suggesting that simply learning more about the service can still move attitudes. Yet the specific framing of who “owns” or sponsors the platform altered the direction of that movement.

The study also references a separate 2023 Kenya survey in which 43 percent of respondents expressed trust in government. That figure is not a 2026 national measurement from this research, and the authors caution against generalizing the MyCountyHub results to every citizen, every country, or every platform. The design captures stated intentions in a survey setting around one set of county services in and near Nairobi. It does not track whether people later completed a permit application, paid a parking fee through the portal, or altered their broader political behavior. Those limits are explicit in the available account.

Still, the distinction between attracting users and transferring trust is sharp enough to reframe how public-private digital partnerships are discussed. A company logo that residents already encounter daily can lower the perceived cost of trying a government service. It can signal that the interface will be familiar, that support might be reachable, or that the technical layer is competent. None of those signals necessarily revise a person’s prior view of the government’s motives, competence, or fairness. In the company-only condition, the very familiarity that increased willingness appears to have made the subsequent drop in government trust more visible among those already inclined to doubt the state. The partnership, in that framing, did not function as a bridge so much as a contrast.

For governments facing low baseline trust, the practical implication is narrower than the slogan “partner with a trusted brand.” The brand can enlarge the pool of people willing to click, inquire, or experiment. It does not, on the evidence here, reliably enlarge the pool of people who come away more trusting of the government that delivers the underlying service. When both names appear together, the average result was no net gain in government trust over attributing the portal solely to the government. The benefit of the partnership, under these conditions, was concentrated among people who already held favorable views of both parties. For everyone else, the joint branding neither repaired distrust nor clearly outperformed the government speaking for itself.

That heterogeneity is the study’s most useful caution. Messaging that works for one segment can reinforce distance for another. A resident who already sees Safaricom as reliable and the county government as unreliable may interpret the company’s involvement as evidence that the public service only becomes usable once a private actor is attached—an interpretation that leaves the government’s own standing unchanged or slightly worse. A resident who trusts both may read the same message as confirmation that capable institutions are cooperating. The same portal information, presented with different labels, therefore produces different trust updates depending on the priors people bring to the page.

The broader information effect remains relevant. Simply receiving details about what the portal offers was linked to higher trust overall. Clarity about available services, eligibility, or processes can still move attitudes even when institutional trust is fragile. The attribution experiment isolates an additional layer: who is credited for making the service available can change whether that clarity translates into greater confidence in the government or merely greater willingness to use a channel that feels privately supported. Willingness and trust are not the same variable, and the study shows they can move in opposite directions under certain framings.

None of this establishes that private partnerships are counterproductive in every setting. The research is specific to one portal, one company, one urban and peri-urban sample, and one moment of survey measurement. It does not speak to whether repeated, successful use of the actual service would later revise trust, nor to whether different companies, different service types, or different national contexts would produce the same split between interest and institutional trust. It also does not measure downstream outcomes such as compliance or political participation. Those absences are part of the record.

What the MIT account does establish is a measurable separation. A trusted commercial name can increase stated interest in a government digital service. That increase does not require, and under the company-only condition did not produce, a corresponding increase in trust toward the government. Joint credit did not outperform government-only credit on the trust measure for the sample as a whole. The trust reduction that appeared was concentrated among respondents who already distinguished sharply between the company they trusted and the government they did not. For digital platforms intended both to deliver services and to rebuild institutional confidence, the experiment suggests these two goals are not automatically aligned by the presence of a familiar private logo.

The practical question left open is how governments and their partners choose to frame the relationship when baseline trust is uneven. Attributing a portal solely to a company may maximize initial curiosity among some users while leaving, or even widening, the trust gap for others. Attributing it solely to the government may forgo some of that curiosity but avoid the contrast effect. Presenting both may reassure those already positively disposed without converting the skeptical. Each choice carries a different distribution of willingness and trust, and the study supplies evidence that the distribution is not uniform.

In the end, MyCountyHub’s test under controlled attribution conditions illustrates a limit that is easy to overlook when private brands are invited into public service delivery. Familiarity can lower the threshold for trying a new channel. It does not, by itself, rewrite the reasons a person already withholds trust from the institution behind that channel. The brand can attract the click. On the evidence reported by MIT, it does not reliably convert that click into greater confidence that the government has earned it.

If a government service you use regularly began carrying a well-known private company’s name alongside its own, would that make you more inclined to try new features, less inclined to credit the government for improvements, or neither?
