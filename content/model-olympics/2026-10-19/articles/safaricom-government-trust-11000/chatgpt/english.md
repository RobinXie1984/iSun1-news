Three headline options

Suspenseful: A Trusted Brand Got People Interested in a Government Portal. Then the Trust Scores Told a Different Story.
Emotional Resonance: You Can Trust the Service—and Still Distrust the Government Behind It
Counter-intuitive: Borrowing a Trusted Brand May Win Users—and Cost Government Trust
The Brand Got Them Interested. The Government Didn’t Get the Credit.

A government needs people to use its digital services. A familiar company has something the government cannot simply build into an app: public trust.

Put the two together, and the logic seems irresistible.

The company’s reputation reassures people. More people become interested in the service. Government gets some credit for making life easier. A commercial partnership becomes a bridge to public confidence.

Except the bridge may carry traffic in only one direction.

According to an MIT Governance Lab account published on October 15, 2026, research involving more than 11,000 online survey respondents in and around Nairobi found that emphasizing Safaricom’s connection to a county-service portal increased people’s stated willingness to use it and learn more about it.

But that did not mean the government benefited reputationally.

When Safaricom alone received the attribution, subsequent trust in government was lower than under government-only attribution. Giving both institutions credit did not, overall, lift government trust above the government-only condition.

The distinction is small enough to disappear inside a celebratory announcement—and large enough to change what a successful partnership means.

A brand can help people approach a public service without helping them believe in the public institution behind it.

That is the tension at the center of this research. And it raises a question that extends well beyond a website: when government improves the way it reaches people, who gets to own the improvement?

One Portal, More Than One Story

The research, conducted by MIT Governance Lab and Busara, examined Kenya’s MyCountyHub county-service portal and its partnership with Safaricom. The services described included permits, parking, emergency requests and information.

These are practical points of contact between citizens and government. They concern what people need to obtain, report, request or understand.

A portal brings such functions into a digital interface. But an interface also presents a story about who is responsible.

Is this government making its services more accessible?

Is this a company providing something useful?

Or is this a partnership in which both deserve recognition?

The researchers varied those attributions. Respondents received information crediting government alone, the company alone, or both. Trust was measured before and after the information, alongside outcomes including stated willingness to use the portal and interest in learning about it.

That design matters because the study was examining how people responded to information about a service and its institutional associations.

It was not tracking years of actual use. It was not demonstrating that a rollout had succeeded. It was not reporting a new election result or proving that anyone changed their vote.

The study appeared in the September issue of Government Information Quarterly, according to MIT’s account. Its authors include Lily L. Tsai and Lula Chen.

The findings discussed here come from that institutional account; the underlying journal paper was not retrieved for this article.

Within those boundaries, the result is striking: the attribution that made the service more appealing did not necessarily make government more trusted.

The Seductive Arithmetic of Borrowed Trust

There is an appealing formula behind many institutional partnerships:

A trusted organization joins a less trusted organization. The stronger reputation reassures the audience. Confidence spreads.

It is easy to see why this idea attracts attention. Institutions can spend years trying to establish credibility. A partnership appears to offer a faster route.

But trust is not a liquid that automatically flows between connected containers.

People can make separate judgments about separate institutions. They can welcome a company’s involvement while remaining skeptical of government. They can find a service promising without revising their beliefs about the public body associated with it.

The MyCountyHub findings are consistent with precisely that separation.

According to MIT, emphasizing Safaricom alone increased willingness to use the portal and learn more about it. Yet government trust afterward was lower relative to government-only attribution.

Both outcomes can exist at once.

The service becomes more attractive. The government receives less confidence than it would under another presentation of the same initiative.

That does not establish that the partnership itself damaged government trust in everyday life. The comparison concerns the survey’s attribution conditions. Nor does it establish a universal penalty for involving private companies.

It does show why interest in a service cannot serve as a substitute for measuring trust in its sponsor.

Getting someone through the digital front door is one achievement. Changing whom they trust is another.

The Audience Brought Its Own History

The most revealing part of MIT’s account concerns what respondents believed before encountering the portal information.

Baseline trust mattered.

Among people who initially trusted both institutions, partnership messaging increased trust. For that group, the partnership could reinforce an already favorable view of the organizations involved.

The reduction in government trust, meanwhile, was driven by respondents who trusted Safaricom but distrusted government.

This is more specific—and more useful—than saying that corporate partnerships help or hurt public trust.

The same institutional pairing did not mean the same thing to everyone.

For someone already confident in both parties, a partnership may fit an existing belief that capable institutions can work together.

For someone who trusts the company but distrusts government, the company’s involvement may make the portal worth considering without making government itself more credible.

Those are interpretations of the reported pattern, not quotations from participants or proof of their private reasoning. The supplied account does not establish exactly what each respondent thought.

Still, the implication for communication is substantial.

Officials do not deliver messages into empty space. They deliver them into accumulated judgments.

A new logo arrangement does not erase what an audience already believes.

This also explains why national averages can be misleading. An overall result may conceal groups moving in different directions. A message that reassures one audience can leave another unconvinced—or compare unfavorably with a different message.

The relevant question is therefore not simply whether people trust a partnership.

It is: what did they trust before the partnership was introduced?

Why Giving Everyone Credit Wasn’t Enough

There is an obvious response to the attribution problem: put both names on the service.

Let the company supply reassurance. Let the government remain visibly involved. Share the credit and capture both benefits.

But according to MIT’s account, giving both institutions credit did not overall raise government trust above government-only attribution.

That finding does not make joint attribution pointless. It does not establish that every subgroup reacted identically. It does not show that the same pattern would persist after sustained experience with a reliable service.

It does challenge the assumption that two institutional names necessarily produce more public confidence than one.

Visibility and credibility are different things.

A government can be visibly present in a partnership without receiving a reputational bonus from its partner. People may recognize the relationship and still reserve their favorable judgment for one participant.

The broader analytical lesson is that credit is not allocated by graphic design alone.

Institutions can decide whose name appears on a page. They cannot simply dictate what that name means to the person reading it.

The More Hopeful Finding Hiding Beside the Headline

The research was not an account of digital government’s inevitable failure to earn confidence.

MIT also reported that general information about the portal was associated with increased trust overall.

That deserves attention.

The attribution findings suggest limits to borrowed reputation. The broader information finding suggests that explaining a public service may itself be valuable.

Those observations can coexist. Learning about a useful government-related initiative may improve trust overall, while different presentations of institutional responsibility produce different comparative outcomes.

The distinction prevents an overly dramatic reading: that mentioning a company always harms government, or that digital services cannot improve public perceptions.

Neither conclusion is supported here.

A more defensible interpretation is that information and attribution perform different jobs. One tells people what is available. The other helps shape whom they associate with it.

For policymakers, this points toward a practical question: how clearly does communication explain the service itself?

What can someone do through the portal? What problem is it intended to address? Which institution is responsible for which function?

The study does not prove that a particular explanatory strategy will produce lasting trust. But its reported results offer a reason to examine substantive information alongside branding.

A partnership announcement tells people who joined forces. A service explanation tells them why they should care.

The Success Dashboard Could Tell the Wrong Story

Imagine a hypothetical evaluation dashboard—not an observed outcome from this study.

Interest rises. More respondents say they would use the service. More want to learn about it.

The communication team has grounds to report progress.

Now imagine that the project also has an unstated objective: improving confidence in government.

The first set of measures cannot establish the second.

This is where a seemingly technical distinction becomes a management problem. If a project is judged only by interest, its leaders may infer a reputational achievement they have not measured.

The MyCountyHub findings suggest that at least three outcomes should remain separate:

Interest: Do people want to learn more or say they would use the service?
Use: Do they actually adopt it, complete tasks and return?
Institutional trust: Does their confidence in government change?

The reported survey speaks to stated willingness, interest and measured trust responses. It does not establish sustained adoption.

Even actual adoption, if later demonstrated, would not automatically prove improved institutional trust. People may use a service because it is convenient, necessary or better than the alternatives.

That is an analytical possibility, not a finding about these respondents.

The point is methodological as much as political: measure the outcome you claim.

A full queue is not a confidence vote. Neither is a busy portal.

What This Study Cannot Tell Us

The limits are essential to the story.

The respondents were recruited online in and around Nairobi. Their responses should not be generalized to every Kenyan, every country or every digital platform.

The supplied material does not provide exact effect sizes. It therefore cannot support claims that government trust collapsed, that willingness surged by a particular percentage, or that one messaging strategy overwhelmingly outperformed another.

It also does not provide evidence of changes in tax compliance, voting, long-term political attitudes or actual sustained use.

MIT’s article cites 43% government trust in a separate 2023 Kenya survey. That figure is historical context. It is not a measured 2026 national trust level, and it should not be presented as one.

Nor do the results reveal Safaricom’s motives. A finding about public responses is not evidence that the company sought to capture political credit or weaken government.

These boundaries do not drain the research of significance. They locate its significance correctly.

The evidence concerns a large online survey, different institutional attributions and differing responses—not the final political consequences of digital public-private partnerships.

A narrow finding can expose a big assumption without proving a grand theory.

What Governments Should Ask Next

The broader implications are best treated as questions for testing.

First, does willingness become actual use?

A promising survey response is an opening. The next evidence would involve whether people successfully complete tasks, return when needed and find the service useful over time.

Second, does experience change the attribution effect?

Someone’s reaction to a description may differ from their reaction after repeated successful interactions—or after a failed request. The supplied research does not settle that question.

Third, do people understand institutional responsibility?

A partnership may be easier to evaluate when users know who operates the interface, who delivers the underlying service and who must resolve a problem. Whether that clarity improves trust would require further evidence.

Finally, do different audiences continue to respond differently?

Because baseline trust mattered here, evaluations should examine those differences rather than rely exclusively on an average.

These are not cosmetic refinements. They determine whether a partnership is solving an access problem, a service problem, a confidence problem—or some combination.

Those goals can support one another. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

The Difference Between Being Useful and Being Believed

The larger story is about the limits of association.

A trusted company can make a government-linked service seem more approachable. That is valuable. If the interest eventually translates into successful use, it could help people obtain services they need.

But the government’s reputational benefit remains a separate question.

According to MIT’s October 15 account, the MyCountyHub research found precisely that separation: highlighting Safaricom attracted stated interest and willingness, while government trust compared less favorably with government-only attribution. Joint credit did not provide an overall trust advantage over government alone. Existing beliefs helped determine the response.

This is not an argument against partnerships.

It is an argument for knowing what a partnership has actually achieved.

A familiar name may persuade people to give a service a chance. Whether government earns lasting confidence depends on evidence this survey cannot yet provide.

Borrowed credibility may open the door. What happens afterward still has to earn its own reputation.
