The Logo Problem: What a Kenyan Government Portal Reveals About Borrowed Trust
Three Headline Options

Suspenseful:
Researchers Changed One Detail About a Government App for 11,000 People. It Shifted Who Got the Trust.

Emotional Resonance:
“I’ll Use It. I Just Won’t Trust You More”: What a Kenyan Study Says About Citizens and the State

Counter-intuitive:
The Famous Brand That Made a Government Portal More Appealing May Have Left the Government Looking Worse

The Article

Most of us don’t read the small print about who built the apps we use.

We see a logo, a colour scheme, a name we recognise, and in half a second we decide whether to tap. That half-second is now a governance question.

A study from MIT’s Governance Lab, described in an MIT News report published on October 15, 2026, put that half-second under a microscope. The researchers wanted to know what happens when a government launches a digital service with a well-known private company beside it, and you change only one detail: who gets the credit.

What they found doesn’t fit the usual story about public-private partnerships.

A trusted brand can bring people to the door. It doesn’t automatically send its trust through to the government behind it.

What was actually studied

This section sticks to what MIT’s account reports.

The setting was Kenya. The platform was MyCountyHub, a county-service portal developed in partnership with Safaricom, one of the most widely recognised companies in the country. MIT’s account says the portal offers practical, everyday services: permits, parking, emergency requests, and information.

The research was carried out by MIT Governance Lab together with Busara, and the authors include Lily L. Tsai and Lula Chen. According to MIT, the study was published in September 2026 in Government Information Quarterly.

One caveat up front, because it matters for everything that follows. This article is based on MIT’s institutional account of the research, not on the underlying journal paper, which was not retrieved for this piece. The findings below are MIT’s description of them. Exact effect sizes are not given here, and you won’t find any invented ones either.

The design was simple:

More than 11,000 online survey respondents in and around Nairobi took part.
Each person got information about the portal attributed in one of three ways: the government alone, the company alone, or both together.
Trust was measured before and after respondents saw the information.
The outcomes were stated willingness to use the portal and interest in learning about it.

That last point carries a lot of weight, so it’s worth stating plainly: this study measured what people said they would do, not what they did. It did not track actual long-term use of MyCountyHub. It does not report effects on tax compliance, voting, or any long-run political outcome. It is not a report of a successful platform rollout, and it is not an election result.

The study is a controlled look at one moment of perception. Within that limit, it is revealing.

Finding one: the brand works as a magnet

Source facts, per MIT’s account.

When information about the portal highlighted Safaricom alone, respondents reported greater willingness to use the portal and to learn more about it.

On its own, that is the result any marketing team would want. Put the familiar name at the front and more people lean in.

If adoption were the only goal, the analysis could stop here, and plenty of real-world launches implicitly do stop here.

Finding two: the trust bill arrives

Source facts, per MIT’s account.

The same Safaricom-only framing that raised interest was followed by lower government trust compared with the framing that credited the government alone.

So the version that drew more interest in the service also left the government in a weaker position on trust than simply putting the government’s own name on it.

The app gained. The state behind the app did not.

Finding three: sharing credit is no shortcut

Source facts, per MIT’s account.

The obvious fix is to credit both: put the company and the government side by side and let the company’s reputation rub off.

According to MIT’s account, that did not work overall. Giving both institutions credit did not raise government trust above the government-alone framing.

Co-branding did not deliver the boost it seemed to promise.

Finding four: information itself helped

Source facts, per MIT’s account.

Before this turns into a gloomy story, there is a hopeful result in the data.

General information about the portal was associated with increased trust overall.

Put plainly, telling people what the service does (permits, parking, emergency requests, information) was associated with more trust. That suggests showing what a service does may matter at least as much as whose logo sits on it.

Competence, explained clearly, can do work that branding can’t.

The twist is in who was listening

Source facts, per MIT’s account.

This is the part that keeps the study from being a simple warning against partnerships.

People’s trust before the study shaped how they reacted.

Among respondents who already trusted both the government and Safaricom, partnership messaging increased trust.
The drop in government trust was driven by respondents who trusted Safaricom but distrusted the government.

So the effect was not uniform. The same message landed differently depending on what each person already believed.

MIT’s account adds a clear limit: these findings should not be generalised to every citizen, every country, or every platform. This was a particular population, in and around Nairobi, responding to a particular portal and a particular company.

What I think this means

From here, this is my analysis, not the study’s claims.

I find it helpful to think of trust as a ledger rather than a mood.

When a government stands next to a trusted brand, a casual observer might expect trust to flow from the more trusted party to the less trusted one, like heat moving to a colder object. The MIT account suggests something closer to a credit-assignment problem.

Here is one plausible reading, which I can’t confirm from the source because the motives of respondents were not reported: if you already distrust the government but like the company, a message that foregrounds the company may reinforce a story you already hold, namely that the competent part of this is the company’s doing. The portal looks good, and the reason it looks good belongs to someone other than the state.

If that reading holds, it explains why a brand can pull people in while doing nothing for the government’s standing. The halo stays attached to whoever wears it.

It also explains why partnership messaging helped people who trusted both. For them there was no gap to close and no story to confirm. Two institutions they already trusted were working together, and that combination looked good.

A concrete example from outside the study shows the pattern. Picture a city that partners with a popular ride-hailing company to run a new transit card. Riders who like the company sign up quickly. Ask them later who made their commute easier and many will name the company. The city gets the usage numbers but not the credit. To be clear, this is an illustration of the logic, not something the MIT study tested.

Three uncomfortable lessons for anyone building public services

Analysis.

1. Adoption and legitimacy are different metrics, and they can move apart.

A dashboard that counts sign-ups will reward putting the strongest logo up front. A dashboard that tracks trust in government might discourage it. If you measure only one, you will optimise for the wrong thing without noticing. The MIT account suggests both can be true at once: more interest in the tool, weaker trust in the government.

2. The most valuable audience is the hardest one.

The decline in government trust came mainly from people who trusted the company but not the government. That is arguably the group a public service most needs to reach: people who are engaged and digitally comfortable but sceptical of the state. These are the citizens a well-run portal could, in principle, win over. A message that leans on borrowed credibility may push them further away instead.

3. Explaining what a service does may matter more than whose name is on it.

The result I’d underline is the one that drew the least attention: information about what the portal does was associated with higher trust overall. You can’t borrow a reputation for competence. You may be able to earn some of it by clearly showing a service that works.

Lending your brand to a cause is different from lending it your credibility.

What we still don’t know

Analysis of the evidence’s limits.

Good research is honest about its limits, and so should anyone writing about it.

Stated intent isn’t behaviour. Saying “I’d be willing to use it” is cheap. Whether those people actually renewed a parking permit through MyCountyHub months later is not covered here.
No effect sizes in this account. We know the direction of the effects, not their size. A small shift and a large one call for very different policy responses.
One portal, one company, one region. The findings come from an online sample in and around Nairobi. MIT’s account itself warns against treating them as universal.
Short-term trust is not long-term legitimacy. Trust measured immediately after reading information is a snapshot. Whether repeated use of a working service eventually builds trust in government, regardless of the logo, is a separate question this study doesn’t answer.

There is also a detail that’s easy to misread. MIT’s account cites 43% trust in government from a separate 2023 survey of Kenya. That figure is context from a different study. It is not a measurement of national trust in 2026, and it shouldn’t be read as this study’s baseline.

Why this matters beyond Kenya

Analysis.

Governments everywhere are digitising. Many lack the engineering capacity, the user-experience talent, or the public reputation to do it alone, so they partner with telecoms, banks, payment providers, and tech platforms. These collaborations often deliver real services faster.

The MIT account doesn’t say this is a mistake. It says that the way a partnership is presented has political consequences that adoption numbers won’t show.

A government that brings in a trusted brand to fill a gap in competence may be renting legitimacy. Renting, by definition, isn’t owning. The service may run well while the institution behind it stays where it started, or loses ground among the people it most needed to persuade.

Run that forward in your head across dozens of services over a decade, and it becomes a real question whether citizens come to see the state as competent or as a landlord for competent contractors. The MIT findings cover a single survey moment; that ten-year scenario is my extrapolation, not their result.

The takeaway

If you remember one line, make it this:

A trusted logo can win the first click. Whether the government ever earns the trust depends on what happens after.

The study’s quietest finding may be its most useful: explaining what a service actually does was associated with more trust overall. In a crowded attention economy, that sounds almost old-fashioned. Tell people what the service does, show that it works, and let the credit land where the work was done.
