Max Verstappen started at the front. Then two cars went past him.

For a driver still chasing his first Grand Prix victory of 2026, it was an uncomfortable opening to a race that would offer very little certainty. Pole position had given him the best starting place. It had not given him control.

By the end of Sunday, October 4, at Sepang, Verstappen had recovered that control—and then defended it when a late Safety Car put the outcome back in play.

The four-time world champion won the Bahrain Grand Prix in Malaysia, finishing 2.307 seconds ahead of Kimi Antonelli after 55 laps. Lewis Hamilton completed the podium, 4.919 seconds behind the winner.

It was Verstappen’s first Grand Prix victory of the season. It was also Red Bull’s first of 2026, and, according to the FIA, the first victory for Red Bull Ford Powertrains.

Those milestones make the result significant. The way it happened makes it a story.

Rain. Delays. A lead lost at the standing start. A recovery through the Mercedes cars. Different tyre choices. An outside pass at Turn 1. Then, late in the race, another interruption that threatened to turn a hard-earned advantage into unfinished business.

The wait ended on Sunday. The uncertainty lasted almost to the end.

Before following that sequence, one geographical detail deserves clarity: this was officially the Bahrain Grand Prix, but it took place at Sepang in Malaysia. Formula 1 announced the arrangement on July 26, 2026. The unusual event name was established months before race weekend; Sunday’s victory happened in Malaysia.

And it happened the difficult way.

Verstappen arrived on the grid with pole position, intermediate tyres and the opportunity to turn a strong starting position into the result that had eluded him throughout the season.

The weather had already complicated proceedings. Rain caused a delay, and an interrupted start procedure produced another.

Such interruptions are part of the factual record. What they meant inside individual cockpits is not. There is no need to invent frustration, anxiety or a private rallying speech to explain the stakes.

The visible situation was dramatic enough: a multiple world champion, still winless that season, waiting to begin from the front in unsettled conditions.

Then the standing start changed the order.

Antonelli went ahead. Russell did too.

Verstappen, who had begun with nobody in front of him, now had two Mercedes cars to overcome.

That reversal is essential to understanding the victory. This was not a case of converting pole into an uninterrupted afternoon at the head of the field. The driver who would eventually win first had to recover positions he had lost.

A front-row advantage can disappear in seconds. Recovering it can take the rest of a race.

Verstappen subsequently passed Russell, moving one step closer to the lead. Antonelli remained the next obstacle.

The early race had already stripped away any simple narrative. Pole had not settled the contest. Reputation had not preserved track position. Whatever Verstappen’s previous achievements, this particular victory still had to be assembled from the opportunities available that afternoon.

The next opportunity came with a Safety Car.

Valtteri Bottas spun, prompting the intervention and giving drivers on intermediate tyres a chance to switch to slicks.

At that point, the leading teams made different decisions: Red Bull selected Soft tyres for Verstappen; Mercedes chose Mediums.

It is a tempting moment to circle in red and label the entire explanation.

Softs versus Mediums. One call wins the race. Case closed.

But the evidence supports a more careful reading.

The tyre choices were real. So was the pass that followed. The available account does not establish that compound selection alone decided the victory, nor does it separate the precise contribution of the tyres from the driver, car, restart and changing conditions.

What can be said confidently is that Red Bull chose a different route from Mercedes—and Verstappen made progress after racing resumed.

He passed Antonelli around the outside at Turn 1.

There is no need to decorate that move with invented commentary or imagined reactions. Its importance is contained in the sequence itself.

Verstappen had lost the lead at the start. He had passed Russell. Now, after the Safety Car and the switch to slick tyres, he passed Antonelli and established a lead.

The recovery was complete.

The race was not.

Still, that outside move provides the clearest turning point in the story. A strategy decision had created a particular set of circumstances. Verstappen then converted those circumstances into first place on the track.

That distinction matters because racing analysis often compresses a complicated victory into one convenient explanation. Sometimes it is the pit wall. Sometimes the tyre. Sometimes the driver’s reputation.

Here, the record shows a chain of events, each requiring the next.

Red Bull made its choice. The restart provided an opening. Verstappen completed the pass. He then built an advantage.

A strategy can create an opportunity. It cannot make the overtaking move.

That is an interpretation of the race, not a claim about the team’s private reasoning. Without fuller evidence, it would be premature to call the Soft selection an inevitable masterstroke or proof that Mercedes had chosen a losing path.

The more persuasive conclusion is also the more interesting one: Red Bull and Verstappen responded effectively to a changing race.

They did not need the afternoon to unfold perfectly from the start. They needed to keep finding a way forward after it did not.

For a team seeking its first Grand Prix win of the year, that ability matters. A season’s first breakthrough can arrive through overwhelming pace, flawless execution or a sequence of recoveries.

At Sepang, the recoveries were impossible to miss.

Once ahead, Verstappen established a lead. For the first time, the story could reasonably begin shifting from pursuit to protection.

But Formula 1 has a way of making a margin look solid until an incident elsewhere makes it suddenly fragile.

Alex Albon’s late stoppage brought out a Virtual Safety Car. A full Safety Car followed.

Further pit stops and the compression of the field put Verstappen’s lead at risk again.

This was the second major change in the race’s shape. Earlier, a Safety Car had helped create the conditions for his move into first place. Later, another intervention threatened the advantage he had built there.

That symmetry is what gives the victory its tension.

An interruption can open a door. The next one can bring everybody back to it.

The practical effect was that the leader could no longer rely on the same cushion. Cars were gathered closer together, and the closing phase became another examination of whether Verstappen could remain ahead.

The supplied reports do not justify inventing a last-lap duel, a particular defensive manoeuvre or an exact moment when the result became safe. What they establish is sufficient: his lead faced renewed pressure, and he retained it through the finish.

After 55 laps, the official gap to Antonelli was 2.307 seconds.

Hamilton followed in third, 4.919 seconds behind Verstappen.

Those numbers describe the final separation. They do not reveal every competitive detail of the closing laps, and they should not be stretched into claims about what would have happened with another lap or another restart.

They do, however, give the race a precise ending.

Verstappen first. Antonelli second. Hamilton third.

A season-long wait reduced, at last, to a completed result.

Russell’s afternoon ended differently.

After featuring in the early fight at the front, he retired having completed 49 laps. The supplied material does not establish the cause of his failure, so attributing it to a particular component or technical problem would go beyond the evidence.

His retirement belongs in the account because he had been central to its opening: one of the two drivers who passed Verstappen at the standing start.

By the finish, those early protagonists had sharply different outcomes. Verstappen had recovered to win. Antonelli stood second in the classification. Russell had retired.

That contrast illustrates how little the opening order could promise about the final one.

It also makes Antonelli’s result worth acknowledging on its own terms. He took the lead at the start and finished runner-up, just over two seconds behind the winner. Hamilton completed the podium within five seconds of first place.

Neither result needs to be inflated into a larger claim about the championship. The finishing positions themselves are clear achievements in a disrupted race.

For Verstappen and Red Bull, though, the significance extended beyond the podium order.

Until Sunday, neither had won a Grand Prix in 2026. Now both had.

That sounds almost too simple to carry much weight. Yet it marks a concrete change in the season’s record. A possibility became an outcome. A pursuit became a result.

For Red Bull Ford Powertrains, the FIA’s designation adds another milestone: a first victory.

What it does not add is proof of a permanent technical advantage.

One win cannot, by itself, establish that an engineering programme has solved every weakness, that a team will dominate the next event or that its rivals have reached their limits. A race affected by rain, Safety Cars and differing tyre choices is especially resistant to such sweeping conclusions.

The result demonstrates that this combination won at Sepang, under these circumstances, on October 4.

That is substantial enough.

A breakthrough deserves recognition before it becomes a prediction.

The same restraint applies to Verstappen’s championship prospects.

A first win can change the tone of a season. It cannot retrospectively erase everything that preceded it, and the supplied facts offer no basis for declaring a championship comeback.

There is no points calculation here establishing a new title trajectory. No evidence that future victories are guaranteed. No reason to turn one successful Sunday into a settled forecast.

The strongest reading is narrower: Verstappen and Red Bull have now shown that they can turn a difficult, interrupted Grand Prix into a win in 2026.

The next question is whether they can repeat it.

Repeatability is what separates a breakthrough from a sustained competitive shift. That answer will have to come from later races.

For now, Sepang offers something more immediate and more satisfying than speculation: a complete sporting arc.

The pole-sitter lost the lead.

The pursuer passed Russell.

The challenger took a different tyre choice from Mercedes and passed Antonelli around the outside.

The leader established an advantage.

Then the late Safety Car made that advantage vulnerable, and the leader still finished first.

Each stage changed what Verstappen needed to do. None could be skipped simply because he was a four-time world champion.

That is why this first victory of the season carries narrative force. Past success supplied the context. Sunday supplied the test.

It would be easy to end with a grand declaration that Verstappen is “back,” as though a driver’s competitive standing can be settled by a single afternoon. It would be equally easy to reduce the race to a tyre gamble that happened to pay off.

Both descriptions flatten what actually happened.

The record shows setbacks, decisions, overtaking and a final result secured after the field closed up again. It shows a driver and team succeeding through several changes in circumstance, without proving that the next race will resemble this one.

There is room to celebrate that achievement without borrowing certainty from the future.

On October 4, 2026, at Sepang, Verstappen ended his wait. Red Bull ended its wait. Red Bull Ford Powertrains recorded the first victory identified by the FIA.

The championship remained unfinished. The next win remained unpromised.
