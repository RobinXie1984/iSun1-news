Max Verstappen's 2026 Wait Is Over at Sepang. Pole Position Didn't Make It Easy.
Three headline options

Suspenseful: Pole, Lost Lead, Two Safety Cars: How Verstappen Kept Hold of His First 2026 Win

Emotional resonance: Four Titles, Then Months Without a Win. Sepang Ended Max Verstappen's 2026 Wait.

Counter-intuitive: Verstappen Started on Pole, Lost the Lead Immediately, and Won Anyway

The Article

There's an old assumption in Formula 1: put the best driver on pole and the race takes care of itself.

Sepang didn't go that way.

On October 4, 2026, Max Verstappen won a Grand Prix for the first time this season. It was also Red Bull's first win of 2026, and the FIA described it as the first victory for Red Bull Ford Powertrains.

A pole-sitter winning sounds like a simple result. This one wasn't. He lost the lead at the start, got it back, and then nearly lost it again late in the race.

Before anything else, here is what this win was and what it wasn't.

It was not Verstappen's first career victory. He's a four-time world champion. It was his first win of 2026, and that's worth stating clearly before the hot takes start. This was a long wait for a win, not a career breakthrough.

It also wasn't a race in Bahrain. Officially it was the Bahrain Grand Prix, run in Malaysia, at Sepang. Formula 1 announced that arrangement on July 26, 2026. That part isn't news. The news is what happened on the track.

Part One: The rain arrived first

What the sources report: Rain delayed the race. After that, an interrupted start procedure caused another delay.

So the race was late getting going, and then late again.

Verstappen started from pole on intermediate tyres. Then the lights went out and the advantage disappeared.

What the sources report: At the standing start, Verstappen lost the lead to Kimi Antonelli and George Russell.

He went from first to third before the race had settled.

My read: This is the most underrated part of the story. People often treat pole as the hard part and the race as the payoff. In wet conditions on a track that's still finding grip, pole is closer to a head start that you can lose in the first few hundred metres, and Verstappen lost it right away.

What followed is what makes the win.

Part Two: Working back through the Mercedes cars

What the sources report: Verstappen then passed Russell.

That moved him up to second, behind Antonelli.

The sources don't give a detailed blow-by-blow of that pass, so I won't make one up. The confirmed facts are that he lost two places at the start and won one back.

He was still behind a car, though, and the race was still on intermediates.

Then Valtteri Bottas spun.

Part Three: The tyre decision

What the sources report: A Safety Car after Bottas's spin gave the drivers on intermediates a chance to switch to slicks. Red Bull chose Soft tyres. Mercedes chose Mediums.

This is the race's main strategic split, and it's where a lot of coverage will be tempted to overreach.

What the sources report: After the restart, Verstappen passed Antonelli around the outside at Turn 1 and pulled out a lead.

Around the outside, at Turn 1, on a restart, on a drying track. That's a bold place to make a pass.

My read, and I want to be careful here: The obvious story is that Red Bull gambled on Softs, Mercedes played it safe on Mediums, and the Softs won the race. It's tidy and easy to share.

The record doesn't prove it.

What we know is that two teams made two different tyre choices and that Verstappen passed Antonelli after the restart. Those are observed facts. Whether the Soft compound was the deciding factor, a factor, or mostly irrelevant compared with driver skill and track position is something the supplied facts can't settle. A softer tyre generally warms up and gives grip sooner, so it's a plausible advantage at a restart. Plausible isn't proven, and I'd rather say that plainly than claim more than I know.

What I can say with confidence is that strategy created the opportunity and the driver used it. Tyres don't overtake around the outside at Turn 1. Drivers do.

Part Four: The late threat

Once Verstappen had a lead, it looked like the rest might be routine.

It wasn't.

What the sources report: Alex Albon stopped late in the race, which brought out a Virtual Safety Car and then a full Safety Car. More pit stops followed, the field was bunched up, and the lead was at risk again.

That's the hard part of leading an F1 race. A gap you built over many laps can be wiped out by an incident you had nothing to do with. A Safety Car brings the whole pack back to your rear wing and gives everyone behind you another shot at a restart.

Verstappen had to defend the lead a second time.

What the sources report: He was still ahead at the finish.

Meanwhile, George Russell retired after completing 49 laps.

The supplied record doesn't say why Russell's race ended, and I won't guess. In this sport a guess about a failure often gets repeated as fact. What's confirmed is that he was out after 49 laps of a 55-lap race and that one of the cars Verstappen had been fighting all day didn't finish.

The official result

Here's the classification, without commentary:

Race distance: 55 laps
1st: Max Verstappen
2nd: Kimi Antonelli, +2.307 seconds
3rd: Lewis Hamilton, +4.919 seconds

My read: Look at the margins. 2.3 seconds to second and under five seconds to third. After that many interruptions, those gaps are small. This wasn't a dominant win. Verstappen led at the end of a race that kept trying to take the lead away from him, and the official result shows how close it was.

What this win tells us, and what it doesn't

This is where reporting stops and opinion starts, so here is my analysis, labelled as analysis.

1. Getting the lead and keeping it are separate skills

Verstappen had to win the lead back twice: once after a bad start and once after a late Safety Car. Pole showed he had speed. The result showed he could recover. Those aren't the same thing, and Sepang tested both.

2. "First win for Red Bull Ford Powertrains" is a milestone, not a verdict

The FIA's wording matters because it marks a first for the powertrain project. A first win shows the package can win. It doesn't show the package will keep winning. This victory came in wet, disrupted conditions with Safety Car timing, a tyre split, and a rival retirement all in play. Some of that came down to circumstance.

So I won't call it an engineering breakthrough. Nothing in the supplied facts supports that. One win in a chaotic race tells you the car was competitive on this day, in these conditions, and nothing more general than that.

3. Strategy versus skill is a false choice

The most useful thing to take from this race, I think, is that the tyre call and the pass weren't competing explanations. One set up the other. A strategy only pays off if the driver can execute it, and the most aggressive driver still needs the team to give him a chance. Sepang needed both, and I'd be suspicious of any analysis that credits only one.

4. The wait is over. What comes next is unknown.

This is where the coverage is most likely to run ahead of the facts.

A first win after months without one invites a comeback story: the champion is back, the season turns, the title fight restarts. Maybe that happens and maybe it doesn't. Neither I nor anyone else knows yet. The facts here are about one race. Future wins and championship outcomes remain unknown, and an article that claims otherwise is guessing.

What we do know is that the wait for a 2026 win, for Verstappen and for Red Bull, ended at Sepang on October 4.
