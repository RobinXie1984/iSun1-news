# An AI-Assisted Proof Narrows Quantum’s Query Advantage on Most Unstructured Yes/No Inputs—What Remains Beyond Its Reach?

Quantum computers process information using the rules of quantum physics, and mathematicians have spent decades asking where those rules give them an advantage. On November 6, 2026, the research company Physical Superintelligence, or PSI, announced a proof of the long-standing Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture. Its implications concern a particular contest: answering yes/no questions about most random, unstructured inputs while counting how often a computer consults its information source. A human exposition bears the date November 7, and conjecture co-originator Scott Aaronson expressed confidence in the proof on November 8. Together, these developments bring a difficult mathematical question into sharper focus—and raise another question about the AI system credited with finding the answer.

The broader promise of quantum computing rests on differences in how machines handle information. An ordinary computer works with bits, each carrying a value of zero or one. A quantum computer can arrange its information in a superposition, a state whose possibilities combine according to quantum rules. Carefully designed operations allow those possibilities to reinforce or cancel one another, helping an algorithm extract a useful answer. The size of that advantage depends on the problem and on the resources being measured.

Here, the resource is access to information. Researchers study an input through an “oracle,” a mathematical name for a black-box information source. An algorithm can ask it questions while its internal construction remains hidden. In the setting relevant to this result, a classical query reads one input bit. A quantum query can address positions in superposition. Query complexity counts these accesses, giving researchers a precise way to compare two methods even when the engineering of a complete computer remains far more complicated.

For an everyday illustration, imagine a sealed board holding many switches, each either up or down. Two people must answer the same yes/no question about the board. One may inspect a chosen switch with each probe. The other has a special instrument that probes combinations of positions according to quantum rules. The contest counts probes. Time spent thinking between probes belongs to a separate account.

This imaginary instrument stands in for quantum access; actual superposition produces information through a quantum state and its measurement. Receiving every switch setting in a single glance would give the illustration a different power. The useful part of the picture is the sealed board: both contestants must learn enough about hidden information, and each access has a price.

A yes/no question is called a decision problem. Asking whether a board has a specified property fits that category. Asking someone to locate a particular switch is a search task. Asking for any output satisfying a rule belongs to a broader class called relation problems. These distinctions matter because the form of the requested answer can change the opportunities available to an algorithm.

The Aaronson–Ambainis result concerns the decision setting on most Boolean inputs—strings of zeroes and ones—under a random or unstructured view of the input. “Most” carries mathematical weight. A statement about typical inputs leaves room for exceptional inputs and for families deliberately chosen to have special structure. The theorem’s setting therefore needs to travel with every account of its significance.

Within that setting, the result ties classical and quantum query requirements together through polynomial bounds. In plain language, the classical query allowance can be controlled by fixed powers of the relevant quantities in the comparison. That gives a powerful theoretical restriction on how far the query counts can separate. A polynomial relationship can still accommodate a substantial practical gap, and it supplies a relationship among mathematical resources rather than a measured speed ratio between two machines.

The distinction becomes especially important between queries. An algorithm might use comparatively few accesses while performing extensive calculations to decide which question to ask next. Memory, elapsed time, energy use and hardware construction each have their own costs. This proof addresses one carefully defined part of computing, allowing a precise conclusion at that level.

How can a theorem about hidden bits reach into the behaviour of a quantum algorithm? The bridge is an algebraic expression called a polynomial. A polynomial combines numbers and variables through addition and multiplication. Here, its variables represent the input bits, and its value describes the probability that the quantum algorithm answers “yes.”

If the algorithm makes \(T\) queries, that probability can be represented by a polynomial whose degree is at most \(2T\). To find the degree, add up the exponents of the variables in each term, then take the largest total among terms with nonzero coefficients. Equivalently, count the multiplied variable factors in each term, including repetitions: \(x\) has degree one, \(xy\) has degree two, and \(x^2y\) has degree three. For the relevant representation, this gives a way to track the algebraic interactions produced as the algorithm gathers information. Degree is a property of the expression, distinct from the number of processors or the physical size of a device.

The probability also stays within its natural bounds, from zero to one. Those constraints give mathematicians something firm to study: an expression with limited degree and controlled values, even when its input contains many bits. The quantum procedure has become a mathematical object whose sensitivity to individual bits can be examined.

That sensitivity is the role of “influence.” Roughly, a bit has influence when changing it affects the function’s value. Another quantity, variance, measures how much the function’s value changes across inputs. A function that barely changes presents a different information problem from one whose values vary substantially.

The conjecture connects these ideas. For a bounded polynomial of the relevant kind, some input coordinate has appreciable influence relative to the polynomial’s degree and variance. Its useful variation must leave a sufficiently strong trace in at least one individual bit. The exact statement depends on mathematical definitions; the conceptual point is that limited algebraic complexity constrains how thoroughly the information can be dispersed.

Return to the switch board. Suppose the answer probability changes meaningfully from board to board. The influence result offers a reason that some switch can provide useful information, with its importance controlled by the relevant mathematical quantities. Such informative coordinates support a classical strategy for learning about the answer. Turning that insight into a theorem requires the full argument, but the connection is accessible: a rule governing where information appears becomes a rule governing how many questions a computer needs to ask.

This mathematical route has a human history. Earlier work includes a plan by Shachar Lovett and Jiapeng Zhang and ideas associated with David Reimer. The argument draws on analysis of Boolean functions, which studies rules operating on bits, and probabilistic combinatorics, which studies arrangements and counting with the help of probability. The proof belongs to that accumulated body of mathematics, even as its reported discovery introduces a new participant.

PSI says its internally developed research system used instances of a non-public Anthropic model. According to the company, a first complete proof emerged on the third day of a run, followed by two improvements. That sequence gives the discovery a striking pace. Its evidential scope is one company-described run. Public identification of the model currently extends to its developer and non-public status. Reconstructing the complete discovery procedure and separating the model’s contribution from that of the surrounding software remain open research tasks. The run’s computing requirements and financial cost likewise await disclosure.

The third-day detail consequently describes a chronology. Estimating how reliably such a system solves difficult problems would require a broader record of attempts, their selection and their outcomes. The evidence available here supports an account of a particular achievement while leaving that wider research-performance question open.

A 32-page exposition of the shared argument by Guy Blanc, Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle and Li-Yang Tan is dated November 7. They report using public Anthropic models to help prepare their explanation, a separate activity from PSI’s discovery run. They also state that they received zero financial compensation from PSI or Anthropic and describe the document as an exposition outside the journal and conference submission process.

That explanatory effort matters because a proof has several audiences. A formal system requires an exact logical construction. Human readers need to understand the ideas, their connections and the scope of the conclusion. An exposition gives mathematicians a route through the argument and a basis for further scrutiny. Conventional journal peer review remains a separate status.

Aaronson’s November 8 assessment adds another kind of evidence. He says he believes the proof is correct, citing a Lean certificate and examination by human experts. His own understanding of the full argument remains incomplete. Those two statements belong together: his confidence rests on specified grounds, while his personal reconstruction of the proof remains in progress.

Lean is a proof assistant, software that checks formal mathematical reasoning. A user supplies a precisely written proposition and a proof built from accepted foundations and permitted logical steps. Lean’s checking machinery evaluates whether that formal proof establishes the stated proposition. This gives readers something more concrete than a confident announcement: an exact claim and a logical construction available for inspection.

The public PSI repository supplies the paper and Lean materials. Its developers also report source and dependency audits, examinations of the logical assumptions used, replays through the core proof-checking machinery, and a comparator or checker route. These records describe several checks addressing different possible faults. The historical verification notes carry an October 22 date, preceding the November 6 announcement. Their status here is developer-reported verification; this publication’s account rests on those records.

Formal checking also has a boundary that deserves plain explanation. A checker establishes a relationship between the proposition written in its formal language and the proof submitted for it. Mathematicians must additionally assess whether that proposition captures the intended conjecture and whether the prose explanation faithfully matches the formal argument. The repository’s notes distinguish these tasks. The logical foundations and software remain part of the system on which the checking depends.

That distinction resembles checking a carefully drafted contract against a set of rules. A rigorous check can establish that the written terms satisfy those rules. Understanding whether the terms express the intended agreement requires attention to meaning as well. For mathematics, this correspondence between intention, formal statement and explanation is part of assessing what has actually been proved.

Taken together, the public claim, formal materials, reported checks and human exposition create a substantial basis for mathematical scrutiny. Each contributes a different kind of evidence. Their combined value comes from allowing readers to examine the argument at several levels, with the theorem’s precise scope remaining central.

That scope also explains why major quantum applications continue to stand on their own foundations. Shor’s 1994 factoring algorithm exploits periodic mathematical structure. Its method belongs to a setting shaped by that structure, compatible with a theorem concerning most unstructured inputs. The consequences for encryption depend on additional questions about algorithms, machine capabilities and the systems being protected.

Simulating physical systems, including those relevant to physics and chemistry, likewise remains a separate application area. Search and relation problems can behave differently from yes/no decision problems even with random oracles. Hardware usefulness and investment value require their own evidence about tasks, costs and performance. The mathematical result sharpens one boundary in this larger landscape.

There is a second boundary running through the story: the distance between access to a result and access to the process that discovered it. Readers can study the published mathematics, formal statements, explanatory work and verification records. Reproducing PSI’s complete discovery run would require further access to the model, surrounding research software and experimental choices. Public scrutiny of a proof and reproducible discovery are distinct achievements, each answering a worthwhile question.

For quantum computing, the advance offers a more precise account of where a particular kind of advantage can arise. For AI-assisted research, it presents an inspectable mathematical result alongside a partly disclosed discovery process. The immediate work is the continuing examination of the proof and its meaning. Beyond that lies a compelling question: how much of this reported success can become a repeatable method that other researchers can understand, test and use?
