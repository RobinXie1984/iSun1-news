# A Company's AI System Found a Long-Sought Quantum Proof. How Much Does It Settle?

On November 6, 2026, a research company called Physical Superintelligence, or PSI, announced a proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, a mathematical statement that theorists of quantum computing have studied for many years. A conjecture is a careful guess that mathematicians believe and have yet to prove. This one is named for the two researchers who proposed it, one of whom is the computer scientist Scott Aaronson. It concerns quantum computers, which are machines that use the rules of quantum physics to process information. The conjecture asks a simple-sounding question. When such a machine answers a yes/no question about a large, random string of data, how much of its advantage could an ordinary computer recover by asking clever questions of its own? PSI says the proof came from its own research system, which ran a non-public model made by Anthropic. Other researchers soon engaged with the result. A 32-page explanation of the proof, dated November 7, comes from four researchers: Guy Blanc of MIT and Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle and Li-Yang Tan of Stanford. On November 8, Aaronson wrote about the result on his blog.

These three events form an unusual sequence. Announcements about artificial intelligence and quantum computing often arrive as a single bold claim. This one came with material that anyone with the right training can open. It includes a written paper and a library of machine-checkable proof files in a public repository. It also includes an explanation by researchers outside the company and a qualified endorsement from one of the people who posed the problem. A phrase like "AI proves quantum conjecture" suggests a turning point for quantum machines. The actual result is narrower, and its exact limits are the story. The theorem counts how many times a computer must look at its input. It covers yes/no questions, on most inputs, in a random, unstructured setting. To see what that rules out and what it leaves standing, start with the problem itself.

## The question inside the box

Quantum computers attract attention because of a hope called quantum advantage. The hope is that for certain tasks, a quantum machine will finish dramatically faster than any ordinary computer, which researchers call a classical computer. Theorists test that hope in many ways. One of the cleanest is called query complexity.

Picture the input to a problem sealed inside a box that answers questions. Researchers call such a box an oracle. An algorithm, meaning a step-by-step method, may ask the box about any position in the input. Query complexity counts how many questions it asks. Everything else sits outside the count, including the arithmetic between questions, memory, electricity, engineering and price. Query complexity measures how much information an algorithm must pull from its input. That narrow focus makes it a sharp tool for comparing two kinds of machines.

The input in this story is a long string of bits. Each bit is a 0 or a 1, and mathematicians call such inputs Boolean. A classical query names one position and receives the bit stored there. A quantum query can address many positions at once through superposition. Superposition is a quantum condition in which a system holds a weighted combination of possibilities. Later steps can make those possibilities reinforce or cancel one another. The question asked about the whole string is a decision problem, meaning it has a yes or no answer. An example is whether the string satisfies a particular rule.

An everyday picture can help, and it is purely an illustration. Imagine a sealed cabinet with thousands of numbered switches, each set up or down. A rule gives a yes or no verdict about the whole arrangement. One investigator opens small doors one at a time and looks at single switches. A second investigator holds a special probe that consults the cabinet in a combined way. Each use of the probe gathers a blended signal from many switches. Now count how many times each investigator uses their tool. The Aaronson-Ambainis question asks about cabinets whose switches are set at random. Can the probe-holder win by an enormous margin there, or does the door-opener always stay within reach? The picture has clear limits. Real superposition is a mathematical property of quantum states, which is different from opening many doors at once. The cabinet also illustrates query counts alone and leaves running time and hardware aside.

The new result answers the question in the door-opener's favor. PSI's announcement, Aaronson's discussion and the exposition all describe the same result. For decision problems, on most Boolean inputs in a random or unstructured oracle setting, a classical algorithm can imitate a quantum algorithm. The number of classical queries it needs is polynomially related to the number of quantum queries. "Polynomially related" means the classical count stays within a fixed power of the quantum count.

The distinction is easiest to see in symbols. Call the quantum count T. A polynomial relationship means the classical count is bounded by T raised to some fixed power. An exponential gap would mean a classical count that grows like a fixed number raised to the power T, a quantity that pulls ahead of every fixed power of T as T increases. The public proof gives precise mathematical bounds, and the sources state them using differing conventions. This article keeps to the general polynomial picture.

Each qualifying word in that result matters. "Most" inputs leaves room for rare, special arrangements where the result may behave differently. "Random or unstructured" describes the input string: its bits are drawn like coin flips, and the result covers most strings drawn that way. The algorithm may know the yes/no rule in full. The randomness lives in the string it reads through queries. "Decision problems" limits the claim to yes/no questions. A polynomial relationship between query counts is also separate from any promise of fast running time on a real classical computer. The cited sources address query counts alone. Any practical speedup or slowdown on real machines remains unmeasured in them.

## How a quantum algorithm becomes a polynomial

The link between quantum machines and this conjecture is an older piece of mathematics. Suppose a quantum algorithm asks the oracle T questions. The probability that it answers "yes" can then be written as a polynomial in the input bits. A polynomial here is a formula built by multiplying bits together and adding the results. Its value stays between zero and one, and its degree is at most 2T. Degree counts how many bits are multiplied together in the formula's largest term. It roughly measures how intricately the bits interact. Degree describes the complexity of the formula, which is separate from the length of the input and from the physical size of any quantum device.

Turning the algorithm into a polynomial changes the question into one about polynomials. Two more ideas come in at this point. Variance measures how much the formula's output swings as the input changes; a formula that gives nearly the same number everywhere has low variance. Influence measures how much flipping one particular bit tends to change the output. The conjecture says that every such bounded polynomial contains at least one bit with real influence, measured against the polynomial's degree and variance. The sources write that bound using different conventions, so this article describes it in words.

An influential bit helps the classical investigator because it tells the door-opener where to look. The algorithm queries that bit and fixes its value, and the remaining formula becomes simpler. Repeating this, the classical algorithm narrows the possibilities step by step. On most inputs, it eventually reaches a point where it can predict what the quantum algorithm would answer. In the cabinet picture, the theorem's conditions still apply. The result says the yes/no rule has some switch whose influence is appreciable when measured against the rule's degree and variance. That switch gives the door-opener a worthwhile place to check, and repeating the process carries the search forward on most random cabinets.

The path to the proof drew on earlier human mathematics. PSI's announcement and the exposition connect the argument to a plan proposed by Shachar Lovett and Jiapeng Zhang. They also connect it to ideas associated with David Reimer. The exposition places the argument in two fields of mathematics. One is the analysis of Boolean functions, the study of formulas whose inputs are bits. The other is probabilistic combinatorics, which uses probability to reason about arrangements and counts. The work described in these sources consists of mathematical argument, formal proof files and software checks, and the result's support rests on that mathematics. The public accounts leave each contributor's share of the final argument unmeasured. A complete account of the work names these researchers alongside the machine.

## What the company says happened

The account of how the proof was discovered comes from PSI. The company says its internally developed research system ran copies of a non-public Anthropic model. It says a first complete proof appeared on the third day of a run, and that the system then improved the result twice. These are process claims reported by the company.

Several details remain unknown from the public record. These include the model version, how the work divided between the model and the proprietary PSI system around it, the full sequence of steps needed to repeat the discovery, and the computing and cash costs. The proof itself is public, while the system that found it remains largely closed.

## A ladder of evidence

For a reader deciding how much to believe, the evidence works like a ladder. Each rung supports a different claim.

The first rung is Lean. PSI's public repository holds a paper and a Lean library. Lean is a proof assistant, a program that checks each step of a proof written in a precise formal language. It checks those steps against stated propositions and a fixed set of logical foundations. Two notes written by the repository's maintainers describe checks that go beyond one successful build.

One note, AXIOM_AUDIT.md, reports an audit of the formal source files and of the assumptions and outside code they depend on. The other, VERIFICATION.md, reports two kinds of check. The first replays the proof through Lean's core checker, called the kernel. The second runs it through a separate comparator or checking path. The verification records are dated October 22, two weeks before the November 6 announcement, and they describe the maintainers' own checks at that time. This article relies on the maintainers' reports.

Formal checking answers one specific question: does the formal theorem follow from its formal premises? The repository notes keep that question apart from two others. One is whether the formal statement captures every nuance of the intended conjecture written in ordinary mathematical language. The other is whether the prose paper matches the formal files line by line. A reader also has to trust Lean's own software and the standard logical foundations. Within those limits, the reported checks are strong evidence for the formal proposition.

The second rung is the exposition dated November 7. Blanc, Docter, Strassle and Tan say they studied the proof PSI shared and wrote a 32-page explanation of it. They report that public Anthropic models helped with that writing work. They also state that they received zero financial payment from PSI or Anthropic. They describe the document as an explanation meant to stay outside the journal and conference system. Their work adds something that formal checking supplies poorly: human understanding of what the argument means and why it holds together. The exposition is serious expert engagement with the proof, and it is separate from conventional peer review.

The third rung is Aaronson. On November 8, he wrote that he believes the proof is correct. He pointed to the Lean certificate and to examination by human experts as his reasons. In the same post, he said that he has yet to understand the entire proof himself. The two statements belong together. His confidence carries weight because he helped pose the problem and knows where it is difficult. His stated limit makes the post an informed endorsement. It is different from a personal line-by-line check or a formal referee's report.

Together, the three rungs support a narrow and solid conclusion. A long-standing conjecture now has a proof package that anyone can inspect, along with reported formal checks, an independent human explanation and a qualified endorsement from a co-originator. That is stronger evidence than an announcement alone. Acceptance by a journal remains unestablished. Mathematicians' confidence in the result will grow, or meet challenges, as more of them read it.

## What remains outside the theorem

Quantum advantage covers far more ground than this result. Peter Shor's 1994 factoring algorithm is the famous quantum method for breaking large numbers into their prime factors. It works by exploiting a hidden periodic structure, a repeating rhythm in the numbers involved. The new theorem concerns random, unstructured inputs, so Shor's algorithm lies outside its reach. Quantum simulation of physics and chemistry, where a quantum machine models molecules and materials, is also outside what the theorem rules out. Search problems ask the algorithm to find a particular object. Relation problems ask it to produce one of many acceptable answers. Both kinds can behave differently from yes/no questions, even in random oracle settings.

The broader practical questions remain where they were. The value of quantum hardware programs, the security of particular encryption systems and the classical difficulty of quantum problems in general all depend on evidence beyond this theorem. The theorem settles one specific boundary. For yes/no questions about most random inputs, quantum machines hold at most a polynomial edge in query counts. In the cabinet picture, the probe-holder can still save effort on a random cabinet, and those savings stay within a bounded scale.

That leaves two different objects side by side. The proof is open to checking. A mathematician can read the exposition, a programmer can run Lean on the files, and a skeptic can test whether the formal statement matches the conjecture. Repeating the discovery itself requires access that PSI controls. More mathematical results may come from systems the public sees only through what they produce. That raises a practical question. How should readers weigh a discovery when the result is open to inspection, and the process that produced it remains largely private?
