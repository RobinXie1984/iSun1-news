# New York Bet Nearly $13 Million on 15 Energy Storage Projects. Which Ones Will Deliver?

On November 6, 2026, the New York State Energy Research and Development Authority, known as NYSERDA, announced nearly $13 million in awards to 15 projects that develop new ways to store electricity. NYSERDA is the state's public-benefit authority for energy research and development. It chooses promising ideas and helps pay for them, while companies and university labs design, build and test the equipment. NYSERDA made these awards through its Power Generation and Storage Innovation program, which is funded by New York's Clean Energy Fund; the announcement reports the awards, and the timing of payments to each team remains unstated. The winning teams range from companies proposing complete battery systems for the power grid to groups improving a single ingredient inside a battery cell. Some awards fund planned demonstrations in real-world settings such as a substation or a vehicle charging site; others fund laboratory prototypes, new materials and factory methods. Together they raise one question: which of these ideas can grow into equipment that does a specific job safely, reliably and at a sensible cost?

That question is open today. The announcement records funding decisions and describes what each team plans to do. The public record available for this article covers those decisions and plans; project-specific operating results and verified savings lie outside it. The story of these awards is the gap between a promising plan and a working machine, and the measurements that could close it.

## Why storage matters, and how it works

Electricity has an unusual habit. The amount produced and the amount used must match from moment to moment, all day and all night. When people come home and switch on stoves, heaters and air conditioners, the grid must supply that extra demand at once. When the sun sets on solar panels or the wind drops, other sources must take over.

Energy storage gives the grid some slack in that balancing act. The U.S. Energy Information Administration, the federal government's energy statistics agency, describes storage as a secondary source of electricity. A storage system takes in electricity that some power plant already produced, holds that energy in another form, and releases part of it later. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, a battery stores chemical potential energy: charging drives chemical reactions that hold energy in the battery's materials, and discharging runs those reactions in reverse to release electricity. Other systems hold energy as compressed air or heat.

Storage always returns less electricity than it took in. Charging, conversion and the machinery that keeps a system running all consume some energy along the way. The trade becomes worthwhile when the returned energy arrives at a more valuable time or place: during an evening peak, at a crowded local substation, or during a disruption. According to the EIA, storage can help balance supply and demand, ease strain on parts of the network, support reliability and smooth out variable sources such as wind and solar. Those benefits depend on where a system sits, how it connects, what electricity charges it and how operators run it.

Two numbers describe any storage system, and they answer different questions. Power is a rate, measured in kilowatts (kW) or megawatts (MW); one megawatt equals 1,000 kilowatts. Energy is an amount, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) or megawatt-hours (MWh); one megawatt-hour equals 1,000 kilowatt-hours.

An everyday picture helps here, purely as an illustration. Imagine a water tank with a tap at the bottom. The size of the tank is like energy: it tells you how much water is stored. The width of the tap is like power: it tells you how fast water can pour out. A big tank with a narrow tap can trickle for a long time. A small tank with a wide tap can gush briefly and then run dry. To learn how long the tank will last, you need both numbers. Real electrical storage adds complications that a water tank leaves out, such as charging losses, wear over many cycles and safety limits, so the picture explains the units and stops there.

Duration, the number of hours a system can keep delivering, comes from dividing energy by power. A system holding 20 MWh that releases energy at a steady 2 MW would, in an idealized case with perfect usable capacity, run for 10 hours. That arithmetic serves as a teaching example. How much usable energy a real system delivers depends on the convention used to rate its capacity and on its operating conditions, and its duration depends on the output rate an operator chooses.

## What New York is trying to buy

NYSERDA's storage program pursues two broad goals. One is long-duration energy storage, a category of systems that can deliver power for many hours at a stretch. The program's background material describes a focus on durations of roughly 10 to 100 hours. The other goal is to improve on standard lithium-ion designs and to expand the range of alternatives.

The new announcement narrows that ambition for part of the group. NYSERDA says new scalable long-duration designs in this round target more than 10 hours at rated power, meaning the full output the system is built to supply. That target belongs to those specific designs. Other awards in the same group fund new battery materials, factory methods and portable equipment, and they carry their own goals. The 100-hour figure describes the program's horizon.

Those 15 awards follow different physical routes toward different jobs. That variety is the point of the portfolio, and it is also why each project deserves its own test.

## A battery for the neighborhood substation

Inlyte Energy received over $2.4 million to deploy an iron-sodium battery intended to deliver power for more than 10 hours. Its target is a problem many utilities now face: substations, the fenced yards of equipment that step down high-voltage power for neighborhood lines, are seeing peak demand grow. The project also aims to strengthen distribution resilience, the ability of local lines to keep customers supplied during stress.

Picture a substation as the narrow end of a funnel. During a hot evening, demand from the surrounding streets pushes against the funnel's capacity. A battery parked near the substation could charge during quiet hours and release energy during the peak, letting the existing equipment handle a larger crowd. How much of a long evening peak or a stretch of unusual strain a battery designed for more than 10 hours could cover depends on its power, its usable energy and the actual load it meets.

That is the intended use. The announcement describes a planned deployment. Assessing the proposed project will take evidence on delivered energy, real-world duration and cost compared with alternatives such as upgrading the substation itself. The timing and form of any public reporting on those results remain open in the announcement.

## A small test for a large machine

Aestus Energy Storage LLC received over $590,000 for a project that shows how carefully the numbers in these announcements deserve reading. Aestus works on advanced compressed-air energy storage. In this approach, electricity drives a compressor that squeezes air into a container. Later, the stored air is released through machinery that turns its pressure back into electricity.

Heat is the hidden challenge. Squeezing air makes it hot, as anyone who has felt a bicycle pump warm up knows. If that heat escapes, the system wastes energy. If the system can capture the heat and return it when the air expands, more of the original electricity comes back. Aestus's award pays for a subscale test of about 35 kWh using two alumina-silica ceramic materials as heat sinks, solid blocks that soak up heat and later give it back.

That test supports the design of a 20 MWh compressed-air system. The two figures belong to different stages. The 35 kWh figure describes the scale of a planned small experiment on materials. The 20 MWh figure describes a larger plant that exists on the drawing board. The 20 MWh figure is also an amount of energy; the plant's power rating remains unstated in the announcement, so its duration is unknown. The earlier 10-hour example at 2 MW serves as illustration only. The test's real prize is knowledge: how well these ceramics store and return heat at a manageable scale, before anyone commits to a full plant.

## Storage for chargers, homes and labs

Other awards point storage at entirely different jobs. Sprocket Power received over $1 million to demonstrate direct-current fast charging for electric vehicles supplied by an underground vanadium redox flow battery. A flow battery stores chemical energy in liquid electrolytes held in tanks. Pumps circulate those liquids through electrochemical cells, where they flow along either side of a membrane; according to the Department of Energy, ions cross that membrane during charging and discharging as the reactions take place. Larger tanks hold more chemical energy, which makes flow batteries a natural candidate for longer durations. Fast chargers draw heavy bursts of power, and a battery beside them could soften those bursts for the local grid. The success test for this project is about chargers and drivers, a different measuring stick from Inlyte's substation.

Standard Potential received $1.8 million to design, prototype and certify a sodium-ion portable power station for appliance-level demand response in cities. Demand response means shifting electricity use away from peak hours. A portable station could charge overnight and then run a refrigerator or other household appliance during an evening peak, trimming demand one home at a time. Certification is part of the planned work, so its outcome remains ahead.

Alsym Energy received over $290,000 to design and test a 10 kWh sodium-ion prototype with the Battery Development Center at Rochester Institute of Technology. NYSERDA describes the proposed system as non-flammable. That description comes from the agency and applies to the proposed design; safety testing on the prototype is the stage where such a claim gains or loses its strength. The 10 kWh figure is an amount of energy and marks the planned size of the prototype; the project's power rating sits outside the announcement.

Urban Electric Power received nearly $1.5 million to develop and validate a zinc-manganese dioxide system, built from metals familiar from ordinary household batteries, along with monitoring and control tools for long-duration storage. Validation will show how well the chemistry and its controls perform together.

## Lithium stays in the lab

The portfolio also keeps lithium in play. AlphaEn received over $350,000 for a manufacturing platform that makes battery electrodes from recycled lithium. Other awarded projects work on polymer or organic cathodes, silicon-rich anodes and solid-state components. The cathode and anode are the two electrodes, the positive and negative ends where a battery's chemistry happens. Between them sits the electrolyte, the material that carries ions from one side to the other. According to the Department of Energy, solid-state batteries use a solid electrolyte in place of the usual liquid one.

Each of these projects changes one part of a cell. A cathode made from organic materials still sits in a battery that may contain lithium elsewhere. A silicon-rich anode improves one electrode while the rest of the cell keeps its own mix of minerals. The awards show New York spreading its bets across components and manufacturing methods. Their effect on lithium-ion's share of the market is a question for the future and for measurement.

## What has been measured, and at what scale?

Gather the figures from the announcement and they look impressive: more than 10 hours, 20 MWh, nearly $13 million, 15 projects. Each figure carries a precise meaning, and the meanings differ.

The more-than-10-hour figure is a target for certain long-duration designs. The 20 MWh figure describes a plant in design. The 35 kWh and 10 kWh figures describe planned test and prototype scales. The dollar amounts are awards, which record funding decisions; payments to the teams are a separate matter. The press release gives individual amounts with phrases such as "over" and "nearly," so its rounded figures leave the exact split between projects open. NYSERDA's separate program portfolio page lists exact award amounts for some projects and offers a useful cross-check.

The current public record therefore supports a clear status statement. New York has made awards to a diverse set of storage ideas at stages ranging from material tests to planned field deployments. The announcement and program page describe awards, plans and agency goals; project-specific operating results and verified savings lie outside that record. Judging the projects will take measured performance: round-trip efficiency, meaning the share of input electricity that comes back out; usable capacity; how quickly the systems wear out; safety qualification results; maintenance needs; and lifetime cost. The program page serves as context for the agency's interests, separate from independent testing. Claims about lower power bills, lower emissions or new local manufacturing will need evidence from operating projects.

This stage still has value. A public research portfolio works a bit like a set of experiments run side by side. A flow battery beside a fast charger, an iron-sodium battery at a substation, a ceramic heat store for compressed air and a sodium-ion box behind a refrigerator each face their own conditions. Some may perform well in their setting, some may reveal problems, and some may find a better fit than the job they were funded for. Funding several physically different approaches gives New York a chance to learn which ideas suit which settings, using results gathered in its own labs and, for the demonstration projects, on its own streets.

A success in one setting will answer only that setting's question. A flow battery that serves chargers well tells engineers about chargers. A substation battery that lasts a full evening peak tells utilities about substations. Each project carries its own scorecard.

## The test ahead

Judging these awards fairly will take specific evidence. For each long-duration claim, the deciding facts are the rated power, the usable energy actually delivered, the conditions of the test and the method used to count the hours. For each demonstration, the deciding facts are its location, its connection to the grid, what electricity charges it, how it performs over months and whether an independent party checks the results. For safety, the deciding facts are qualification methods and incident records. For cost, they include efficiency, wear, upkeep and the baseline used to measure any advantage.

Those measurements are the evidence needed to judge each project. The announcement leaves the timing and form of any public results unstated. Until such evidence appears, the 15 awards stand as 15 open questions. Some concern a job on a particular part of the grid; others concern a material, a prototype or a factory method that could later feed into larger equipment. The iron-sodium battery, the ceramic heat store, the underground flow battery and the portable sodium-ion station each now have an award and a plan. Which of them will hold its charge, keep its promise on safety and deliver its stored energy at a cost New Yorkers can accept when the evening peak arrives?
