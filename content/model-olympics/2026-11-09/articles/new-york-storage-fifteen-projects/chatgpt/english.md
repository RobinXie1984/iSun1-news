# New York Has Funded 15 Storage Projects. Which Will Deliver When Power Is Needed?

Electricity storage connects two moments: when power is available and when someone needs to use it. That timing matters to the wires serving a neighbourhood, the charger filling a vehicle’s battery and the appliances drawing electricity during a busy evening. On November 6, 2026, the New York State Energy Research and Development Authority, or NYSERDA, announced nearly $13 million in awards for 15 storage projects. The public-benefit energy research authority is funding development and demonstration through its Power Generation and Storage Innovation program. The awards establish resources committed; payment progress and operating results remain separate questions.

The bigger problem begins with an electricity system that must continually match supply with demand. People switch appliances on, businesses open and vehicles arrive to charge. Solar and wind output also changes with conditions. Storage can take in energy earlier and return some of it later, giving the system another way to handle those changes. Each charging and discharge cycle consumes energy along the way, so the electricity returned is smaller than the amount supplied.

That trade can still be valuable. Power delivered during a crowded evening may serve a more pressing need than power available several hours earlier. A battery connected near a local bottleneck may help meet demand on the constrained side of that bottleneck. Yet the useful size, operating schedule and cost of that battery depend on the particular job. New York’s awards open several routes toward answering a deceptively simple question: which storage systems can deliver useful, safe and affordable power where the state needs it?

## The tank, the tap and the place they serve

An everyday illustration helps separate two measurements that often appear together. Imagine a water tank with a tap. The tank’s usable volume resembles stored energy. The tap’s flow rate resembles power. A large tank with a narrow tap can supply a modest stream for a long time; a wide tap can empty the same tank quickly.

For electricity, kilowatts, abbreviated kW, and megawatts, MW, measure power: the rate at which energy moves. Kilowatt-hours, kWh, and megawatt-hours, MWh, measure an amount of energy. One megawatt equals 1,000 kilowatts, and one megawatt-hour equals 1,000 kilowatt-hours. Keeping those units separate is essential to understanding what a storage announcement promises.

Consider a purely illustrative system with 100 kWh of usable electrical energy. Delivering a steady 10 kW would exhaust that usable amount in ten hours. Delivering 50 kW would exhaust it in two hours, assuming the equipment supports that output. Those figures describe imaginary equipment and simplify actual operating conditions.

The water analogy also has limits. Electrical storage loses energy during operation, and its connection to the electricity network constrains what it can receive and deliver. A large energy reserve can serve a particular load only if the equipment and connection can supply the required power. Counting households served therefore requires information about their demand, the hours of service and the operating conditions.

“Long-duration” storage concerns how long a system can deliver its rated power, meaning its specified output rate. Other clocks measure other properties: how long energy remains available during standby, how many charging cycles the equipment survives and how many years it stays in service. Each answers a different practical question.

NYSERDA’s broader program describes a 10–100-hour focus. That is a research and development aim. Within the November announcement, new scalable designs target more than ten hours at rated power, while the awards span several kinds of work. The funding comes through the Clean Energy Fund.

Duration becomes meaningful when placed beside a demand pattern. A short, sharp surge calls for substantial power over a brief interval. A long evening of elevated demand calls for more stored energy. A system serving repeated surges also needs opportunities to recharge between them. The operating schedule is part of the engineering problem.

## Different routes to the useful hour

Inlyte Energy received over $2.4 million for an intended more-than-ten-hour iron-sodium deployment serving substation peak-load growth and distribution resilience.

A substation helps move electricity between sections of the network, including by changing voltage for local distribution. Its equipment serves a particular area, so demand growth there creates a local problem. In principle, storage can charge during quieter periods and discharge when local demand reaches its peak. That changes the amount of power the upstream network must supply at that moment.

This application gives the duration target a purpose. If elevated demand stretches across several hours, the battery must keep delivering throughout the relevant period. Its usefulness also depends on the height of the peak. A long-running system with a modest power rating would make a modest contribution to a large surge.

Resilience adds another layer. Supporting service during a disruption depends on the location of the fault, the electrical connections and the controls governing the equipment. Those circumstances determine which loads stored energy can reach. For Inlyte, the award identifies a proposed application; an operating record and measured cost advantage remain unresolved.

Aestus Energy Storage received over $590,000 for an approximately 35 kWh subscale test of two alumina-silica ceramic heat-storage materials, informing a proposed 20 MWh compressed-air system.

Compressed-air storage takes a different physical route. Electricity powers equipment that squeezes air, storing energy through pressure. Later, the air expands and helps drive equipment that generates electricity. Temperature changes accompany that process: compression heats the air, while expansion cools it. Heat management therefore affects how the system works and how much electricity it can return.

The ceramic materials address this thermal part of the process. Their proposed role makes the small test consequential: the behaviour of a material can influence the design of a much larger machine. Testing can help establish how a material receives and releases heat under defined conditions. A complete storage system must then combine that behaviour with compressors, expansion equipment, electrical machinery and controls.

The two energy figures mark distinct stages. The approximately 35 kWh figure describes the subscale material test. The 20 MWh figure, equivalent to 20,000 kWh, describes the intended system design. Scaling from one to the other requires further engineering and validation. Treating the larger figure as demonstrated plant output would collapse those stages into a single claim.

Both figures measure energy. To calculate a delivery duration for the intended system, readers would also need a power rating and a defined amount of usable electrical output. Its measured efficiency remains an open question. The project’s immediate contribution is narrower and concrete: evidence about materials intended to help manage heat in a compressed-air design.

Sprocket Power received over $1 million to demonstrate fast charging supplied by an underground vanadium redox flow battery.

A flow battery holds its active materials in liquids called electrolytes. Pumps move those liquids through a cell, where chemical reactions take in or release electrical energy. In this family of systems, the liquid storage and the equipment delivering power offer distinct design choices. Their sizing still has to work together as a complete installation.

A vehicle-charging site presents a different pattern of demand from a substation. Charging sessions can create substantial bursts of electricity use. Storage could accumulate energy ahead of those sessions and release it as vehicles charge. The question becomes whether the battery, charger and grid connection can work together across the site’s actual sequence of arrivals.

Several closely spaced sessions could draw down the available energy. A quiet interval could offer time to replenish it. The demonstration can therefore examine an operating pattern as well as a chemical process. The underground arrangement also makes installation, access and maintenance part of the practical assessment. The award establishes the intended demonstration; performance at a working charging site remains to be established.

Alsym Energy received over $290,000 to design and test a 10 kWh sodium-ion prototype with Rochester Institute of Technology’s Battery Development Center; NYSERDA describes it as non-flammable.

That safety description deserves its attribution. A material or system characteristic contributes to safety, while the behaviour of assembled equipment under particular conditions requires testing. Electrical faults, temperature control and the way components are packaged remain relevant engineering concerns. The prototype stage offers an opportunity to examine such behaviour before larger-scale use.

Its 10 kWh figure describes an energy amount. A ten-hour delivery claim would additionally require a suitable power rating and usable output under specified conditions. Keeping that distinction visible allows the prototype to be understood on its own terms: a small system intended for design and testing, with safety performance still requiring qualification.

Urban Electric Power received nearly $1.5 million to develop and validate a zinc-manganese dioxide long-duration system with monitoring and control. Those functions connect the battery’s physical behaviour to decisions about when it charges and delivers power. Development and validation will examine that combination.

Standard Potential received $1.8 million to design, prototype and certify a sodium-ion portable power station for appliance-level urban demand response. Demand response means changing when or how much electricity users draw in response to grid needs or incentives. A portable station could supply an appliance from previously stored energy during a period of high demand, shifting that appliance’s draw from the grid to another time. Its contribution would depend on charging schedules, appliance demand and available energy. Certification is part of the planned work.

The portfolio also includes AlphaEn’s recycled-lithium electrode manufacturing work, awarded over $350,000, alongside other lithium-based component improvements.

That breadth matters because a storage system contains several places where an improvement might occur. An electrode is a component through which the battery’s electrical and chemical processes interact. Changing an electrode material or its manufacturing process addresses one part of the cell. Other work changes the complete storage mechanism or the arrangement of a system at a site.

Consequently, a promising component result and a successful grid demonstration carry different meanings. The component must work within a cell and a larger assembly. The demonstration must coordinate equipment with a real load and electrical connection. Both can advance storage, though each supplies evidence at a different level.

The mixture of lithium-based improvements and other approaches also keeps the question open. Different materials may suit different operating conditions, manufacturing processes and uses. The portfolio creates room to explore those possibilities. Its eventual value depends on the results each route produces.

## What would make the awards pay off?

Across these projects, the first meaningful comparison is between a system’s intended job and its measured behaviour. For a substation application, that means following the local demand peak and seeing how much support arrives throughout it. For a charging site, it means tracking successive charging sessions and the battery’s ability to replenish its energy. For a material test, it means assessing the property that the larger design depends upon.

Energy losses belong in that assessment. Engineers often describe “round-trip efficiency”: the share of charging energy that comes back as useful electrical output. A system returning a smaller share requires more charging energy to deliver the same later output. That affects operating cost and the demands placed on the electricity supply.

Repeated use matters as well. Usable capacity may change as equipment ages, and maintenance can interrupt availability. A single successful discharge establishes one event. Repeated operation under clearly described conditions begins to establish a service that an electricity customer or network operator could rely upon.

Affordability reaches beyond the price of the storage hardware. Installation, grid connection, charging electricity, maintenance and eventual replacement all contribute to the cost of providing useful power. Their importance varies by site and technology. An equipment price becomes informative when connected to the amount and quality of service delivered over time.

The same discipline applies to emissions. The source and timing of charging electricity influence the consequences of a storage cycle, alongside energy losses and the electricity supply displaced during discharge. A funding award alone leaves those operating relationships unresolved. Claims about lower bills or cleaner electricity require evidence from the relevant use.

NYSERDA’s announcement supplies a map of projects and intended work. Its program page supplies broader context from the same authority. The U.S. Energy Information Administration explains the underlying storage principles. Together, they support an account of what is being attempted and why the mechanisms matter. Comparable project-level performance and cost results remain the next evidentiary stage.

New York has committed public resources to several ways of moving energy across time. Some work begins with a material; some with a prototype; some aims at an application in the electricity network. The suspense lies in the distance between those starting points and dependable service. When demand rises at a particular place and hour, which of these systems will have the energy, power and operating record to answer it?
