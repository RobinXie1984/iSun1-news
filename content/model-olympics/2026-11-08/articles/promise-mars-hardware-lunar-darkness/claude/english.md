# A Mars Rover's Earthbound Twin Is Headed for the Moon. What Will It Find in the Dark?

For years, a near copy of NASA's Curiosity Mars rover has stayed at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California. While the real Curiosity explored Mars, engineers used this twin on the ground as a test bed, a stand-in vehicle for trying out the mission's hardware and systems. On November 7, 2026, NASA announced that the twin will become the core of a new machine bound for the Moon. The project is called PROMISE, short for Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration. JPL, a division of Caltech, will manage and build it. NASA describes PROMISE as a six-wheeled, SUV-size scout for the region around the Moon's South Pole, where the agency hopes to establish an outpost for astronauts. NASA is targeting a launch in late 2028 and plans an 18-month primary mission.

The project draws on two long-running lines of NASA work. The first is the Mars rover program, which has given JPL more than three decades of experience building and driving robots on another world. The second is the agency's effort to send people back to the Moon and have astronauts live, work and explore near the lunar South Pole. NASA calls that larger effort the Moon Base. PROMISE belongs to its first phase, a rapid series of robotic missions meant to scout the ground, run experiments and prepare for astronauts. JPL manages PROMISE on behalf of NASA's Human Spaceflight Mission Directorate, the part of the agency in charge of crewed exploration. The rover's science instruments and technology demonstrations are sponsored by three NASA mission directorates: Science, Research and Technology, and Human Spaceflight.

The November 7 announcement marks a documented milestone in a story with earlier chapters. The test vehicle and spare parts already existed, and the delivery deal expands an earlier agreement from May 2026. In November, NASA announced a decision to move forward with developing the rover and an award to deliver it. The launch, landing, drives and measurements all lie ahead. The suspense of the mission sits in the distance between a clever engineering plan and the hard local evidence a lunar base will eventually need.

## From a Mars stand-in to a Moon scout

The premise is easy to misread. Curiosity and Perseverance, the two NASA rovers now working on Mars, will stay on Mars. PROMISE uses hardware that has remained on Earth the whole time. According to NASA, JPL engineers are rebuilding the electronics, body structure and mobility system of Curiosity's duplicate test-bed vehicle. The mobility system is the set of wheels, suspension and drive parts that moves the rover. The engineers plan to add more electronics, together with flight-spare and test-bed parts originally made for the Curiosity and Perseverance missions. In NASA's description, these spares and test-bed parts are extra hardware produced for those two Mars missions, now set aside for a second life on the Moon.

An everyday comparison can help explain the logic. It is only an illustration. Picture a delivery company that keeps a duplicate of its long-haul truck in the workshop, where mechanics test repairs before trying them on the road. One day the company converts that workshop truck into a survey vehicle for a much colder, darker region. The existing frame, steering and dashboard electronics give the team a head start. The new climate still calls for suitable equipment and fresh testing. Once the truck arrives, the crew still has to measure the roads themselves. The comparison covers the engineering shortcut. Mars and the Moon remain very different places, and the Moon will test these Mars-bred parts in its own ways.

NASA's case for the shortcut rests on time and money. The agency says that relying on proven hardware, software and Mars operating methods lowers cost and shortens integration and testing. Integration is the stage when separate components are joined into one working machine and checked together. NASA also says it assessed whether the chosen hardware suits the lunar environment and concluded that it can tolerate the harsh South Pole conditions. These cost and compatibility conclusions are NASA's own engineering judgments, and the mission itself will put them to the test on the Moon. The mission page describes the rebuilding and integration as work still to come.

This creates the central tension of the project. Heritage hardware spares engineers part of the job of designing from scratch. The Moon, however, will be a new working environment for this particular vehicle. Its performance there will become clear only after it arrives.

## The ride and the bill

PROMISE will reach the Moon aboard a commercial lander. NASA awarded Blue Origin a $94 million base contract to deliver the rover. The award also includes an optional additional $140 million tied to performance. The deal expands a task order NASA gave Blue Origin in May 2026 to deliver two lunar terrain vehicles. Blue Origin plans to land and deploy PROMISE using its Mark 1 lander.

These figures describe a delivery service. The $94 million pays for getting the rover to the surface, and the cost of building PROMISE is a separate sum. The $140 million is a conditional option whose payment depends on future performance. The business relationships also overlap: Honeybee Robotics, which designs and leads the rover's drill, is a subsidiary of Blue Origin.

## Light, shadow and a nuclear heat source

To understand why NASA chose this destination and this power source, it helps to picture the lunar South Pole. Near the pole, the Sun always stays low on the horizon, much as a late-afternoon sun throws long shadows across a parking lot. Lunar hills and crater rims cast shadows that stretch great distances. NASA describes the region as a patchwork. Some high ground receives sunlight for extended periods, while the floors of some craters and depressions stay permanently dark. Scientists call these places permanently shadowed regions.

Permanent shadow differs from an ordinary lunar night. Every place on the Moon has nights, when its side of the Moon faces away from the Sun. A permanently shadowed crater floor stays dark even during local daytime because the surrounding terrain blocks the low Sun. NASA's mission page uses Shackleton Crater as an example. Near the South Pole, the crater measures about 21 kilometres across and 4 kilometres deep. Its shadowed interior may contain water ice and other frozen materials.

Those cold, dark pockets are the reason for the trip. NASA notes that earlier missions found evidence of water ice and other frozen material in some South Pole regions. The material appears in a complex pattern, with concentrations that vary from place to place. Scientists think some of the coldest shadowed areas act as cold traps, where volatiles may stay frozen for very long periods. Volatiles are substances, such as water, that easily turn into gas when warmed. NASA counts ice among the resources it considers essential for sustainable exploration.

Darkness creates problems for a rover that depends on sunlight. Solar panels need light, and electronics need warmth. PROMISE will instead carry a flight-spare multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator, or MMRTG, which NASA describes as essentially a nuclear generator. It contains plutonium-238, a radioactive material that naturally decays and gives off heat as it does so. The generator turns part of that heat into electricity using thermoelectric materials, which produce an electric current when one side is hot and the other is cold. The remaining heat helps keep the rover's systems warm. Because the heat comes from steady natural decay, this power source is separate from the chain reaction inside a nuclear reactor. It also keeps working in darkness. NASA's background material says MMRTGs already provide power and heat for Curiosity and Perseverance on Mars. For PROMISE, the generator will be installed in a specialized facility after the rover reaches the launch site.

NASA intends the generator to keep PROMISE operating through lunar nights and inside permanently shadowed regions. Electrical power is one requirement for working in darkness. Seeing the ground, steering safely and coping with dust are separate challenges.

## Seeing and steering in the dark

A rover deep in shadow needs a way to see where it is going. PROMISE will carry Lunar Dashcam, a new JPL camera suite mounted on the rover's mast. It is designed to take continuous stereo panoramas while the rover drives. Stereo images are pairs of pictures taken from slightly different angles, much like the views from two human eyes, and they allow depth to be measured. The images will be sent to Earth and turned into three-dimensional terrain maps. Lights mounted on the rover will illuminate dark areas. The system is based on the camera system that recorded Perseverance's descent and landing on Mars.

PROMISE will also test three linked technologies that NASA hopes will let it drive farther inside permanently shadowed regions. SCAN-3D, led by NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center, uses a commercial lidar. A lidar sends out laser light, detects the light that bounces back from nearby rocks and slopes, and uses those returns to build a real-time three-dimensional map. HPSC, short for High Performance Spaceflight Computing, is NASA's next-generation computer designed to tolerate radiation. A commercial graphics processor will help it process data quickly. HI-RATE, led by NASA Ames with support from JPL and NASA's Johnson Space Center, is navigation software inspired by self-driving cars. It will combine lidar and mobility-sensor data so the rover can make more driving decisions by itself and need fewer instructions from operators on Earth.

These are planned technology demonstrations. They will show how far autonomous lunar driving can go once the rover is working on the Moon. A smaller device on the rover will help with the more basic question of location. The Laser Retroreflector Array, a passive reflector about 5 centimetres across led by NASA Goddard, will send laser light from an orbiting spacecraft straight back to its source. That will allow the rover's position to be measured to within centimetres. Reflectors of this kind have flown since Apollo 11.

## Following the clue downward

The science instruments are designed to work in a sequence, with each step narrowing the uncertainty left by the previous one.

The first step is a clue. The Neutron Spectrometer System, or NSS, built by Lockheed Martin and led by NASA Ames, will measure how much hydrogen lies in the ground beneath the rover. Hydrogen is part of every water molecule, so a hydrogen-rich area marks a promising place to look for buried water. Hydrogen can also occur in other chemical forms. A strong signal therefore shows where to investigate next and leaves the presence of water to be confirmed.

The second step is digging. TRIDENT, a rotary-percussive drill that spins and hammers at the same time, will bring up regolith, the Moon's fine dust and broken rock. It can reach as deep as about 1 metre and works in 10-centimetre increments, placing each batch on the surface for examination. As it drills, it will also measure how strong and tightly packed the material is and how cold it is.

The third step is identification. MSOLO, a commercial mass spectrometer adapted for lunar conditions and led by NASA's Kennedy Space Center, will analyze the chemical makeup of volatiles released from the material TRIDENT brings up. NIRVSS, an instrument led by NASA Ames that studies near-infrared light, is designed to find out which ices, if any, are present and to identify minerals at the surface. LAFORGE, an infrared radiometer led by the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory, will search for cold traps and help determine the composition of the regolith. Its sensors draw on technology from an instrument aboard NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. NASA's mission page says temperatures in permanently shadowed regions may fall as low as −242°C. That figure describes a possible extreme in those regions; PROMISE's own temperature readings lie ahead.

Together, these readings can build a layered profile of a site: where hydrogen points, what lies at each depth, which compounds escape as gas, and how cold and firm the ground is. The drill is a prospecting tool for testing small sites, and its measurements will describe the composition of the spots the rover actually samples. Estimating how much ice a wider deposit holds, and testing whether it can be extracted, belong to later stages of work. NASA adds one wider connection. TRIDENT, MSOLO, NIRVSS and NSS are also part of NASA's VIPER rover mission, so the two rovers are expected to collect comparable data from two different areas of the South Pole.

## The ground itself as evidence

The rover will also learn from its own wheels. NASA plans to study engineering data from the wheels' contact with the surface, including inside permanently shadowed regions, to learn whether the regolith is firm, loose or uneven. The mission page gives one possible example: PROMISE might use a wheel to dig a trench and record what happens. That information could guide future landing-site choices and the design of later vehicles and infrastructure.

NASA's description of the South Pole explains why this matters. The terrain around craters is rugged, and lunar dust can disrupt navigation and wear down seals, mechanisms, optics, radiators and solar surfaces. The landing, the climb or descent into shadowed terrain, the wear on moving parts, and the rover's ability to control its temperature will all be real tests for reused hardware in a new setting.

A final instrument looks ahead to human visitors. The Radiation Assessment Detector, or RAD, led by Southwest Research Institute, will measure high-energy particles from the Sun and deep space that could harm astronauts. An earlier version of RAD has operated aboard Curiosity for 15 years, linking the Mars program to PROMISE once again.

PROMISE's assigned role is that of a scout, sent ahead of the Moon Base itself. NASA's plan gives it the job of testing the ground, looking for usable resources and helping planners make better decisions before crews arrive. If the rover lands and works as planned, it could help identify the most promising places for future missions. Its information will cover the specific locations it actually drives through, drills and measures. A workshop vehicle and a set of Mars spares are being prepared for one of the darkest and coldest places astronauts may someday visit. When PROMISE reaches the edge of a shadowed crater, will its reused hardware hold up, and will it send back measurements precise enough to show where ice and solid ground actually lie?
