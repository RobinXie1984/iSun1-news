# NASA Will Build a Moon Scout From Mars Spares. What Will It Find in the Shadows?

NASA plans to turn an Earth-based duplicate of its Curiosity Mars rover, together with Mars-mission spare parts, into a scout for the Moon’s South Pole. Announced on November 7, 2026, the project would send instruments into a region where deep shadows may preserve water ice and other frozen material. For future explorers, the central challenge is learning where those materials lie, what surrounds them and whether a vehicle can reach them.

The planned six-wheeled rover is called PROMISE, short for Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration. The last phrase means investigating material where it occurs. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, a division of Caltech, will manage and build the vehicle. Its intended job is to bring measurements from particular patches of lunar ground into the larger discussion about establishing a human outpost near the pole.

That ground presents an unusual combination of opportunity and difficulty. Near the lunar South Pole, the Sun stays low in the sky. Hills and crater rims cast long shadows, while some elevated areas receive extended sunlight. Certain depressions remain permanently shadowed: their shape keeps direct sunlight from reaching the interior throughout the relevant lunar cycles. An ordinary lunar night passes as illumination changes. A permanent shadow is a persistent feature of the terrain.

NASA describes evidence from previous missions for water ice and other frozen materials in some polar regions. Their distribution is complex, and concentrations vary. This gives scientists a reason to investigate particular places while leaving their practical usefulness open. A regional indication of ice can guide exploration; choosing a site for future operations requires much finer knowledge of depth, composition, temperature and access.

An everyday analogy helps explain the gap. Imagine a map suggesting groundwater beneath a neighborhood. Test wells could establish which locations contain water, how deep it lies and what layers surround it. PROMISE would perform a comparable kind of local investigation, using a shallow drill and several instruments. The lunar case concerns possible frozen material in difficult terrain, and the rover’s drill is a prospecting tool. Its purpose is to gather evidence about a resource.

NASA is targeting a late 2028 launch and an 18-month primary mission. Both remain planning figures. The milestone reached in November 2026 is the decision to proceed with development and delivery arrangements; launch, landing, driving and lunar measurements lie ahead. The mission descriptions leave the precise landing location and route open, so the character of the ground PROMISE would actually examine remains an important uncertainty.

The transportation agreement gives that plan a concrete commercial element. NASA awarded Blue Origin a $94 million base contract for PROMISE’s delivery, with an optional additional $140 million tied to performance. The award expands a May 2026 task order for two lunar terrain vehicles. Blue Origin plans to carry and deploy PROMISE using its Mark 1 lander. The $94 million figure covers the delivery contract’s base; rover construction and the full mission budget are separate accounting questions. The additional payment remains conditional.

The vehicle itself would begin with hardware already on Earth. Curiosity’s duplicate vehicle-system test bed—a working engineering counterpart used to support the Mars mission—would supply electronics, body structure and the mobility system. Engineers would combine these with flight-spare and engineering or test components developed for Curiosity and Perseverance. The connection to Mars therefore runs through terrestrial equipment, designs and operating experience.

NASA says this reuse can lower cost and shorten the work of assembling components and testing them together. The agency also says it assessed compatibility with the lunar environment and determined that the hardware could tolerate harsh South Pole conditions. Those are NASA’s engineering assessments. The announcement provides a rationale for proceeding, while measured lunar reliability will depend on the completed vehicle’s performance. Quantified savings and a total rover construction cost remain unspecified in the cited material.

The engineering attraction is understandable. A sophisticated test vehicle offers a starting structure, and established software and operating methods give a team experience to draw upon. Each reused component still has to work within its new combination of equipment and conditions. For PROMISE, the practical test of that inheritance would be whether the assembled rover can carry its instruments through polar terrain and keep them functioning long enough to produce useful observations.

Its proposed power source is especially relevant to the destination. NASA’s mission page specifies a flight-spare multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator, usually shortened to MMRTG. The device converts heat from the natural radioactive decay of plutonium-238 into electricity. It also supplies heat. This is a nuclear power technology based on decay heat; a fission reactor uses a controlled chain reaction.

Because the heat source operates independently of sunlight, an MMRTG offers a way to power a rover during lunar nights and inside persistent shadows. NASA’s general account of the technology describes its use for power and heat on Curiosity and Perseverance. PROMISE’s own electrical output and operating lifetime remain to be established in mission-specific terms. Its planned 18-month primary mission describes the intended period of work.

Electricity is one part of making the shadowed ground accessible. The vehicle would also face rough terrain, difficult visibility, dust and demanding thermal conditions. Lunar dust can affect seals, moving mechanisms, camera optics and surfaces that release heat. Landing and deployment must place the rover where it can begin useful travel. The power system addresses a major obstacle, while the rest of the vehicle must handle the physical environment around it.

Once on suitable ground, PROMISE’s scientific strength would come from connecting several kinds of measurement. A reading suggesting hydrogen becomes more informative when placed beside a sample’s chemistry, its depth and the temperature of its surroundings. Images and driving data would add another layer: the condition of the ground through which a future machine might have to travel. Together, these observations could describe both the material and the effort required to approach it.

The planned TRIDENT drill would begin that investigation beneath the surface. Led by Honeybee Robotics, it uses rotation and repeated hammering to work into regolith, the loose dust and rocky material covering the Moon. Its intended reach is about 1 metre, with material brought up in 10-centimetre increments and deposited on the surface for analysis. That stepped approach would allow measurements to be associated with progressively deeper material.

TRIDENT would also measure strength, compactness and temperature. These physical properties matter alongside chemistry. A patch containing interesting frozen material could present a very different engineering task depending on how firmly the surrounding grains are packed. Sampling at several depths could help scientists understand changes beneath the visible surface. The planned drill reach defines the shallow layer PROMISE could investigate; deeper conditions would remain beyond those samples.

The MSOLO mass spectrometer would examine the chemical makeup of volatile substances released from material raised by the drill. Volatiles are substances that can readily turn into vapor under the relevant conditions. A mass spectrometer helps identify chemical constituents by measuring particles according to their mass and electrical charge. In this mission, its role would be to help determine what the sampled ground releases.

A second instrument, NIRVSS, would examine near-infrared light, which lies just beyond the red end of human vision. Different materials interact with light in characteristic ways. NIRVSS is designed to assess the presence and identity of ice and to characterize surface minerals. Its findings would offer a complementary view of the material, linking possible frozen substances with the mineral ground around them.

The NSS instrument would measure hydrogen abundance to help locate possible subsurface water. Water contains hydrogen, making that element a useful clue. Turning a hydrogen reading into a judgment about accessible water requires additional evidence about composition, location and physical conditions. The instruments therefore serve different purposes within the same investigation: finding promising signals, examining material and describing the setting in which it occurs.

Temperature observations would help explain why frozen material might persist in a particular place. Scientists use the term cold trap for a location whose conditions can retain volatile substances as ice. Mapping those conditions alongside chemical evidence could make the pattern of a deposit easier to interpret. Radiation sensors would add measurements relevant to the environment future astronauts could encounter. These readings would extend the rover’s value beyond a simple inventory of possible ice.

Every observation would also need a place on the map. NASA plans a Lunar Dashcam camera suite that would collect continuous stereo panoramas during drives. Stereo images view terrain from different positions, helping reveal depth, much as human eyes do. Data sent to Earth would support three-dimensional terrain maps. Rover-mounted illuminators are intended to light dark areas, and the camera design draws on the system that recorded Perseverance’s arrival at Mars.

For movement, PROMISE would carry a separate set of technology demonstrations. SCAN-3D would use lidar, a system that sends out laser light and measures its return, to build a three-dimensional picture of nearby terrain. This gives the rover a way to measure the shape of its surroundings. Boulders, slopes and uneven surfaces would then become information that navigation software could use.

The planned High Performance Spaceflight Computing system would process these data, assisted by a commercial graphics processor. NASA describes the computing system as radiation-tolerant, meaning it is designed to function amid radiation that can disrupt electronics. HI-RATE navigation software would combine lidar and mobility data to support driving with less intervention from operators. These remain proposed demonstrations; their practical contribution would emerge through testing and eventual lunar operation.

The science and navigation tasks are closely connected. A rover’s instruments can examine only the places it reaches, and each traverse would expose it to a particular sample of the landscape. If the vehicle could cross several kinds of terrain, its observations could reveal how conditions vary between them. A successful reading at one location would still describe that location; the broader map would gain strength through additional measurements tied to identifiable places.

Even the wheels could supply evidence. NASA proposes using their interaction with regolith to assess whether the ground is firm, loose or uneven. A wheel-dug trench appears in the mission description as one possible investigation. Such an activity could reveal how material responds to disturbance, while routine driving could provide engineering observations about the surface. These are future investigations whose usefulness would depend on the terrain encountered and the quality of the recorded data.

This is where the rover’s resource search meets the practical needs of a prospective Moon Base. A chemical result could identify material worth studying. A temperature record could help explain its persistence. Depth measurements could describe where it lies, and wheel data could reveal the ground a later vehicle would have to cross. In combination, those findings could inform choices about sites, vehicles and infrastructure. NASA places PROMISE within an initial phase of robotic scouting for that wider ambition.

An actionable map would therefore carry several kinds of meaning at once. It would connect possible resources to their setting and show how conditions change along the rover’s path. Even a positive ice finding would open further questions about quantity, distribution and the work required to recover it. PROMISE’s planned instruments would contribute local evidence to those questions; industrial extraction and a dependable water supply would require further development and proof.

The November 7 decision establishes a path toward collecting that evidence, using hardware whose history began with Mars exploration. The eventual value would be measured in what the rover could reach, observe and explain on the Moon. Could this rebuilt test vehicle cross enough contrasting polar ground to turn a regional possibility into a map that future explorers can use?
