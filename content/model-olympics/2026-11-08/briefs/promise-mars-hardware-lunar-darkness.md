# PROMISE: a future lunar mission built from existing rover hardware

Public source review, November 8, 2026. NASA’s November 7 announcement advances PROMISE, the Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration. The six-wheeled vehicle is intended to scout the lunar South Pole, characterise surface and subsurface material and inform future landing and infrastructure choices. Its launch, landing and measurements are future events.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, a division of Caltech, will build and manage the rover. The planned vehicle reuses components from the Earth-based duplicate Curiosity test bed, plus spare and test parts developed for Curiosity and Perseverance. The operating Mars rovers themselves are not being brought back from Mars. A flight-spare multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator is planned to provide electricity and heat using energy from natural radioactive decay. This is a different technology from a chain-reaction reactor or a sunlight-dependent solar array.

The South Pole offers a demanding mix of rugged terrain, extended sunlight in some places and permanent shadow in others. Prior NASA missions have found evidence of frozen material in some shadowed regions; distribution and concentration vary. A power source able to support operations in darkness may help prospecting, while landing, dust, terrain, thermal management and system integration remain engineering tasks.

The planned payload includes a drill reaching about one metre below the surface, chemical spectrometers, cameras, temperature and radiation sensors. TRIDENT would bring up material for analysis; other instruments would examine volatile chemistry, surface composition and hydrogen signals. Hydrogen detection, local ice characterisation and proof of commercially useful extraction are different evidence steps. Stereo camera data, lidar, radiation-tolerant computing and autonomy software are planned capabilities or demonstrations, not completed lunar driving results.

NASA targets a late-2028 launch and an 18-month primary mission. The agency announced a $94 million base delivery award to Blue Origin, with an additional $140 million option dependent on performance. These are delivery-contract terms, not a complete rover price, a final total mission cost or cash already paid. NASA attributes reduced cost and shorter integration to reuse, but the inspected sources provide no numerical saving or independent qualification report.
