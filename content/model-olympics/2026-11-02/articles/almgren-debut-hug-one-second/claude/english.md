One Mile Left: The Moment a Marathon Newcomer Took New York

A marathon is a long road race of 42.195 kilometres, or about 26.2 miles. That equals a little over 105 laps of a standard 400-metre running track, all run in one go.

Every autumn, New York Road Runners (NYRR), a nonprofit organization that puts on races, holds the New York City Marathon. The race began in 1970. In 1976 it moved to a course that runs through all five of the city’s boroughs: Staten Island, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Manhattan. The 2026 race, held on Sunday, November 1, marked 50 years of that five-borough route.

NYRR describes the event as drawing nearly 60,000 athletes. That figure is the organizer’s approximate count of participants. People who sign up, people who start and people who finish are three separate counts, and NYRR said it would release official finisher numbers on November 2. When the material for this article was collected on November 2, the organizer’s report still carried that note, so the final finisher total remains open.

Race day holds several separate contests. Elite runners compete in the open divisions for men and women. Wheelchair athletes race in their own divisions, with racing chairs, classification rules and tactics of their own. Each division crowns its own champions, and each finishing time belongs with the others from its own division.

The men’s open race produced the day’s most striking story. Andreas Almgren of Sweden won it in his first race at the marathon distance. “First marathon” here describes a new distance for an accomplished athlete. NYRR identifies Almgren as the 2026 World Athletics Half Marathon champion and the European champion at 10,000 metres. A half marathon is about 21.1 kilometres, or 13.1 miles; 10,000 metres is 10 kilometres, about 6.2 miles. Almgren came to New York as a proven champion at shorter distances, stepping up to a race twice the length of his half-marathon title race.

An everyday comparison helps explain why that step is so demanding. Think of a phone battery. A shorter race resembles an afternoon out: you can keep the screen bright and still get home with charge to spare. A marathon resembles a whole day away from a charger. You manage the brightness carefully all day so the battery still has power in the evening, when you need it most. This is an analogy for pacing; the race itself is what follows.

According to NYRR, Almgren stayed with the lead pack, the group of front runners moving together, through 25 miles. With one mile remaining, he made the move that decided the race. In the terms of the analogy, he still had charge left for the evening. NYRR calls him the first man since Gebre Gebremariam in 2010 to win the New York open division in his debut at the distance, and the first Swede to win any division of the race. The organizer’s race report, the main source here, leaves out his exact finishing time, so this article leaves it out as well.

Behind him, Yeman Crippa of Italy finished second and Kamohelo Mofolo of Lesotho third. Mofolo turned 21 on race day and became the first athlete from his country to reach this race’s podium.

In the women’s open division, Sheila Chepkirui of Kenya won in 2 hours, 24 minutes and 36 seconds, her second New York title after her 2024 victory. American Jess McClain took second place ahead of Hellen Obiri; the organizer’s report gives the order of that finish.

The wheelchair races delivered a different kind of drama. Marcel Hug of Switzerland won the men’s wheelchair division in 1:28:14, one second ahead of American Daniel Romanchuk, according to NYRR. Across 42 kilometres, one second is roughly the time it takes to say the word “marathon” twice. The victory gave Hug his eighth men’s wheelchair title in New York, extending his record in this event. His countrywoman Catherine Debrunner won the women’s wheelchair division in 1:40:02, nearly eight and a half minutes ahead of Tatyana McFadden.

These results come from the organizer’s own records, published November 1. They describe one race, and Almgren’s win belongs to an elite athlete with major titles at shorter distances, which makes it one well-documented case of a champion moving up in distance. It also sets up a question that runners and coaches return to often. For an athlete who is already among the fastest in the world over 10 kilometres and the half marathon, how much of the marathon is a test of speed, and how much is a test of patience: the judgment to hold that speed in reserve for 25 miles and spend it in the last one?
