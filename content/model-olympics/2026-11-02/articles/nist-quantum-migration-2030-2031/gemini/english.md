How Do We Lock Up Secrets When the Keys Are Changing?

The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology builds the rulers and scales that help the modern world agree. The agency develops measurement methods and technical standards that guide engineering and security. In digital communication, a central standard acts like a secure envelope system. Digital signatures help prove a message truly came from its sender, and key establishment lets two parties create a shared secret code before exchanging sensitive information.

Imagine two people sending locked boxes to each other using padlocks. Currently, mathematicians have designed very strong padlocks that are exceptionally difficult for today’s standard computers to pick. A completely different kind of machine, a quantum computer, uses the peculiar rules of physics at the atomic level. Engineers are working to build sufficiently powerful quantum computers that could pick these current mathematical locks with ease. Post-quantum cryptography involves new mathematical padlocks designed to resist even those future quantum machines. These new padlocks still run on conventional computers; they represent new math, separate from the complex physical hardware of quantum communication.

In August 2024, NIST released its first completed post-quantum security standards. The organization has now finalized the map that tells users when and how to switch their padlocks. On October 27, 2026, researchers Dustin Moody, Ray Perlner, Andrew Regenscheid, Angela Robinson, and David Cooper published the final transition report, NISTIR 8547.

The report names the specific current signature and key-exchange standards that face vulnerabilities from future quantum computers, alongside their new, resistant replacements. NIST outlines broad targets for U.S. federal agencies to complete their migration. Under National Security Memorandum 10, the general federal migration goal is 2035. An accelerated schedule in Executive Order 14412 moves that target earlier for high-priority federal assets and high-impact systems, aiming for 2030 for key establishment and 2031 for digital signatures. Companies working as suppliers for federal agencies might fall under similar rules, varying with specific agency requirements.

The transition schedule touches on a specific risk mechanism called "harvest now, decrypt later." Adversaries could copy encrypted information traveling across the internet today and store those digital boxes in a warehouse. Once a powerful quantum computer becomes available in the future, they could use it to unlock the old secrets.

Because of this specific risk, NIST points out that highly sensitive information requiring a long lifespan of confidentiality may make migrating key-establishment systems—specifically the FIPS 203 standard—an immediate priority. The transition remains a complex engineering challenge. An organization must identify every place a padlock is used within its software, supply chains, and external dependencies. NIST's migration demonstration center works with cross-sector partners to test tools and build cryptographic visibility to manage this transition.

To help organizations maintain security during the changeover, NIST accommodates hybrid approaches. An organization can lock its box twice, applying both a conventional padlock and a new post-quantum padlock. This dual method requires extra computational effort but ensures the information remains protected by current methods while organizations test the new mathematical designs.

The publication of this finalized roadmap clarifies the specific standards slated for retirement and replacement. The schedule sets distinct targets for federal migration over the coming decade. As institutions begin to assess their sprawling software supply chains and identify the lifespan of their hidden data, this planning phase presents a distinct challenge. How do organizations protect secrets meant to stay quiet for decades while the fundamental infrastructure of secrecy undergoes a complete renovation?
