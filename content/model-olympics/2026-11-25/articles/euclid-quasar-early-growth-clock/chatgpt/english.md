# A Giant Black Hole Was Already There at 630 Million Years. What Gave It a Head Start?

Astronomers have identified a luminous quasar from a time when the universe was about 630 million years old, bringing an unusually large black hole into view near the beginning of cosmic history. A quasar is the brilliant centre of a galaxy, powered by gas flowing towards a central black hole. This one, called J1504+6252 and nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, emerged from observations by the European Space Agency’s Euclid telescope. Follow-up observations allowed a team led by Julien Wolf, working with Silvia Belladitta and collaborators, to estimate the black hole’s mass at roughly 360 million times the mass of our Sun.

That ancient light carries a snapshot of a young universe. During the preceding hundreds of millions of years, the first stars and galaxies had begun assembling, creating the environments in which black holes could form and grow. Dún Aonghasa places a demanding question within that larger story: how did so much mass gather into one black hole so early?

The discovery’s record has a precise scope. As of the reports published on 24 November 2026, Dún Aonghasa was the most distant known luminous quasar. ESA described its light as coming from approximately 40 million years earlier in cosmic history than the previous quasar record. That figure measures an advance in the epoch astronomers can examine. The black hole’s own age depends on when its first, smaller ancestor formed.

Even the 630-million-year cosmic age gives only an upper bound on the time available for growth. The starting black hole formed sometime after the Big Bang. Its actual growth interval was therefore shorter. The team’s measurements add another intriguing detail: at the moment preserved in its light, the quasar was shining at a relatively modest fraction of a standard luminosity scale for a black hole of its estimated mass.

A large early mass, a limited calendar and a comparatively subdued observed phase now have to fit into the same physical history.

Euclid supplied the first step towards establishing that history. A wide survey can examine extensive areas of sky and pick out rare sources through their brightness in different wavelength bands. The relative brightness across those bands gives each source a set of colours, including infrared colours beyond human vision. These can flag objects whose light has travelled from very early cosmic epochs.

Dún Aonghasa first entered the investigation as such a candidate. Its full survey designation, EUCL J150452.291+625224.39, records its position on the sky; J1504+6252 is the shorter working name. The team then used follow-up observations involving Keck, the Hubble Space Telescope, the Large Binocular Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope to establish its distance and investigate its physical properties.

The distinction between selection and confirmation matters. Colours help astronomers decide where to look more closely. A spectrum supplies a more detailed test by separating light into its constituent wavelengths. Atoms and gas produce recognizable features in that spectrum. As the universe expands, travelling light stretches, shifting those features towards longer wavelengths.

Astronomers describe this stretching through a quantity called redshift. The team’s author manuscript gives Dún Aonghasa a redshift of 8.129, with a quoted uncertainty of 0.005, usually rounded to 8.13. At that redshift, observed wavelengths are about 9.13 times their emitted lengths. Matching recognizable spectral features to their stretched positions establishes the redshift; a cosmological model then relates it to the universe’s age when the light began its journey.

This is how a survey candidate becomes evidence about the early universe. Euclid’s broad search and the follow-up telescopes perform complementary jobs: finding a rare source across a large area, then extracting enough information from its light to place it in cosmic time.

The mass estimate requires a further layer of interpretation. A black hole has an event horizon, the boundary beyond which light remains trapped. The quasar’s visible energy comes from material outside that boundary. Gas releases energy as it moves inward, producing the extraordinary brightness that allows astronomers to study such a distant source.

Accretion is the name for this inward flow of matter. It both powers the quasar and provides a way for the black hole to gain mass. The light therefore carries clues about the engine producing it, even though the event horizon itself remains unresolved in these observations.

Some of those clues appear in the widths of emission lines, bright features produced by gas. Gas moving at different speeds spreads a feature across a range of wavelengths. Astronomers combine those widths with luminosity measurements and established calibrations to infer the central mass. The calculation connects observable light to the gravitational influence of the black hole.

Wolf and colleagues obtain a reference estimate of approximately 360 million solar masses. One solar mass means the mass of our Sun, so the figure describes the amount of matter, rather than the object’s diameter. Different calibrations in the manuscript yield estimates spanning roughly 210 million to 780 million solar masses. The reference estimate also carries substantial uncertainty.

Those values describe alternative estimates for the same object. Their spread matters when researchers calculate which growth histories could reach the inferred mass. Across that calibration range, however, the underlying challenge remains substantial: a black hole containing hundreds of millions of solar masses was already present during the universe’s first 630 million years.

To understand the challenge, begin with the proposed starting point. Astronomers call an initial black hole a seed. One possible route starts with the death of an early massive star, leaving a relatively small black hole behind. Another proposed route involves the direct collapse of a large gas cloud, producing a much heavier seed. Each supplies a different opening condition for subsequent growth.

A savings account provides a useful illustration. Imagine a balance that must reach a particular target by a fixed deadline. A larger opening deposit requires less growth along the way. A smaller deposit requires stronger growth, more sustained growth, or some combination of the two. For Dún Aonghasa, the seed mass is the opening deposit, and the cosmic age recorded in its light supplies the deadline.

The analogy has a physical limit. A black hole’s supply of gas depends on its surroundings, and its feeding can slow, accelerate or pause. Different flows also release different amounts of light for the mass they deliver. Any financial rate of return belongs solely to the illustration; the astronomical problem involves gravity, gas and radiation changing together.

That is why the size of the seed and the feeding history have to be considered jointly. A heavy seed gives the system a head start. A lighter seed demands conditions that sustain enough growth over the available interval. The observed quasar provides an endpoint and a glimpse of activity at that endpoint. Its earlier feeding history remains open to reconstruction through models.

The activity measurement makes that reconstruction especially interesting. The team estimates an Eddington ratio between roughly 0.12 and 0.44, depending on the mass calibration, with a reference value near 0.26.

The Eddington ratio compares the quasar’s luminosity with a theoretical luminosity scale tied to the black hole’s mass. In the idealized picture behind that scale, gravity pulls gas inward while radiation pushes outward. The Eddington luminosity marks a balance between those opposing effects under the assumed conditions.

A ratio of 0.26 therefore means that the inferred luminosity is about 26 per cent of that reference scale. It describes the brightness at the ancient epoch being observed. The share of the black hole’s life spent feeding is a separate quantity, as is the amount of mass acquired during any particular episode.

Light also gives an indirect view of how much gas reaches the black hole. The conversion depends on radiative efficiency: how much of the incoming material’s available mass-energy emerges as radiation. The shape of the flow and the way radiation moves through it also affect the relationship. The Eddington scale is consequently a useful physical reference whose application depends on the conditions around the black hole.

Dún Aonghasa’s comparatively modest observed ratio sharpens the historical question. Extending that state across its entire growth interval would place stronger demands on the seed mass. Earlier, more intense feeding episodes offer another possible history. A quasar can arrive at the same mass through different combinations of starting size, feeding duration and efficiency.

The authors explore this problem using illustrative growth curves. These calculations make their assumptions explicit: the seed appears at redshift 20, earlier than the observed quasar; radiative efficiency is set at 0.10; and different feeding duty cycles are considered. A duty cycle is the share of an assumed interval spent in a specified feeding state.

With efficiency set to 0.10, the model treats one-tenth of the incoming material’s rest-mass energy as radiation. That choice helps connect luminosity to growth. Together with the seed’s assumed starting epoch and the feeding schedule, it determines how much mass the black hole can accumulate before reaching the observed epoch.

Under continuous Eddington-limited growth with those assumptions, the calculation requires a seed of about 100,000 solar masses. Less continuous feeding calls for a heavier seed, because the black hole spends a smaller share of the available time gaining mass at the specified rate.

The result gives a concrete meaning to the head-start idea. A seed already containing around 100,000 solar masses begins much closer to the observed target than a small stellar remnant. Yet that number belongs to the stated model. The curves describe possible histories that satisfy chosen conditions; the observations establish the endpoint against which those histories are tested.

Other assumptions can change the result. Greater mass supply or growth that releases less escaping radiation for each unit of incoming mass can permit lighter seeds. In some flows, radiation can become trapped and carried inward with the gas. That allows the relationship between observed brightness and mass delivery to differ from the simple efficient-radiation picture.

Sustaining favourable conditions presents its own physical problem. Energy released during feeding can heat or expel surrounding gas, reducing the available supply. This process, called feedback, can be especially consequential around a young seed whose gravitational hold on its surroundings is comparatively weak. Changes in efficiency can alter the growth path further.

The models therefore help identify what each explanation demands. A small-seed history may require unusually effective feeding. A large-seed history requires a way to create that substantial starting mass. Dún Aonghasa makes both questions more specific while leaving several formation routes viable.

The discovery also raises a population question. A luminous quasar becomes visible during an active phase, when surrounding gas releases enough energy for telescopes to detect it across cosmic distances. Fainter black holes, quiet ones and sources obscured by dust enter a survey differently. A collection of bright quasars therefore samples particular conditions within the larger early black-hole population.

The authors suggest that Dún Aonghasa’s relatively low observed Eddington ratio leaves room for similarly massive objects in brighter phases to be discovered. That is a possibility derived from the object’s inferred properties. The size of any such population remains uncertain.

Additional discoveries could give the growth models a much broader test. Repeated examples of large masses at very early epochs would establish a pattern that formation theories must explain. A wider range of luminosities and inferred feeding states could help reveal how varied those early histories were. Detailed spectra would improve the measurements on which those comparisons depend.

For now, Dún Aonghasa supplies one unusually early, well-characterized point in that investigation. The survey found a candidate, spectroscopy placed its light at redshift 8.13, and analysis connected that light to a massive black hole and its observed activity. Those steps have turned a general puzzle about rapid growth into a set of measurable constraints.

The light preserves the black hole after it had already become enormous. The next question reaches into the interval before that snapshot: how much of its early success came from a large beginning, and how much came from the way it fed?
