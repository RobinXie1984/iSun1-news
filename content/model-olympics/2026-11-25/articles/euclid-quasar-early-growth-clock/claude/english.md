# The Race Against the Cosmic Clock: How Did a 360-Million-Sun Black Hole Exist 630 Million Years After the Big Bang?

On 24 November 2026, the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy (MPIA) in Heidelberg announced that Euclid had helped find the most distant quasar known. Euclid is ESA's wide-field survey telescope, launched in 2023 to map large stretches of the sky. A quasar is the brilliant core of a galaxy, lit up by gas pouring toward a giant black hole. The new object carries the catalogue name EUCL J150452.291+625224.39, usually shortened to J1504+6252. The discovery team nicknamed it Dún Aonghasa, after a prehistoric stone fort on an Irish clifftop. Its light set out when the universe was about 630 million years old. The black hole at its heart has an estimated mass of roughly 360 million times the mass of our Sun.

A team led by Julien Wolf reported the discovery in the journal *Astronomy & Astrophysics*. Wolf did the work as a postdoctoral researcher at MPIA and has since moved to the European Southern Observatory in Garching. Silvia Belladitta led crucial follow-up observations with the Large Binocular Telescope in Arizona. Their result pairs a very large mass with a very early moment in cosmic history, and that pairing raises a sharp question. How could anything grow so heavy so soon?

The pace of the black hole's feeding sharpens the puzzle further. When we catch it, the black hole is eating at a moderate pace. The giant looks well fed, but its table manners look calm.

## Looking far means looking back

Light travels at a fixed speed, so light from distant places takes time to reach us. Starlight shows the star as it was when the light left. For the most distant objects, that delay covers almost the whole history of the universe. Dún Aonghasa's light shows the quasar during the universe's early childhood. The announcement came in 2026, while the event it records is ancient.

Astronomers measure this distance through redshift. As the universe expands, it stretches light travelling across it. Stretched light shifts toward longer, redder wavelengths. The amount of stretching tells astronomers how far back in time they are looking. Dún Aonghasa has a redshift of about 8.13. In plain terms, every wave of its light now spans about 9.13 times its original length. The research paper gives a precise value of 8.129, with an uncertainty of 0.005. That small uncertainty makes the distance one of the firmest numbers in the story.

The record has a clear scope. Dún Aonghasa is the first luminous quasar confirmed beyond redshift 8, and it was the most distant known quasar as of the 24 November reports. ESA framed the advance as 40 million years. That figure compares two moments in cosmic history. The previous record-holding quasar appears about 40 million years later in the universe's timeline than Dún Aonghasa does. The 40 million years measures how much further back astronomers can now see; the black hole's own age and growth history are separate questions.

## From a speck in a survey to a confirmed giant

Finding such an object resembles searching a beach for one particular grain of sand. Euclid's Wide Survey photographs enormous areas of sky in several colours of light, and it records millions of sources. Very distant quasars have a telltale colour pattern. Much of their bluer light has been stretched or absorbed along the way, so they glow mainly at longer wavelengths. Wolf and colleagues sifted roughly 18 months of Euclid data for candidates that fit this pattern. Those 18 months are the period of data the team analysed. The data capture a single snapshot of a faraway object, frozen at one ancient moment.

A colour pattern marks a promising suspect. Proof of distance requires a spectrum. A spectrum spreads light out by wavelength, the way a prism spreads sunlight into a rainbow. Atoms in hot gas emit light at specific, well-known wavelengths, which act like fingerprints. In a distant quasar, those fingerprints appear shifted to longer wavelengths by the cosmic stretching. Measuring the shift gives the redshift directly.

Several observatories contributed. Follow-up work involved the Keck Observatory in Hawaii, the Hubble Space Telescope, the Large Binocular Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope. Together they established the quasar's distance and measured its physical properties. Euclid acted as the wide-angle scout, and the follow-up telescopes supplied the detailed close-ups. Each kind of observation supports a different conclusion. The survey images point to a candidate, and the spectra fix its distance and character.

## What exactly is shining?

A black hole is a region where matter is packed so tightly that its gravity traps everything crossing a boundary called the event horizon. NASA's overview describes how light itself stays captive inside that boundary. So the brilliance of a quasar comes from just outside the black hole.

Gas drawn toward the black hole settles into a swirling disk. As the gas spirals inward, friction and compression heat it to extreme temperatures, and it glows fiercely. This process of matter falling inward is called accretion. The light we see comes from this heated material before it crosses the horizon. A quasar can outshine all the stars in its galaxy combined. That brightness is the reason astronomers can detect one so far away.

Measuring a black hole's mass at this distance takes some detective work. Astronomers rely entirely on the light that reaches them; the horizon itself lies far beyond any telescope's ability to photograph it. Clouds of gas orbit the black hole, and their speed reveals the strength of its gravity. Faster-moving gas blurs the atomic fingerprints in the spectrum into wider lines, so line width indicates speed. The quasar's brightness offers a rough guide to how far those gas clouds sit from the centre, through relationships calibrated on closer, better-studied black holes. Speed plus distance gives a mass estimate.

Wolf and colleagues arrive at a reference mass of about 360 million Suns. A "solar mass" is the mass of our Sun, so the figure measures how much matter the black hole contains compared with the Sun; its physical size is a separate quantity. Different established calibrations give somewhat different answers. Across them, the team reports a range of roughly 210 million to 780 million solar masses. These figures describe one object measured through several methods, and the spread reflects real uncertainty in the method. Even the low end of the range is enormous for a black hole seen so early.

## The clock problem

Black holes like this one start as a "seed," a smaller black hole that later grows. In the ordinary astrophysical routes astronomers usually consider, a seed forms some time after the Big Bang, once the first structures have had time to come together. Under those routes, Dún Aonghasa had less than 630 million years to reach its present size, and the real window was shorter still. The paper also considers a more speculative option: primordial black holes, hypothetical seeds that would have formed much earlier. That idea remains hypothetical, and this observation leaves the seed's origin open.

Within the ordinary routes, astronomers discuss two broad families of seeds. Light seeds are the remains of the very first generation of stars, which collapsed into modest black holes at the end of their lives. Heavy seeds could form through the direct collapse of a huge cloud of gas into a black hole, skipping the star stage and starting life much larger. A heavy seed gets a head start. A light seed needs more growth to catch up. Direct collapse remains a possibility that fits the models; this observation records a mature quasar, and the seed's birth lies far earlier than the light captured here. The paper treats these as competing explanations to test.

Growth can compound. Falling gas heats up and shines, and its light pushes outward on the surrounding gas. At a certain brightness, the outward push of light balances the inward pull of gravity. Physicists call this idealized balance point the Eddington limit, after the astronomer Arthur Eddington. It serves as a reference point for steady feeding under simple conditions. A heavier black hole has a higher limit, so as it grows, that reference rate rises too, and in principle growth can speed up. Actual growth depends on how much gas reaches the black hole and on how efficiently the inflow turns into light.

Astronomers compare a quasar's actual brightness with this limit through a number called the Eddington ratio. A ratio of 1 means the quasar shines right at the balance point. A ratio of 0.5 means it shines at half that level.

This brings in the twist. The team's mass calibrations give Dún Aonghasa an Eddington ratio between about 0.12 and 0.44, with a reference value near 0.26. At the moment its light left, the quasar was shining at roughly a quarter of its theoretical limit. The ratio describes the single ancient moment captured in the light. It compares brightness with a calculated balance point. The fraction of the black hole's life spent feeding, and the share of mass gained in any period, are separate quantities that this ratio leaves open. Because it depends on the mass estimate, it carries the same uncertainty.

The ratio still matters. A black hole that grew at a quarter of its limit for its entire life would need a very large starting mass to reach 360 million Suns in the time available. So the observed calm phase pushes in one of two directions. Either the seed began heavy, or the black hole went through earlier periods of much faster feeding.

## An illustration: the reservoir and the deadline

An everyday comparison can help here, with the caveat that it is only an illustration. Picture a reservoir that must hold a certain amount of water by a fixed deadline. The starting water level stands for the seed mass. The inflow from the taps stands for gas feeding the black hole. Periods when the taps close stand for pauses in feeding.

Astronomers have, in effect, glanced at the reservoir once and seen water running in at a moderate rate. They also know roughly how full it is and how close the deadline was. From that single glance, many histories remain possible. The reservoir might have started nearly full. It might have started low and had the taps running hard earlier. It might have filled in bursts with long dry spells between them.

Real accretion has one feature a reservoir lacks. The inflowing gas releases light, and that light can push back against further inflow. How strongly it pushes depends on how brightly the gas shines and on the shape and motion of the flow. A heavier black hole raises the idealized Eddington limit, while its actual feeding depends on how much gas arrives and how efficiently that gas turns into light. Gravity and radiation make the real process far more tangled than plumbing. The comparison captures one idea: a snapshot of the present rate leaves the starting level and the full history open.

## What the models say, and what they assume

To explore the possible histories, Wolf and colleagues ran simple growth calculations. These calculations explore "what if" questions under stated assumptions, and they map possibilities for a past that remains unobserved.

In one example, the team places the seed at redshift 20, a very early stage of the universe, well before the moment we observe. They assume a radiative efficiency of 0.10. Efficiency here means the share of the infalling matter's energy released as light. At 10 percent, a tenth of that energy shines away, and most of the infalling material adds to the black hole's mass. They also assume the black hole feeds continuously at exactly the Eddington limit. Under those conditions, reaching the estimated mass requires a seed of about 100,000 solar masses. That is a heavy seed, in the range people associate with direct collapse.

Change the assumptions and the answer moves. If the black hole fed in stops and starts, the time spent growing shrinks, and the seed has to start even heavier. If gas arrived faster than the standard picture allows, lighter seeds become workable. One route involves what physicists call radiatively inefficient growth. In very dense inflows, light can be trapped in the gas and carried inward along with it. More mass then reaches the black hole while comparatively little light escapes to push back. Under such conditions, the brake loosens, and a smaller seed could grow quickly.

Other effects push the other way. Energy from a feeding black hole can blow gas out of its surroundings, starving it for a while. This process is known as feedback. A black hole's spin can change its efficiency over time, altering how much light it releases per unit of mass swallowed. The paper's curves show which feeding rates and seed sizes would work under each set of assumptions. They map the possibilities and leave the actual history open.

## One bright window onto a hidden population

Dún Aonghasa is visible because it is luminous. Survey searches favour objects bright enough to cross a detection threshold, in a phase when they are actively feeding, and in directions where dust leaves their light clear. Black holes that are quieter, dust-shrouded, or between meals at this epoch would slip past such searches. So one bright quasar reveals one active giant, and the wider early population remains largely uncounted.

The authors draw a forward-looking idea from the moderate feeding rate. If a 360-million-Sun black hole can appear this early while shining at about a quarter of its limit, similarly massive black holes caught in brighter phases might also exist and be detectable. That is a prediction for future surveys to test. Euclid continues to map the sky, and Webb continues to provide detailed follow-up, so the test is practical.

The two announcements came from ESA and MPIA, institutions connected to the discovery team. They are accounts of the same research and carry the same evidence. Independent confirmation from separate groups would add further weight. This article draws on the team's public author manuscript, dated 11 September 2026, together with those two reports and NASA's black-hole background material. The publisher's final text was inaccessible during preparation, so any late changes to its equations or tables remain unchecked here.

The finding has a clear payoff. The distance rests on spectroscopy. The mass, though uncertain, is large across every calibration the team used. The feeding rate at the observed moment is moderate. Together these give theorists a firm target. Any account of how the first giant black holes formed must deliver this much mass within this little time. It must also allow for a black hole that, when we catch it, is eating at a measured pace.

The question that remains is a concrete one. Which combinations of seed size and feeding history could build a black hole like Dún Aonghasa in a universe barely 630 million years old? And how many others like it are still waiting beyond the edge of what our surveys can currently see?
