# A Flare Has Begun. Will It Still Be Shining When the Rocket Looks?

On November 4, 2026, NASA Goddard published a report by Vanessa Thomas about a contest of timing between a rocket and the Sun. The report describes a flare alert system that helps scientists decide when to launch sounding rockets, which are small research rockets that carry instruments briefly above Earth's air and then fall back to the ground. According to NASA, rocket teams used the alert during launches from Alaska in April 2024 and May 2026, and those rockets observed solar flares while the flares were still underway. NASA says the researchers described the work the same day in a paper in *The Astrophysical Journal*. The launches happened months and years before the announcement. The November report is when the story reached the public, and it raises a practical question: when a flare has already begun, how can a team on the ground judge whether it will still be shining by the time their instrument is ready to look?

To follow that question, it helps to start with the Sun itself.

## A short flight and a short flare

A solar flare is a sudden burst of energy from the Sun. NASA describes flares as releasing energy and speeding up particles, and solar activity of this kind can affect Earth and the technology people depend on. Flares come from active regions, which NASA describes as magnetically complex areas of the Sun. One way to picture an active region is as a tangle of invisible magnetic loops that can snap into a new arrangement and release stored energy in a flash.

Much of a flare's story is written in kinds of light our eyes miss. Two of the most useful kinds are X-rays, the high-energy light doctors use to see bones, and ultraviolet light, the kind that causes sunburn. NASA explains that Earth's atmosphere absorbs some of the light the researchers want to measure. That absorption is good news for people on the ground, because the air acts as a shield. For scientists, the same shield hides part of the signal. A telescope sitting on a mountain receives only what the air lets through.

Researchers have two broad ways to get above that shield. Satellites stay in space for years and keep watch. Sounding rockets make a quick trip upward and come back. NASA's program overview says sounding rockets carry instruments along a parabolic path, an arc shaped like the path of a ball tossed high into the air. They rise, coast, and descend, and they stay suborbital, meaning they return before circling the planet. NASA puts the typical time in space for these rockets at 5 to 20 minutes. The agency also describes them as relatively inexpensive, because they skip the expensive boosters and the long tracking coverage that an orbiting mission requires. That cost explanation applies to sounding rockets in general.

That brief trip shapes the whole problem. In this campaign, NASA says, the rockets offered about five minutes of useful observing opportunity before heading back down. That figure measures something narrower than the overview's 5 to 20 minutes. The overview describes how long sounding rockets generally spend in space. The five minutes describes how long this team's instruments had to study a flare. One is the general stretch a payload spends in space, above the atmosphere; the other is the shorter portion this campaign's instruments could spend looking at the target. The total flight time of these rockets, from launch to landing, is a separate figure that lies outside these sources.

NASA's report adds the other half of the timing problem: flares can fade on a similarly short timescale. So the team faces a matching pair of short clocks. The instrument gets a few minutes of looking time, and the flare may have only a few minutes of brightness left. If the rocket's window opens after the flare has dimmed, the instrument records the fading remains. The useful launch is one where the window and the flare overlap.

As an everyday comparison, picture a friend sending you a message that a street band has just started playing in a park across town. You have a short break and a limited chance to watch. Two facts decide whether the trip is worth it: which park the band is in, and roughly how long it will keep playing. If the set is likely to run long, you head out and arrive while the music continues. If the band is already packing up, the trip brings you to an empty bandstand. This comparison is purely an illustration of the decision; the real alert's accuracy is a separate matter, taken up later.

## How the alert works

The alert system does the job of that friend's message. According to NASA, it analyzes X-ray and ultraviolet emission from active regions. It draws on observations from NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory, from satellites in the GOES series operated by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the US agency for weather, oceans and the atmosphere, and from observatories on the ground. NASA says that within minutes of spacecraft detecting a flare, the system pinpoints where the flare is on the Sun and estimates how long it will last.

Both pieces of information matter to a rocket team. The location tells the team where on the Sun to look. The duration estimate tells the team whether the flare is likely to remain bright long enough for a launch to pay off.

The Solar Dynamics Observatory, often shortened to SDO, is long-standing equipment in this story. NASA launched it on February 11, 2010, as a space observatory designed to study how solar activity arises and drives space weather. It monitors the Sun's interior, its atmosphere, its magnetic field and its energy output. Space weather is the name for conditions in space shaped by the Sun, much as ordinary weather describes conditions in the air around us. SDO's launch date belongs to the observatory's history; the flares in this story came much later.

NASA's report presents these observatories together as the system's data sources. Learning the instrument-by-instrument method would require checking the paper, whose full text sits behind publisher access; what the paper says on that point remains to be verified, and this account relies on NASA's summary.

It is worth being precise about what the alert estimates. It responds to a flare that spacecraft have already detected. Its task is to judge where that flare is and how much life it has left. Forecasting when the next flare will begin is a separate task, and the report presents the system as a tool for the first job.

The arrangement also shows how two kinds of instruments can work together. The satellites and ground observatories provide ongoing observations of the Sun, and monitoring from space helps flag the moment a flare opportunity appears, much as a lifeguard keeps an eye on the whole beach. The rocket carries a specialized instrument for a brief, close look, the way a swimmer might dive in to study one particular wave. The watchers spot the opportunity; the rocket seizes it. That division of labor is an interpretation of the roles NASA describes, and the report presents it as a working partnership in these campaigns.

## Two reported cases from Alaska

The first reported case came in April 2024. NASA says the alert helped this rocket team and another team coordinate the launch of two rockets from Alaska, and that together they studied a flare before it disappeared. The count matters here: two rockets watched one flare. Coordinating them meant two sets of instruments examined the same event during the same short life, which gives scientists more than one view of a single outburst. Finer flight details for this case would require checking the paper and mission records; what those documents contain remains to be verified.

The second case came two years later with an improved version of the alert. NASA's image caption identifies the flight: a Black Brant IX rocket carrying FOXSI-5 lifted off on May 14, 2026, at 11:23 a.m. local time from Poker Flat Research Range near Fairbanks, Alaska. FOXSI stands for Focusing Optics X-ray Solar Imager, and the 5 marks the fifth version of the instrument. NASA reports that during this flight, FOXSI-5 observed three small flares.

An X-ray imager works like a camera for X-ray light. It gathers the light a flare gives off and builds a picture from it. The rocket stays close to home in cosmic terms, rising above enough of the atmosphere for the X-rays to reach the instrument and then returning to Earth. The light travels from the Sun to the camera, and the camera records it.

NASA describes these launches as the first time its sounding rockets used this alert system to study a solar flare in progress. That claim is specific. It concerns NASA's sounding rockets paired with this alert. Scientists have observed solar flares for a long time, and research rockets have a long history of their own. The new step is the alert-guided timing.

## What a full record would show

At this point, the story has a clear achievement. A team judging a flare's remaining life now had a tool offering a location and a duration estimate within minutes. In the two reported cases, launches lined up with flares, and the instruments gathered observations: two rockets studied one flare in April 2024, and one rocket recorded three small flares in May 2026. These are real scientific results from difficult, tightly timed experiments.

The harder question concerns how far those results stretch. Observing three flares on one flight is a successful catch. Measuring how reliable an alert is calls for a different kind of evidence: a score across many tries.

A weather forecaster offers a helpful comparison. People come to trust a forecaster through a long record, in which each forecast is lined up against the weather that followed. One sunny afternoon at a picnic shows that one forecast worked. A season of forecasts compared with a season of weather shows how often the forecaster gets it right.

For the flare alert, a full record of that kind would include every alert the system issued during the campaign periods, the duration it estimated each time, and how long each flare actually lasted. It would include the timestamps that separate the parts of the delay: the moment spacecraft spotted the flare, the moment the alert reached the team, the moment people decided to launch, and the moment the instrument began observing. It would list the alerts that led to launches, the alerts the teams passed on, and the launches whose observations proved more or less useful. For the May flight, a first step would be to establish which of the three small flares received alerts and duration estimates; those forecasts could then be compared with how long each flare actually lasted. The public report presents the featured observations. Judging reliability would require checking the paper's methods and the teams' records for a complete sample, error rates and uncertainty ranges; whether those documents hold such a tally remains to be verified.

There is also the question of comparison. Rocket teams made launch decisions before this alert existed, using their own judgment and the data in front of them. To say the alert improves decisions, researchers would compare its record against that earlier process across similar opportunities. That comparison would show how much of the success belongs to the alert and how much to skilled teams, favorable solar activity, or both.

The achievement of the two reported cases stands on its own. The fuller record describes the next layer of evidence, the one that would turn two well-timed cases into a measured track record.

## The bigger picture, and its boundaries

Flares matter beyond the laboratory. NASA notes that solar flares release energy and accelerate particles, and solar activity can affect Earth and technology. It is tempting to leap from a better launch decision to a better warning service for satellites, power grids or aircraft. The reported achievement is narrower and concrete: helping a rocket's instrument start observing while a flare is still underway. Estimating how long a flare will stay bright is one question. Predicting whether the flare's related activity will reach Earth, and what it might do there, is a separate question. Uses in wider space weather forecasting, or in scheduling observations by other telescopes, remain possible future directions for the idea.

NASA says the team is continuing to improve the system with support from a NASA Early Career Investigator Program grant. That makes the alert a research tool still in development. The May 2026 flight already used an enhanced version, which suggests the system is changing from one campaign to the next.

The underlying idea has an appealing simplicity. Some experiments last only minutes, and some targets live only minutes. Ongoing watchers in space and on the ground can spot the moment when a brief experiment has its best chance, and an estimate of how long the target will last can turn a guess into a reasoned decision. The aim is modest and useful: to collect more good observations of a violent, fleeting event. The rocket leaves the Sun to do as it pleases. It simply tries to be ready when the show begins.

The two reported cases show the method working on the days NASA describes. The open question is the one any new forecast eventually faces. Across every flare the alert flags, how often does its estimate send a rocket up in time to see something worth seeing, and how much better is that record than the judgment teams relied on before?
