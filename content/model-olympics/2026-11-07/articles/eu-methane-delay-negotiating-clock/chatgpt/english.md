# Europe’s Council Seeks a Methane Delay. What Will Be Ready When Time Runs Out?

Europe is trying to connect the fossil fuels it buys with reliable evidence about the methane released during their production. That effort reached a new negotiating stage on November 6, 2026, when the Council of the European Union, representing national governments, backed a proposal to delay selected requirements for oil, natural gas and coal. The European Parliament, its fellow legislator, already favours longer extensions for some reporting duties. Behind their competing calendars lies a practical question: how can evidence collected at a production site reach a European buyer in a form that an independent examiner can trust? [S1–S3]

The climate stakes explain why Europe wants that information. Methane contributes to global warming; the Council attributes about 30% of global warming to the gas in its account of the regulation’s background. That figure describes methane’s global importance. The effect of this particular European law depends on its coverage, implementation and the changes it brings to fuel production. Measurement gives authorities a starting point for understanding emissions, comparing performance and assessing whether producers meet the rules. [S1; S2]

The EU methane regulation entered into force in August 2024, following Council adoption that May. Its requirements arrive in stages. Importer information duties began on May 5, 2025, according to the Council’s legislative draft. Later stages address the quality of producers’ monitoring systems, the amount of methane associated with fuel production and, eventually, compliance with maximum emissions-intensity values. The current negotiation concerns specified obligations within that larger framework. [S1; S2, p. 3]

As of November 7, 2026, the proposed extensions remain negotiating positions. The Council’s document is a mandate for talks with Parliament, and Parliament’s October amendments express its own preferred terms. A final compromise, enactment and publication remain unverified in the available official record. The existing schedule therefore remains the legal reference while legislators seek an agreement. [S1–S3]

The Commission, which proposes EU legislation and carries out implementation work, presented the targeted amendment on October 14. Parliament adopted its amendments on October 22, with 341 votes in favour, 225 against and 6 abstentions, then referred the matter back to committee for negotiations between the institutions. The Council agreed its position on November 6 and wants agreement before year-end, ahead of importer requirements currently scheduled to apply from January 1, 2027. [S1–S3]

The Council frames extra time as protection for reliable energy supplies amid disruption linked to conflict in the Middle East. Its draft says companies and authorities need more preparation to meet the requirements and warns that compliance difficulties could affect supplies. This is the Council’s case for changing the timetable. The official materials establish the concern and the proposed response; the scale of any deadline-related supply disruption, and any resulting price relief from an extension, remain unmeasured in those materials. [S1; S2, pp. 4–5]

Understanding the dispute requires following the information alongside the fuel.

Monitoring means gathering evidence about emissions. Reporting means sending the required information to an authority. Verification means an independent assessment of that evidence. For imported fuel, the EU framework asks whether the producer’s monitoring, reporting and verification measures are equivalent to those required under European rules. A producer abroad may operate under a different national system; the equivalence process examines whether that system or the applicable measures meet the required standard. [S2, pp. 3, 6–9; S4]

Imagine, as an illustration, a product passport travelling through a supply chain. The original producer records where the product came from and the relevant facts about its production. Each seller passes the information to the next buyer. An independent examiner checks the supporting records. For the passport to be useful, participants need agreed fields, a reliable way to transfer them and a shared understanding of what the examiner should assess.

That illustration captures the practical challenge described in the Council draft. Its application to methane involves emissions records and commercial relationships. The arrangements for accepting verification, transferring data and recognising overseas systems determine whether the information reaching an importer has a credible foundation. The passport is an explanatory image; the sources describe legal duties and proposed implementation measures. [S2, pp. 8–9; S4]

The chain becomes harder to follow when supplies mix. The Commission’s implementation questions and answers explain that importers still need source information when natural gas is commingled. Information is expected to pass through direct contractual relationships: the businesses buying and selling to each other carry the relevant evidence along that chain. Compliance documents also sit alongside the framework’s specified information requirements and verifier reports. Each serves a role in establishing what happened upstream. [S4]

This creates a practical distinction between obtaining fuel and obtaining dependable information about its production. A commercial transaction moves the fuel through the market. The emissions record depends on additional arrangements among the firms involved. The Council’s proposal treats the development of those arrangements as part of the work that an extension would allow. [S2, pp. 4–5, 8–9; S4]

Its timetable separates several duties that can easily blur into a single headline about delay.

For importer equivalence requirements, the Council proposes moving the application date from January 1, 2027, to January 1, 2028. Under its proposed Article 28 wording, importers would first demonstrate and report compliance by May 31, 2029, using information about supplies during the previous calendar year. January marks the start of the requirement; May marks the deadline for presenting the subsequent annual evidence. Both dates belong to the Council’s proposal and depend on completed legislation. [S1; S2, pp. 6–7]

Contract dates also matter. The proposed compliance report covers contracts concluded or renewed on or after August 4, 2024. For older contracts, the Council draft requires importers to report the results of reasonable efforts to secure equivalent producer-level measures, also by May 31, 2029. Where those efforts fail, importers would explain the failure and describe the actions undertaken. Older contracts therefore carry a different reporting treatment, with continuing information responsibilities. [S2, pp. 6–7]

A separate duty concerns methane intensity: emissions in relation to the amount of fuel produced. This measure helps describe how much methane accompanies production, using a calculation method established under the regulation. The Council proposes moving the first intensity-reporting deadline for EU producers and importers from August 5, 2028, to August 5, 2029. Reporting an intensity value makes information available for scrutiny. [S1; S2, pp. 3, 7]

The later requirement goes further by asking producers and importers to demonstrate that intensity falls below a maximum value. The Council proposes moving that deadline from August 5, 2030, to August 5, 2031. Its draft applies this demonstration to supplies under contracts concluded or renewed after August 5, 2030. Establishing a measurement system, reporting its results and demonstrating performance against a limit are distinct steps, each with its own legal conditions and calendar. [S1; S2, p. 8]

Parliament seeks a longer wait for selected disclosures. Its position sets May 31, 2031, as the first importer equivalence compliance-reporting deadline, compared with May 31, 2029, in the Council draft. Parliament also proposes August 5, 2031, for the first methane-intensity report, compared with the Council’s August 5, 2029. Those comparisons concern the same reporting duties. The Council’s January 2028 application date serves a different function. [S2, pp. 6–8; S3]

Parliament also proposes a scope exception: fuel originating in, or exported directly or indirectly from, countries covered by the specified EU measures prohibiting or restricting those imports would be excluded from the extra time. Its position would require importers to declare those volumes separately, including within mixed cargoes, and the Commission to publish and update an indicative list of the countries concerned. These safeguards form part of Parliament’s negotiating position. [S3]

Domestic oil and gas reporting is also part of the negotiation. The reports at issue combine estimates for individual emissions sources with measurements across a site, allowing the overall measurements to be compared with the combined estimates. An independent verifier assesses the report before submission. For assets an operator runs, the Council proposes February 5, 2028, while Parliament seeks February 5, 2030. For assets in which a company participates while another operator runs them, the respective proposed dates are August 5, 2029, and August 5, 2031. These changes concern specified verified reports within the regulation. [S2, p. 6; S3]

The differences reveal a substantive negotiation over how long companies and authorities should have to assemble dependable evidence. They also bring the immediate consequence of an extension into focus: if enacted, the affected reporting duties and performance demonstrations would occur later. Any compliance benefit from that interval would depend on what participants accomplish during it.

The Council draft offers a way to examine that accomplishment. It proposes a Commission readiness assessment by September 30, 2027, followed by another by June 30, 2028, if the earlier assessment finds the necessary conditions unmet. The Commission would transmit its assessment to Parliament and the Council. This would create a formal point at which preparation could be judged against specified requirements. [S2, pp. 8–9]

The proposed assessment addresses the rules governing independent verification accepted as equivalent to the EU standard and the methods used to transfer methane information between producers and importers. It also asks whether enough independent verifiers will be available when verification is due, using a reasonable forecast grounded in facts. Finally, it examines the procedures and evidence requirements for recognising equivalence in countries outside the EU. [S2, p. 9]

Each condition answers a different practical question. Agreed verification rules define what counts as an acceptable check. Transfer methods help the evidence reach the responsible importer. Qualified examiners provide the capacity to carry out those checks. Procedures for overseas systems establish how a country can demonstrate that its arrangements meet the European framework’s requirements. Together, these conditions describe the supporting work needed to turn a legal obligation into usable evidence.

The capacity test deserves particular care. A proposed requirement to forecast verifier availability establishes a future question for the Commission. The documents presented here leave the present scale of any shortage unresolved. A meaningful assessment would connect the expected workload with the people qualified and available to perform it. That is an implication of the proposed test; a completed capacity finding remains a future result. [S2, p. 9]

If any specified readiness condition is unmet, the Council draft calls for the Commission to adopt a delegated act—a legal measure under authority granted by the legislation—to postpone the listed obligations by a further year. That conditional mechanism reaches importer equivalence requirements, intensity duties and the specified domestic operator reports. Its scope is broader than the import emphasis in the Council’s press release. [S1; S2, p. 9]

Continued implementation problems, including after two consecutive postponements, or a threat to supply security could lead the Commission to propose further legislative changes. Every stage retains its own trigger and procedure. The present record establishes a proposed mechanism for responding to future findings; an additional postponement would depend on the legislation and the assessment it requires. [S2, p. 9]

Parliament proposes its own progress check: a Commission report by July 1, 2029, on implementation of the importer and methane-intensity provisions and on supply security. The report would examine national penalty systems, verifier accreditation, recognition of compliance solutions and information submitted under the regulation. Parliament also calls for the Commission to propose changes where necessary in response to continuing implementation difficulties or a supply-security threat. This reporting proposal forms another part of the negotiation over how preparation would be scrutinised during an extension. [S3]

Some preparation is already described in the Council draft. It records the creation of an expert group and a network of national authorities, a request for European standards, and Commission recommendations on compliance solutions and penalties. Those steps show institutional work under way. Their practical success will be judged through the arrangements they produce and the evidence those arrangements can carry. [S2, pp. 3–5]

The remaining tension is clear. The Council argues that more time can support reliable supplies and workable compliance. A later timetable would also defer the affected disclosures and tests. The climate effect of that deferral, the compliance gains from additional preparation and any market relief remain open empirical questions in the available sources. Each would require evidence suited to the claim: emissions outcomes for climate effects, functioning arrangements for readiness, and supply or price analysis for market effects.

For now, Europe’s legislators are deciding how much time to allow and what should happen during it. The Council’s proposed readiness assessment and Parliament’s proposed implementation report would provide different opportunities to examine that work. Agreed standards, functioning information transfers, sufficient verification capacity and usable procedures for overseas systems give the debate a concrete centre. The question accompanying any final extension is therefore straightforward. When the next deadline approaches, what evidence will show that the extra time has made the emissions record more reliable?
