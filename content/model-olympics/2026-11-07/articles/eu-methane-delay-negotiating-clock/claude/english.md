# EU Governments Propose Delaying Several Methane Deadlines While Current Law Stands. Will the Evidence Be Ready When the Clock Restarts?

On November 6, 2026, the Council of the European Union agreed the position it will carry into negotiations over Europe's methane rules. The Council is the EU's body of national governments, where ministers write laws together with the elected European Parliament. Methane is the main ingredient of natural gas, and it also escapes into the air from oil fields, gas pipelines and coal mines. In 2024 the EU passed a regulation that asks the energy industry to measure, report and cut those emissions, including for fuel that Europe buys from abroad. The Council now wants several of that law's deadlines pushed back by a year, starting with import rules currently due on January 1, 2027. The European Parliament adopted its own set of changes on October 22, with longer delays for several reports. Both positions respond to an October 14 proposal from the European Commission, the EU's executive arm, which drafts laws and oversees how they work in practice. Changing the law requires the two lawmaking bodies to settle on one shared text, so for now the 2024 regulation and its dates remain in force as written.

That gap between a headline and the legal reality sits at the centre of this story. The Council's press release speaks of postponing monitoring rules for a year. The document behind it is a mandate for negotiations: a marked-up draft that shows the changes the Council wants. It carries legislative language, yet it remains a bargaining text. The final wording, and the exact day any amendment would take effect, remain open.

## Why an invisible gas became a paperwork problem

The reason Europe cares about methane is heat. Methane traps warmth in the atmosphere, and the Council's own announcement attributes about 30% of global warming to it. That figure describes methane worldwide. It is background for why the gas matters, and it measures something larger than what any single European rule could address.

The EU's methane regulation was adopted by the Council in May 2024 and came into force in August 2024. Much of it governs companies operating inside Europe. Its most ambitious part reaches across borders. Europe buys large quantities of crude oil, natural gas and coal from other countries, and the regulation asks importers to show that the fuel they bring in comes with methane evidence of a quality comparable to Europe's own.

Those import duties arrive in stages. According to the Council's draft, importers have been required since May 5, 2025 to give national authorities information about what exporters and foreign producers do to measure, report, verify and reduce methane. The next stage is the one now in dispute. Under the law as it stands, from January 1, 2027, importers must demonstrate that contracts signed or renewed after August 4, 2024 cover fuel subject to monitoring, reporting and verification at the producer's level that is equivalent to the EU's own system.

Those three words, monitoring, reporting and verification, describe a chain. The Commission's official guidance on import requirements explains them in plain terms. Monitoring means gathering evidence about emissions. Reporting means passing that evidence to the authorities. Verification means an independent checker examines the evidence and judges whether it holds up.

The difficulty is that emissions happen far upstream, at a well or a mine, while the company bound by the rule sits at the end of a long trading line in Europe. Gas from different fields often travels through the same pipes and gets mixed. The Commission's guidance says importers still need information about the source in those mixed supply chains, and that the information should travel through each company's direct contracts with the next. The guidance also says that documents offered as proof of compliance supplement the detailed information the regulation lists and the independent verifiers' reports; those papers stay part of the required package.

An everyday comparison helps here, and it is only a comparison. Picture a jar of honey labelled as coming from farms that protect bees. For that label to mean something, records have to start at each farm, pass to the company that blends honey from many farms, then to the seller, and finally reach an inspector who checks that the paperwork matches reality. If any link in that relay drops the records, the label becomes a promise with weak support. Methane evidence for imported fuel depends on a similar relay of information through contracts, with an independent checker at the end. The EU's actual system runs on contracts, standards, measurements and accredited verifiers; it follows information about fuel through paperwork, while a honey jar can be tracked as a physical object.

Seen this way, the dispute is about whether the relay is ready to run.

## Three clocks, three different jobs

The Council's proposal moves three main deadlines, and each one marks a different task.

The first clock covers equivalent monitoring, reporting and verification for imported oil, gas and coal. The Council wants it moved from January 1, 2027 to January 1, 2028. Its draft separates two dates here that are easy to confuse. The requirement would apply from January 1, 2028 to supply contracts concluded or renewed on or after August 4, 2024. The first annual demonstration and report to national authorities would then fall due on May 31, 2029, covering the previous calendar year. One date marks when the rule starts to bite; the other marks when importers first hand in proof.

Older contracts receive different treatment. For supply deals signed before August 4, 2024, the draft asks importers to make all reasonable efforts to bring the fuel under equivalent producer-level checks, and to tell authorities each year what those efforts achieved, starting by May 31, 2029. If the efforts fall short, the importer must explain why and describe what it tried. Older contracts therefore remain inside the system with a lighter, effort-based duty.

The second clock covers methane intensity. Intensity expresses how much methane escapes relative to the amount of fuel produced, calculated under a method the EU sets. It works like a fuel-economy figure for a car: a rate. Under the current law, EU producers and importers report it by August 5, 2028. The Council proposes August 5, 2029.

The third clock covers performance. Reporting a number is one task; meeting a limit is another. The 2024 law requires producers and importers to demonstrate by August 5, 2030 that their fuel's methane intensity sits below maximum values the EU will set. The Council proposes August 5, 2031, and its draft attaches that duty to supply contracts concluded or renewed after August 5, 2030.

The Council's draft also reaches into Europe's own oil and gas industry. Under the regulation, operators inside the EU must submit verified reports that combine estimates for individual sources of methane, such as a valve or a compressor, with measurements across a whole site, so the two can be compared. The Council's marked text moves the first such report for assets a company operates itself from February 5, 2027 to February 5, 2028, and for assets it holds while another company operates them from August 5, 2028 to August 5, 2029.

These dates are proposals. They describe the Council's opening hand in negotiations, and each one depends on a final law being agreed and entering into force. The 2024 regulation's goals and structure remain in place under every version on the table, and under that regulation fossil-fuel imports remain legally permitted. The timetable forms the main dispute. Each side also brings added provisions: the Council a readiness test with a conditional further delay, and Parliament a sanctions safeguard and an implementation review, both described below.

## The Council's case, and its checkpoint

The Council frames its position around energy security. Its announcement links the move to protecting Europe's energy supply and avoiding extra pressure on markets already disrupted by conflict in the Middle East. Ireland currently holds the rotating presidency that steers the Council's work, and Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's minister for climate, energy and the environment, presented the position on the presidency's behalf. Separately, EU national leaders meeting as the European Council on October 15 and 16 welcomed the Commission's proposal.

The draft lays out the reasoning in more detail. It describes energy prices as very high and the global situation as volatile. It states that the 2024 regulation leaves energy imports free of legal restriction, and then argues that full compliance needs more time, warning that importers could face difficulty supplying Europe from January 2027. The extra year, the draft says, would let governments finish their national penalty systems, let national accreditation bodies approve qualified verifiers, and let authorities assess and recognise the companies that offer compliance services. The Commission adopted recommendations on July 20, 2026 on model contract clauses and on how penalties should apply to importers, aiming to clarify how complex supply chains can show compliance.

These are the Council's stated reasons and forecasts. The documents contain arguments about supply risk; measured estimates of price relief, avoided shortages or compliance costs lie outside what they report. The supply concern belongs to the Council as its rationale.

The most distinctive part of the Council's plan turns readiness into something that can be checked. The draft tells the Commission to adopt binding standards by June 30, 2027 for two things: what kind of independent verification counts as equivalent to Europe's, and which methods can carry methane information from a producer to an importer. A separate rule setting out how foreign countries can prove their systems are equivalent would also be due by June 30, 2027.

Then comes a test. By September 30, 2027, the Commission would assess whether the pieces needed for the import reporting duties are in place, and send that assessment to Parliament and the Council. It would examine four things: whether the verification standard has been adopted, whether the data-transfer methods have been defined, whether enough independent verifiers are expected to be available when checks fall due, based on a reasonable and factual forecast, and whether the procedure for foreign-country equivalence exists. If one or more of those conditions falls short, the Commission would adopt a further act postponing listed obligations by another year, and it would run the assessment again by June 30, 2028.

That further postponement would reach beyond imports. The draft's list covers the EU operators' verified reports, the importers' equivalence duties and the methane-intensity reporting duties. The Council's press release concentrates on import rules, while its legal text casts a wider net. The draft also says that if problems continue, including after two postponements in a row, or if supply is threatened, the Commission should propose further changes where appropriate. That clause describes a proposal process; any such change would pass through the lawmaking procedure again, so the draft sets limits on how far an automatic delay can run.

Today, the assessment exists as a planned exercise with set criteria and dates. Its verifier test looks ahead to the moment checks fall due, using a forecast.

## Parliament's longer timetable

Parliament arrives at the negotiating table with a different calendar. On October 22, its members adopted amendments by 341 votes to 225, with 6 abstentions, and sent the file back to committee to negotiate with the Council. That vote established Parliament's negotiating position; turning it into law depends on a final agreement with the Council.

Parliament's summary compares its dates with the Commission's proposal, which the Council's draft also follows on these points. For the importers' first equivalence report, Parliament wants May 31, 2031 in place of May 31, 2029. The annual report on reasonable efforts for older contracts would move to the same 2031 date. For the first methane-intensity report by EU producers and importers, Parliament wants August 5, 2031 in place of August 5, 2029. By simple arithmetic, each of those is two years later than the Council's date for the same report. Those comparisons concern reporting deadlines; the Council's separate January 1, 2028 application date for the import duty is a different kind of date.

For Europe's own oil and gas operators, Parliament proposes three more years beyond the original law: February 5, 2030 for operated assets and August 5, 2031 for non-operated ones. The Council's draft points to February 5, 2028 and August 5, 2029.

Parliament also adds conditions of its own. It wants the extra time to exclude crude oil, gas and coal coming directly or indirectly from countries under EU sanctions that restrict those imports. Importers would declare such volumes separately, including inside mixed cargoes, and the Commission would publish and update an indicative list of the countries concerned. Parliament further asks for a Commission report by July 1, 2029 on how the import and intensity rules are working, covering penalties, verifier accreditation, recognised compliance services, the information gathered and the state of energy supply.

Which of these ideas survive talks remains an open matter. The two institutions agree that the timetable needs changes and differ on how far to stretch it and how to police the extra time. The Council favours shorter delays with a built-in readiness test; Parliament favours longer delays with a sanctions safeguard and a later review.

## What the waiting would cost, and what it could buy

Any postponement carries a straightforward consequence. An enacted delay would defer the specified duties on importers to demonstrate equivalence and report to authorities, along with the intensity reporting and limit duties, for whatever period lawmakers finally approve. Those importer demonstrations rest on a separate step: independent verification carried out at the producer's level by accredited checkers, whose timing would follow the final law. The Council's documents offer a forecast in the other direction too: more time could let national accreditation bodies accredit verifiers, let authorities assess and recognise compliance-solution providers, and let producers, importers, operators and traders establish the mechanisms they need, while the Commission defines methods for passing methane information from producer to importer, so that participants can fully comply.

Measured estimates of both effects remain unavailable in these documents. They contain positions, rationales and forecasts. The climate cost of later accountability and the benefit of better preparation remain open questions for future evidence.

The legal clock presents a final open question. The Council wants agreement with Parliament before the end of 2026, ahead of the January 1, 2027 date. Any compromise would still need formal adoption and publication before taking effect, and the timing of that step remains open.

So the story turns on a practical question that the public can follow. If Europe grants itself more time, will the relay of evidence be ready when the clock restarts? The Council's plan offers a visible checkpoint: by June 30, 2027, standards for verification and data transfer, and by September 30, 2027, a Commission assessment for both lawmaking bodies, including a factual forecast of whether enough independent verifiers will be available when checks fall due and a judgment on whether methods for moving information from a distant well to a European buyer have been defined. Whether that checkpoint survives negotiations, and what it finds if it does, will show whether the pause becomes preparation.
