# Ice Still Clings to Puncak Jaya. Has Its Last Glacier Already Stopped Moving?

High mountains can hold ice even in the tropics, where altitude brings cold to a region associated with warmth. Near Puncak Jaya, in Indonesia’s Sudirman Range on New Guinea, that ice has become the subject of a question about what makes a glacier alive as a moving physical system. In a November 16, 2026 account, NASA Earth Observatory reports that scientists regard the surviving patches as too small, thin and stagnant to function as active glaciers. Ice remains visible. The movement that makes it a glacier appears to have ended.

That distinction gives this mountain’s changing landscape a second clock. One clock measures how long ice survives on the rock. The other measures how long accumulated ice continues to move under its own weight. Those clocks can reach their endpoints at different times. A photograph may still show a pale patch after the process that formed and sustained a flowing body of ice has broken down.

For a reader looking at a mountain from far away, the visible patch is the obvious thing to watch. Its outline seems to offer a straightforward answer: ice covers this much ground today, and a smaller amount tomorrow. A glacier, however, has depth, motion and a history of gaining and losing material. Understanding its condition means looking beneath that outline. The central question becomes more precise: what remains when the ice survives, while the glacier’s movement has ceased?

NASA’s comparison uses false-color satellite images dated November 3, 1988, and October 19, 2026, with ice marked in light blue. Climate scientist Donaldi Permana of Indonesia’s meteorology, climatology and geophysics agency, BMKG, estimates that ice covered approximately 4.31 square kilometres in 1988 and 0.09 square kilometres in September 2025. The later estimate is roughly 2% of the 1988 extent.

A square kilometre is the area of a square measuring one kilometre along each side. These figures describe the footprint of the ice: the ground it occupies when viewed from above. The comparison gives the retreat a scale that a pair of images alone can struggle to convey. An expanse once measured in several square kilometres had contracted to a small fraction of one.

The dates also matter. September 2025 belongs to the latest stated area estimate; October 2026 belongs to the newer satellite image. Together, they provide a numerical comparison and a more recent view of the landscape. The exact area in the 2026 scene remains an open measurement. Reading the evidence this way keeps each observation attached to the question it can answer.

Thickness brings the story closer to the rock. At one primary monitoring site on East Northwall Firn, ice measured 32 metres thick in 2010 and four metres in 2023. By November 2024, ice at that site had disappeared.

Thirty-two metres describes a substantial vertical body of ice above a single point. Four metres describes a much shallower remainder. The final observation records exposed ground at that location. This sequence gives the retreat a physical dimension: the change involved the loss of depth as well as the shrinking of a boundary on a map.

Its geographical scope is equally important. A measurement at one monitoring site tells the history of that site. Other surviving patches occupy other ground and have their own shapes and thicknesses. The disappearance recorded there in November 2024 fits alongside the ice visible elsewhere in the October 2026 image.

Area, thickness and motion therefore belong to three related lines of inquiry. Area asks how much ground the ice covers. Thickness asks how much ice lies above a particular spot. Motion asks whether that body changes position through flow. Calculating total volume would require a picture of thickness across the covered ground. Establishing movement requires evidence suited to movement. The roughly 2% area figure expresses the scale of retreat; the functional assessment comes from the scientists NASA cites, including Permana and glaciologist Chris Shuman.

This account draws on NASA’s dated report and historical research; each measurement retains its stated scope, and disappearance estimates remain uncertain.

The mechanics help explain how these different observations can fit together. Snow supplies material to a glacier. When enough survives and accumulates, it can become part of a deeper body of ice. Gravity acts on that body, and sufficient ice can deform and move under its own weight. The movement is slow enough to escape casual observation, yet it is central to the system.

As an everyday illustration, imagine a very slow conveyor carrying ice. Material enters at one part of the conveyor and travels onward as the system operates. If the supply dwindles and the moving layer becomes too thin, pieces may remain along its route after transport has ended. The real glacier has gravity as its driving force, and its ice changes shape as it moves. The conveyor simply helps separate two ideas that a distant view can merge: material being present and material being transported.

A glacier’s gains and losses also have to be considered over time. Scientists call this accounting its mass balance. In plain language, it is a record of how much material joins the ice and how much leaves. A fresh snowfall adds to the gain side. Melting adds to the loss side. What matters for sustained buildup is the material that survives across successive periods of gain and loss.

That makes a snowy day an incomplete guide to a glacier’s condition. A mountain can briefly turn white while its longer-term store of ice continues to diminish. Lasting recovery would involve snow surviving, accumulating and contributing to a body capable of sustaining glacier processes. The difference is between a temporary covering and a renewed supply.

NASA describes several processes that make the remnants vulnerable. Fragmentation exposes more edges. Dark rock absorbs more sunlight than reflective fresh snow, and snow that falls can melt before it rebuilds the ice.

These processes help explain why retreat changes the conditions around the surviving patches. A broad covering of reflective snow and ice gives way to a landscape containing more exposed rock and separated pieces. The boundary between ice and its surroundings grows more prominent. The mountain’s appearance and its handling of incoming sunlight change together.

The scientists’ assessment of stagnant remnants is therefore about a physical transition. The remaining ice still has size and thickness, still responds to weather and still takes time to melt. Its continued presence can coexist with the loss of the movement associated with an active glacier. Each description concerns a different property of the same landscape.

The history of research on Puncak Jaya gives this transition a longer setting. Permana and colleagues published a paper online on December 9, 2019, examining earlier retreat and thinning, including acceleration during the 2015–16 El Niño. Their work identified human-caused atmospheric warming as the broader driver, with El Niño providing episodic amplification.

El Niño is a recurring change in the tropical Pacific Ocean and atmosphere that can alter weather across a wide region. In this story, the distinction between long-term warming and a shorter climate episode matters. The background warming changes the conditions in which the ice exists over many years. An El Niño episode can add a period of greater stress within that changing background.

An everyday way to understand the timing is to think of a slowly rising baseline with occasional surges above it. The surge can produce a particularly sharp period of loss, while the baseline helps explain the direction of change across a longer record. This is an illustration of the relationship, rather than a calculation of how much ice each influence removed.

The 2019 research also looked backward through the ice itself. In mid-2010, researchers recovered two ice cores, each about 32 metres long, reaching bedrock. They used the cores to reconstruct roughly 1964–2010 tropical Pacific climate variability.

An ice core is a long cylinder extracted from a body of ice. Its value comes from the traces of past conditions held within the material. Studying those traces allows researchers to investigate a period extending beyond the moment when they arrive with instruments. In this case, the mountain provided a physical record of earlier tropical climate.

The two uses of the number 32 metres deserve separate attention. One describes thickness at the monitoring site in 2010; the other describes the approximate lengths of the cores recovered that year. Both bring depth into the story. The monitoring sequence follows physical loss at a location, while the core research uses recovered material to investigate earlier climate conditions.

The publication date sets another boundary around the evidence. The 2019 paper supplies historical context for the recent account. Its observations and reconstruction concern earlier years. NASA’s November 2026 report supplies the later description of the remnants. Together, they show how research on a shrinking ice body can move from investigating its past toward documenting the end of its active life.

This change raises a further question about what the mountain’s ice holds. There is the physical body on the mountain, available for observation and possible sampling. There are also the cores already recovered and the measurements already recorded. Their futures differ.

As a matter of scientific consequence, losing accessible ice can narrow opportunities for future sampling. A later researcher may have a new question or a new technique, while the material that could answer it has already melted. The value of a physical archive includes questions that people have still to formulate.

Existing knowledge has a different form of persistence. Recorded observations can remain available for comparison. Recovered samples can retain scientific value where they are preserved. Earlier analyses can be revisited as methods improve. The disappearance of mountain ice changes the opportunities for future work while leaving a body of evidence that researchers have already gathered.

Continued observation can also clarify the final stages. Mapping can follow the changing footprint. Thickness measurements can describe surviving depth at selected places. Evidence of motion can sharpen understanding of the remnants’ functional condition. Each approach adds a particular kind of knowledge, and their combined value comes from keeping those roles clear.

The unresolved timing remains the source of suspense. NASA reports satellite-study suggestions that the main glacial ice areas could disappear around 2030. Permana says BMKG’s estimates allow for earlier loss, with the current El Niño accelerating the decline. A December 2026 aerial survey was planned when NASA published its account.

These are prospective estimates and a planned observation. Their significance lies in the questions they frame: how much ice remains, how quickly its condition is changing, and how the next period of weather will affect its survival. Around 2030 is a possible horizon for disappearance, with an earlier outcome also under consideration. The final date will become an observation only as the landscape reaches it.

The planned survey offers a concrete next step in that record. If completed and reported, it could help update the picture beyond the September 2025 area estimate. Future observations may also reveal how differently individual patches persist as their size, depth and surroundings change.

For now, Puncak Jaya holds two kinds of evidence in the same view. The remaining patches show that ice still occupies part of the mountain. The scientists’ assessment describes a system whose active movement has ceased. Between those conditions lies the final interval of this story: how long can the last visible ice endure after the glacier has stopped moving?
