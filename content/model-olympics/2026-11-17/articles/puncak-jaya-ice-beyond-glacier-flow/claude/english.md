# The Ice Is Still There. Is the Glacier Already Gone?

On November 16, 2026, NASA Earth Observatory published an account by science writer Kathryn Hansen about the last ice on Puncak Jaya, the highest peak in Indonesia's Sudirman Range on the island of New Guinea. New Guinea sits just north of Australia, close to the equator, in a part of the world better known for steaming rainforest than for ice. Yet the summit area of Puncak Jaya rises high enough into cold air that it has carried glaciers, and those glaciers have long drawn scientists from several institutions. NASA Earth Observatory publishes satellite images with plain explanations of what they show. Indonesia's national weather and climate agency, known by its initials BMKG, measures conditions across the country, and its climate scientist Donaldi Permana has studied the Puncak Jaya ice for years. Chris Shuman, a retired glaciologist from the University of Maryland, also contributed his expertise to NASA's account. The central finding of the November report places the reader at an unusual edge. Patches of ice still sit near the summit. According to the scientists cited by NASA, those patches have become too small, too thin and too still to flow, which means they have stopped behaving as active glaciers.

That finding matters well beyond one mountain. Glaciers near the equator are rare, and the Puncak Jaya ice belongs to a region that a 2019 study in the journal *PNAS* called the Western Pacific Warm Pool, a broad stretch of the tropical Pacific where the ocean stays especially warm. Ice in such a warm neighbourhood depends on a narrow set of conditions: altitude, cold air and snowfall. An existing glacier can persist for a time while it loses more than snowfall replaces, and that steady deficit gradually wears it down. Small changes in those conditions show up quickly. Scientists watch tropical glaciers closely for that reason, and the long record from Puncak Jaya turns a remote summit into a measuring stick for a much larger warming world.

This article reports NASA's dated scientific account, with the 2019 study as historical background. Each measurement stays tied to its own date and place, and every estimate of final disappearance remains uncertain.

## Ice and glacier are two different things

The tension in the November report comes from a distinction that everyday speech tends to blur. Ice is a material. A glacier is a system made of that material.

A glacier begins with snow. Snow falls on high ground, and in a cold enough place some of it survives through the warmer months. Year after year, new layers press down on older ones. The buried snow packs tighter, first into a dense, grainy substance that scientists call firn, and eventually into solid glacier ice. When the ice grows thick and heavy enough, its own weight makes it creep slowly downhill, bending and sliding the way a very stiff putty would under a heavy load. That movement is the signature of a living glacier.

Glaciologists keep a running tally of a glacier's gains and losses. On the gain side sits snow that lasts long enough to become ice, usually in a high, cold zone called the accumulation area. On the loss side sits melting, along with ice that evaporates directly into the air. This tally is the mass balance, and it decides whether a glacier grows or shrinks. Flow is a separate physical property. The U.S. National Snow and Ice Data Center explains that a glacier losing mass overall can keep moving, carrying ice downhill toward its lower end, called the terminus, even as that end retreats. The centre also separates a local accumulation zone, where snow builds up on the upper slopes, from the yearly balance of the glacier as a whole; a glacier can hold such a zone while losing mass overall. Shrinking and moving can therefore continue together for years. Persistent losses carry a longer-term consequence: they thin the ice, and ice that grows thin enough loses the weight it needs to keep creeping. NASA's account explains that an active glacier generally needs two things: enough thickness to flow under its own weight, and an accumulation area where snow builds up faster than it melts. These are scientific descriptions of how glaciers work. They describe physical behaviour, and they serve as a guide to understanding rather than an official checklist for a legal label.

Here is an everyday comparison, offered purely as illustration. Picture a slow queue at a ticket counter. People leave at the front as they are served, and new people join at the back, so the queue keeps shuffling forward. Suppose new arrivals slow while people keep leaving at the front. The queue grows shorter, yet for a long while it still shuffles forward, much as a shrinking glacier can keep flowing. Eventually only a handful of people remain, standing in roughly the same spot. Someone glancing across the room would see a cluster of people and assume the queue still works. Yet the queue has stopped functioning as a queue; it has become a few individuals waiting. The comparison has limits. Glacier ice moves through gravity and the slow deformation of crystals, and its gains and losses depend on weather and terrain, while people in a queue make their own choices. The useful idea it carries is the difference between material that remains and a process that continues.

That difference sits at the heart of the Puncak Jaya story. The ice remains. The process, according to the scientists NASA cites, has ended.

## What the pictures show, and when

NASA's report pairs two satellite images of the summit area. Lauren Dauphin of NASA Earth Observatory produced them from Landsat data supplied by the U.S. Geological Survey. The image labels carry two dates: November 3, 1988, and October 19, 2026. Both are false-color images, which means the colors are assigned to make features easy to tell apart rather than to show the scene as a human eye would see it. In this pair, light blue marks ice, brown marks exposed rock, white marks clouds and green marks vegetation.

Side by side, the two images show retreat and fragmentation. In 1988 the ice appears as larger connected bodies. In 2026 it appears as scattered pieces surrounded by brown rock. The picture is vivid, and its job is specific: it shows where ice appears on a given day. The 2026 image offers a visual comparison, and the report supplies the most recent measured area from a separate effort with an earlier date.

## Counting the ice: area

Measuring how much ice covers the ground takes careful mapping. NASA attributes the area figures to Permana and BMKG, who used satellite and aerial data. Their estimates put the ice at about 4.31 square kilometres in 1988 and about 0.09 square kilometres in September 2025.

A square kilometre is the space inside a square that measures one kilometre on each side. So in 1988 the ice covered a little more than four such squares. By September 2025 it covered less than a tenth of one. The 2025 figure works out to roughly 2 percent of the 1988 extent. Put another way, for every fifty patches of ground that held ice in 1988, about one still held ice in 2025.

The dates deserve a clear line of their own, because they explain why a fresh report carries a slightly older number. The first image dates from November 1988, and the first area estimate comes from 1988. The latest area estimate dates from September 2025. The latest image dates from October 19, 2026. A new aerial survey is planned for December 2026; if it is completed and its results are reported, they could update the area. The exact amount of ice covering the summit in 2026 therefore remains an open measurement. The 2025 estimate sits above zero, which confirms that ice remained on the mountain at that time.

## Counting the ice: thickness at one spot

Area describes the footprint of the ice. Thickness describes how deep it goes, and it is measured in metres at a particular place. NASA reports a thickness record from a primary monitoring site on a part of the ice called East Northwall Firn. At that site the ice measured about 32 metres thick in 2010, roughly the height of a ten-storey building. By 2023 it measured about 4 metres. By November 2024, the ice at that monitoring site had disappeared entirely.

That record is dramatic, and its scope is precise. It describes one location. Other patches across the summit have their own thicknesses, which this record leaves unmeasured, and the November 2024 loss applies to that single site. NASA's account also describes how fast the ice thinned during warm spells. During the strong El Niño of 2015–16, thinning ran at roughly five times the usual pace, about 5.3 metres in a single year. During 2023–24, the ice thinned by roughly 2 to 4 metres per year.

These figures describe thinning measured at specific points over specific periods. Area in square kilometres, thickness in metres and thinning in metres per year each count something different. Multiplying a point thickness by the mapped area would produce an invented total volume, and that kind of manufactured number would carry far more confidence than the evidence supports.

Each kind of evidence answers its own question. An image answers where ice appears on a given day. A mapped estimate answers how much ground the ice covers. A monitoring site answers how deep the ice runs at one spot. Evidence about flow and accumulation answers whether the system still works. The November report draws on all four kinds, each with its own date.

## Why shrinking speeds itself up

The mechanism behind the retreat helps explain why the remaining ice struggles to recover. NASA's account describes several linked effects.

When a large ice body breaks into smaller pieces, it gains edges. A single big sheet has a long outline relative to its size; a scatter of small patches has far more outline in total, and each edge meets the surrounding air and rock. More edges mean more places where warmth can attack the ice.

Fragmentation also uncovers bedrock. Fresh snow is bright white and reflects much of the sunlight that lands on it. Bare rock is dark and absorbs that sunlight as heat. As the ice retreats, more dark rock lies open to the sun, the rock warms, and the warmth reaches the nearby ice. Retreat therefore creates conditions that encourage further retreat.

Snow still falls on Puncak Jaya. NASA explains that on bare, warm rock the snow melts away quickly, so it lacks the time to pile up, compress and turn into new ice. A fresh snowfall can brighten the summit for a while, yet it fails to build the lasting supply a glacier needs. In the language of mass balance, the gains side of the ledger has largely closed while losses continue.

## Steady warming and sudden pushes

Two kinds of climate forces shape this story, and they work on different timescales.

The first is long-term warming. The 2019 *PNAS* study, led by Permana, identifies human-caused warming of the atmosphere as the broad underlying driver of the retreat. This warming builds steadily over decades and raises the baseline temperature around the summit.

The second is El Niño, a natural pattern in which the surface of the tropical Pacific warms for months at a time and shifts weather across much of the globe. The 2019 study describes El Niño as an episodic amplifier, a periodic push layered on top of the steady trend. NASA's account explains how such an episode can harm the Puncak Jaya ice. Warmer air raises the freezing level, the height above which temperatures stay cold enough for snow. When that level climbs toward or past the summit, precipitation shifts from snow toward rain. Rain adds water and warmth to the ice surface, and the reduced snow cover leaves less bright surface to reflect sunlight. Melting intensifies as a result.

According to NASA's November account, Permana says the current El Niño is accelerating the loss. Both sources describe these causes in clear physical terms. The precise share of the latest loss belonging to each process remains an open scientific question, and the evidence here supports the broad picture of a warming trend with periodic sharp pushes.

## Ice as a record of the past

The Puncak Jaya ice has value beyond its role as a glacier. Ice preserves a record of the conditions under which it formed. Layers of snow capture chemical traces of the air and water of their time, and scientists can read those layers like pages in a diary.

The 2019 study shows how researchers used that diary. Permana and colleagues, publishing online on December 9, 2019, analysed how the ice had shrunk in area and thinned over the previous eight years or so, and they documented the accelerated thinning during the 2015–16 El Niño. Their work also drew on two ice cores, each about 32 metres long, drilled all the way down to bedrock in mid-2010. Those cores allowed the team to reconstruct tropical Pacific climate variability from roughly 1964 to 2010. The study belongs to an earlier period, and its findings stand as historical research that gives context to the 2026 report.

The cores and the measurements already collected survive as scientific evidence. Researchers can return to them, reanalyse them with new methods and combine them with satellite and aerial observations. What shrinks along with the summit ice is the physical archive still sitting on the mountain, the material that future scientists might study in place or sample with new techniques. A changing natural record narrows, while the records already gathered remain on the shelf.

## The countdown, and its uncertainty

How long will visible ice last? NASA's account reports that satellite studies suggest the main glacial ice areas could disappear around 2030. Permana says BMKG's estimates could place that moment earlier, with the current El Niño accelerating the loss. These are scenarios built on current trends and assumptions about future weather. They carry real uncertainty, and they describe the main ice areas, leaving the fate of every last fragment as a separate matter. The planned December 2026 aerial survey, if it is completed and its results are reported, could sharpen the measured area. Future conditions will decide the actual timing.

That returns the story to its opening tension. Two statements hold at once. The October 2026 image still shows light blue patches on the summit. The scientists cited by NASA say those patches have stopped flowing and have lost the snowy supply that once fed them. The estimates for the main glacial ice areas point to around 2030, or possibly earlier, while the date when the last small fragment melts remains an open question. The loss of active glacier behaviour, according to the scientists NASA cites, describes the present.

So the question worth carrying away concerns which ending counts. If a glacier is a moving system that gathers snow at the top and carries ice downhill, then its most consequential threshold may already lie behind it, while a few silent remnants still catch the light. Many people will mark the end when the last patch melts and the summit turns fully brown in a satellite image. The scientists watching Puncak Jaya point to an earlier ending, the moment the queue stopped moving, even as a few figures still stand in line.
