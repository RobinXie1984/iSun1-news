# A New Satellite Stands Ready to Take Over the Sea-Level Record. Will Three Decades of Measurements Stay Comparable Through the Handoff?

Since the early 1990s, a series of satellites has followed the same ground track around Earth, measuring the height of the sea. Each spacecraft carries a radar instrument that bounces signals off the ocean and times their return. Together they have built one of the longest continuous records of how the ocean surface rises and falls. On November 16, 2026, NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California reported that the newest satellite in the series, Sentinel-6B, has passed its initial system checks and finished calibration. JPL dates that status to October 30. The satellite is now prepared to become the mission's reference, the measuring standard that other sea-level satellites check themselves against. JPL plans that handoff for January.

The reference role carries real weight. Several satellites measure sea height, and each instrument has its own small quirks. To make their readings fit together, scientists compare them with one trusted standard. Today that standard is Sentinel-6B's twin, Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich, which launched first and currently fills the role. The two spacecraft make up Copernicus Sentinel-6/Jason-CS, a sea-level mission run jointly by NASA and European partners. The letters CS stand for Continuity of Service. The name states the mission's purpose: keep a decades-long measurement going as old hardware gives way to new.

The dates contain a small puzzle. Sentinel-6B reached orbit on November 16, 2025, so JPL's report arrived exactly one year after launch. The satellite works, has been checked and calibrated, and still follows its twin in the reference role. Its promotion waits for January.

That waiting period is the heart of this story. Building a working satellite is one achievement. Getting a record more than three decades old to accept a new instrument as its standard is a second achievement, and it calls for patience, comparison and care. The suspense lies in that second step. Can a newcomer take the lead while the long line of sea-level numbers stays trustworthy across the switch?

## How a satellite measures the sea

Sentinel-6B carries a radar altimeter, a radio device for measuring height. It sends short pulses of radio energy straight down at the ocean and listens for each echo. JPL says it sends thousands of pulses every second. Radio waves travel at a known and enormous speed. So the time each pulse takes to reach the water and return gives the distance between the spacecraft and the sea surface below. Scientists call that distance the range.

The range gives the gap between the satellite and the water. NASA's sea-level portal explains that turning this gap into sea height requires two more ingredients.

The first is the precise position of the spacecraft: how high it sits above the centre of Earth, tracked very carefully along its orbit.

The second is a terrestrial reference frame. This is an agreed set of coordinates fixed to the planet. It works somewhat like the grid lines on a map that everyone shares so their measurements line up.

Roughly speaking, scientists subtract the range from the satellite's altitude and place the answer on that shared grid. The result is the height of the sea at that spot at that moment.

Each echo describes a narrow patch of ocean directly beneath the satellite. As the spacecraft travels, it builds a long line of readings. Over days, its repeating path lays down a web of lines across the oceans, and the picture fills in pass by pass.

The figure of thousands of pulses per second describes how busily the instrument samples the sea. Accuracy is a separate quantity, established through careful comparison. The two NASA accounts behind this article give the sampling rate and leave Sentinel-6B's precision unquantified.

That brings us to calibration. Calibration means relating one instrument's readings to an accepted standard, so that numbers from different devices mean the same thing.

An everyday comparison helps here, and it is purely an illustration. Imagine a town that has recorded its outdoor temperature every day for decades with a trusted thermometer. The time comes to install a replacement. Suppose the new thermometer happens to read half a degree warm. The town's long-term chart would then show a sudden step upward on the day of the changeover, and that step would come entirely from the equipment. A careful town runs both thermometers together for a while and learns exactly how their readings relate. Only then does it let the new one carry the chart. That comparison helps the town recognise a jump caused by the replacement itself. Explaining any later rise on the chart still calls for evidence about the weather and the town's surroundings.

The satellites measure sea height with radar, a very different task from reading air temperature, and their calibration involves far more than a side-by-side glance. The shared idea is what matters. A long record stays reliable when each replacement is tied carefully to the standard it succeeds.

## Three decades on one path

The sea-level series that Sentinel-6B joins began in the early 1990s. JPL describes the missions as following the same ground track through every generation. The ground track is the line traced on Earth's surface directly beneath the orbit. Returning to the same ocean locations again and again is what makes the record so valuable. When a satellite measures the same stretch of water this year that its predecessors measured years ago, scientists can compare like with like.

Over decades, small details add up. Ocean change unfolds gradually, so even a small offset between two instruments could matter. A step in the data on the day one satellite replaces another could look, on a graph, like a real shift in the ocean. Careful calibration across each handover lets scientists tell a genuine change in the water apart from a difference between machines. This practice reflects good measurement habits. It also shows the care built into the series. The earlier data remain the foundation the newcomer joins.

At present the twins fly in close formation along that shared track. Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich travels ahead, passing over each point on the path 30 seconds before Sentinel-6B arrives there. That half-minute is a gap in time between two spacecraft on one road. It describes how they are arranged. It is a separate matter from accuracy, warning time or radio delay. The formation places the two instruments over nearly the same water within half a minute of each other.

Other sea-level satellites fly their own orbits for their own purposes. Today they check their readings against Michael Freilich. Under JPL's plan, that job passes to Sentinel-6B in January.

## From readiness to handoff

Put in order, the milestones tell a clear story:

- **November 16, 2025:** Sentinel-6B launched.
- **The following months:** it went through initial system checks and calibration.
- **October 30, 2026:** JPL reports that this work was complete as of this date.
- **November 16, 2026:** JPL published that status.
- **January:** the reference role is planned to transfer. Because the report came in November 2026, this points to January 2027. The report names the month, and the specific day has yet to be published.

Each step has a different status. The launch and the calibration are completed events, as reported by JPL. The handoff is a plan. This article draws on JPL's mission report and NASA's explanation of the measurement method. The January handoff, the later orbit change and their benefits all remain prospective.

JPL also describes a step beyond January. At a later stage, Michael Freilich is expected to move into a different, accompanying orbit. Today the twins trace the same line, half a minute apart. Once they occupy distinct paths, the pair together can sample more of the ocean between passes. JPL expects this to improve their combined spatial coverage and resolution, which means more of the sea surface measured and finer detail in its patterns. The size of that gain remains to be measured, and the timing of the move awaits announcement.

Seen together, these plans show how the mission handles its own succession. The older satellite keeps the standard until its successor has been checked and calibrated. The successor then takes over the reference role. The older satellite is then expected to take on a supporting job that widens the pair's view. JPL presents this as coordinated engineering, a planned relay with each runner's role assigned in advance.

## What the record is used for

Sea height may sound like a narrow measurement, yet it carries a great deal of information. Water expands as it warms, so a patch of ocean that has absorbed heat stands slightly higher than cooler water around it. A map of sea height therefore offers clues about where heat is stored in the ocean. Other factors also affect height, so a single reading can point to several possible causes. Scientists combine many sources of evidence to tell those causes apart. JPL notes that the mission also measures waves and marine winds, which widens what the data can describe.

A current example shows the data at work. El Niño is a recurring pattern in which surface waters across the tropical Pacific become unusually warm, shifting weather in distant parts of the world. Because warm water expands, El Niño raises sea height across a broad area of the Pacific, and satellites like these can track that rise.

JPL reports that the mission's data are already in use during the 2026 El Niño, an event that oceanographers expect could prove historic. That description is an expert expectation. The event's final ranking will come from assessment after it has run its course. The data in use today come from the mission as it currently operates, with Michael Freilich in the reference role. Any effect of January's handoff lies ahead.

JPL also lists practical uses for the mission's data: hurricane forecasting, planning for infrastructure, and shipping. These uses are best understood as a chain:

1. Satellites gather observations.
2. Scientists and forecasting centres analyse those observations, usually alongside many other kinds of data, and produce forecasts or long-range assessments.
3. People such as planners, engineers, ship operators and emergency managers decide what to do.

Each link depends on the one before it, and each involves its own judgement. JPL's report describes the first link and the kinds of work the data support. Measuring the benefit at the end of the chain, such as a sharper forecast or a loss avoided, is a task for separate studies.

Scale matters too. A long ocean-height record describes broad change across ocean basins and across decades. The flood risk of one particular coast depends on that broad picture and also on local conditions: the shape of the shoreline, the tides, whether the land is rising or settling, and the defences already in place. A global measure of ocean height and a single town's flood forecast answer different questions. The town's forecast draws on far more local detail than a satellite track provides. The satellite record gives the large-scale backdrop against which those local questions are asked.

## The question the handoff answers

Seen this way, the January handoff concerns something easy to overlook: whether the measuring standard itself can be trusted. Most news about satellites centres on launches, which are dramatic and easy to picture. The quieter work comes afterward, in the months when engineers and scientists confirm that a new instrument reads the ocean in a way that fits the record it is joining. JPL's November report marks the completion of that groundwork for Sentinel-6B.

This brings the central question into focus. What lets a new measuring instrument continue a long record reliably? JPL's account shows part of the answer. The new satellite passed its initial system checks. It completed calibration. Its twin flies close by as the current standard. A month has been set for the transfer. Each element helps ensure that a change in the record reflects a change in the ocean, while the shift from one machine to another leaves the record intact.

The next facts to report will be confirmation that the transfer has taken place, followed later by evidence about the accompanying orbit and how much the pair's combined coverage improves. Those results will come from future mission updates and technical reports.

The sea-level record has lasted more than three decades because each generation of satellites has handed it carefully to the next. Every spacecraft eventually ages, so the record continues only as long as each newcomer is tied to the one before. By early 2027, if the plan holds, Sentinel-6B will be the instrument against which other sea-level satellites measure themselves. How smoothly that handover goes, and how much sharper the pair's view of the ocean becomes afterwards, are questions the coming months will answer.
