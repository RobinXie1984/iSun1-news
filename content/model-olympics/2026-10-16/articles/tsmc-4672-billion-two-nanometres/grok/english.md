Counter-intuitive: TSMC’s Strongest Quarter in Years Is Also Its Most Fragile

On October 15, 2026, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company released its results for the three months ended September 30. The numbers arrived the next day in the usual investor formats: a clean English-language release, tables in both New Taiwan dollars and U.S. dollars, and the standard list of risks that has accompanied every TSMC quarter for years. What the figures themselves show is unambiguous. Revenue reached NT$1,494.24 billion. Net income was NT$763.06 billion. In local-currency terms, revenue rose 50.9 percent from the same period a year earlier and net income rose 68.7 percent. Converted at the rates TSMC used, U.S.-dollar revenue stood at $46.72 billion, up 41.2 percent year-on-year. The two growth rates are not interchangeable; the company reports them separately for a reason.

Those are the facts as TSMC stated them. The rest of the story is how those facts sit inside a manufacturing system that remains, by its own description, exposed to the ordinary hazards of heavy industry: customer order patterns, capacity timing, technology transitions, natural disasters, and foreign-exchange movements.

Gross margin for the quarter was 66.4 percent. Operating margin was 58.7 percent. Net margin was 51.1 percent. The three percentages measure different things and should not be collapsed into a single “profitability” number. Of wafer revenue, 2-nanometer processes accounted for 8 percent, 3-nanometer for 33 percent, 5-nanometer for 28 percent, and 7-nanometer for 10 percent. Taken together, the 7-nanometer-and-below category represented 79 percent of wafer revenue. These are shares of revenue from wafers, not counts of chips shipped and not literal measurements of transistor gate length. The distinction matters because the economic value of a leading-edge wafer is not the same as the economic value of an older one, even when both leave the same clean room.

CFO Wendell Huang attributed the demand to leading-edge processes. Management’s outlook for the fourth quarter, released on the same day, called for revenue of $52.3 billion to $54.3 billion, a gross margin of 65–67 percent, and an operating margin of 56.5–58.5 percent, under the assumption that one U.S. dollar equals 31.8 New Taiwan dollars. Those ranges are forecasts. They are not revenue already earned and they are not guarantees of returns.

The tension the release itself surfaces is straightforward: physical manufacturing output and the revenue that accompanies it are growing strongly, yet the next phase of capacity ramp and the customer orders that would fill it still have to materialize. The document lists the usual cautions—cyclicality of the semiconductor industry, changes in customer orders, capacity constraints or under-utilization, technology transitions, natural disasters, and foreign-exchange fluctuations—without quantifying their probability or attaching them to any specific customer or product line. There is no verified share-price reaction in the material, no breakdown of revenue by individual customer, no figure isolating “AI-only” revenue, and no statement that all profits derive from artificial-intelligence workloads.

What can be said without leaving the document is that a company whose most advanced process nodes already generate the large majority of its wafer revenue has just reported the highest absolute profit figures in its recent history, while simultaneously reminding investors that the conditions which produced those figures are not permanent. The 79 percent advanced-node share is a snapshot of one quarter’s wafer mix. It does not describe the installed base of tools, the utilization of older lines, or the pace at which 2-nanometer capacity will be added in subsequent periods. It does indicate that, for the three months ended September 30, the economic weight of the company rested on processes that are still early in their volume lives.

That concentration is both the source of the margins and the reason the risk section remains lengthy. Leading-edge manufacturing requires continuous capital outlays for tools whose useful lives are measured in a handful of years. Once those tools are installed, they generate high incremental margins only while they are fully loaded with wafers that customers are willing to pay premium prices for. If orders slow, or if the next process node takes longer to yield, or if a natural event interrupts power or logistics in Hsinchu, Tainan, or any of the newer sites, the same high fixed-cost base that produced a 51.1 percent net margin can compress quickly. The release does not claim that any of those events is imminent. It simply declines to remove them from the list of factors that could affect future results.

The fourth-quarter guidance offers a concrete illustration of the same point. The midpoint of the revenue range is higher than the third-quarter U.S.-dollar figure, yet the gross-margin band begins at 65 percent—slightly below the 66.4 percent just reported—and the operating-margin band begins at 56.5 percent, also below the 58.7 percent just achieved. Management is therefore projecting continued growth while allowing for the possibility that mix, utilization, or costs will not remain at the levels that produced the third-quarter margins. The exchange-rate assumption is explicit: US$1 = NT$31.8. Any material movement away from that rate would alter the translation of U.S.-dollar guidance back into New Taiwan dollars, and vice versa. The company does not forecast the path of the currency; it simply states the rate it used.

None of this requires inventing scenes inside the fabs or attributing private emotions to executives. The public record is sufficient. A manufacturer whose wafer revenue is already dominated by 7-nanometer and more advanced nodes reported revenue growth of 50.9 percent in local currency and net-income growth of 68.7 percent. It then guided to a further sequential increase in U.S.-dollar revenue while flagging the standard industry risks and the ordinary uncertainties of capacity and demand. The absolute scale of the profit—NT$763.06 billion in a single quarter—makes the ordinary risks more, not less, consequential. At that size, even a modest percentage-point decline in utilization or a delay in the next node’s ramp moves billions of dollars.

The same scale also explains why the node mix is reported in the detail it is. When 3-nanometer alone accounts for a third of wafer revenue and the entire advanced cohort accounts for nearly four-fifths, small shifts in the composition of that cohort become material to the consolidated result. A customer that pulls in or pushes out a large leading-edge order changes the quarter’s mix more sharply than the same action would have when older nodes still formed a larger share of the total. The release does not name the customers or quantify the orders. It simply shows the outcome of whatever orders were executed: 8 percent at 2-nanometer, 33 percent at 3-nanometer, 28 percent at 5-nanometer, 10 percent at 7-nanometer.

CFO Huang’s attribution of demand to leading-edge processes is consistent with that mix. It does not, however, specify how much of the demand is incremental versus replacement, how much is concentrated in a small number of end markets, or how durable the demand is expected to be beyond the current guidance window. Those distinctions are left for subsequent periods to reveal. The third-quarter numbers capture only what was recognized as revenue between July 1 and September 30, 2026.

Investors and observers are therefore left with two simultaneous facts that do not contradict each other. First, the manufacturing system produced its strongest absolute profit figures in the recent record, driven by a wafer mix already weighted toward the newest commercially available nodes. Second, the same system continues to operate under the constraints and uncertainties that have always applied to advanced semiconductor manufacturing: the need for the next ramp to succeed, the dependence on customer orders that have not yet been placed or have not yet been confirmed for future quarters, and the exposure to events outside the company’s control. The release records both facts without attempting to resolve the tension between them.

That tension is the ordinary condition of a foundry whose economic results are now dominated by processes that are still climbing their volume curves. The 66.4 percent gross margin and the 51.1 percent net margin are the product of high utilization and favorable mix in one quarter. They are not a structural floor. The guidance ranges for the following quarter already incorporate a modest step-down in the margin bands while projecting higher revenue, which is one way of acknowledging that the conditions can change even if the absolute level of activity continues to rise. The risk factors listed in the release are the longer list of ways the conditions can change more sharply.

Nothing in the document claims that the current trajectory will persist indefinitely, nor does it claim that the trajectory is about to reverse. It reports what was earned, states what management currently expects for the next three months under a stated exchange-rate assumption, and reminds readers of the variables that remain outside the forecast. The variables include the ordinary cyclicality of the industry, the timing and volume of customer orders, the pace at which new capacity can be brought online and loaded, the success of technology transitions, the possibility of natural disasters, and movements in foreign-exchange rates. Each of those items has appeared in prior TSMC releases. Their continued presence after a quarter of 50.9 percent revenue growth and 68.7 percent net-income growth is itself part of the record.

The practical implication is limited but clear. Anyone reading the October 15 release is looking at a company that has just converted leading-edge wafer demand into the largest absolute profit figures of its recent history, while simultaneously declining to treat that conversion as permanent. The 79 percent advanced-node share of wafer revenue explains the margins. The same share, because it is still early in the volume lives of the newest nodes, also explains why the next ramp and the next set of customer orders remain material to whether those margins can be sustained. The document does not resolve which outcome is more likely. It simply supplies the numbers that make the question consequential.

What the third-quarter results therefore demonstrate, on the terms TSMC itself used, is that the physical act of manufacturing the most advanced chips currently available can generate extraordinary absolute profits when the tools are full and the mix is favorable. They also demonstrate that the company continues to describe the conditions required for those profits as subject to the ordinary risks of the business. The two statements sit side by side in the same release. Neither cancels the other.

What part of that tension do you think the next two quarters will clarify first—the durability of the leading-edge order book, or the pace at which the next node’s capacity can actually be filled?
