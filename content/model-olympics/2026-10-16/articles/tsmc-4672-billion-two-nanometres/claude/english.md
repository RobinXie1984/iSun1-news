# TSMC Q3 2026: Story-Driven Article

## Three Headline Options

1. **Suspenseful:** *TSMC Just Posted a Record-Shattering Quarter. The Real Test Starts in 75 Days.*
2. **Emotional Resonance:** *Every Screen You Touch Runs Through One Company. Here's What Its Biggest Quarter Yet Actually Means.*
3. **Counter-intuitive:** *TSMC Kept 51 Cents of Every Revenue Dollar as Profit. That Makes Its Next Quarter Harder.*

# TSMC Kept 51 Cents of Every Dollar. Now It Has to Do It Again.

On October 15, 2026, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company published its results for the quarter ended September 30.

Read one line and it looks like a strong quarter. Read the whole release and it looks like something rarer: a company whose factories, pricing power and technology lead all showed up in the same set of numbers at once.

Then you reach the last page, and the release reminds you that none of it is guaranteed.

This is a story about both halves: what TSMC earned, and what it still has to earn.

*(A note before we start: every figure below comes from TSMC's own disclosures, collected on October 16, 2026. They are not an independent audit, and this article is not a stock recommendation. Where I add my own arithmetic or interpretation, I label it as analysis.)*

---

## Part 1: The Number That Stops You

**NT$1,494.24 billion.**

That is TSMC's reported revenue for the third quarter of 2026. It is just short of one and a half trillion New Taiwan dollars in three months.

Net income was **NT$763.06 billion**.

Measured in NT dollars, revenue grew **50.9%** year-on-year and net income grew **68.7%**.

Look at the gap between those two growth rates. When profit grows faster than sales, the company is keeping more of each sale. That gap is the real headline of the quarter.

> **Takeaway #1:** Growth is good. Growth that widens your margins is a different category of result.

---

## Part 2: The Currency Trap Most Headlines Will Fall Into

TSMC also reported revenue in US dollars: **US$46.72 billion, up 41.2% year-on-year.**

So which is it, 50.9% growth or 41.2%?

Both are correct, because they measure different things:

- **50.9%** is revenue growth measured in **NT dollars**.
- **41.2%** is revenue growth measured in **US dollars**.

Mix them and you will get the story wrong. "US$46.72 billion, up 50.9%" is a sentence that sounds right and isn't.

**Analysis (my arithmetic, not a TSMC disclosure):** Dividing the reported NT-dollar revenue by the US-dollar revenue implies an average rate of roughly NT$32 per US dollar this quarter. Working the year-ago figures backward from the stated growth rates implies roughly NT$30 a year earlier. That suggests a weaker NT dollar lifted NT-dollar growth above US-dollar growth. This is a back-of-the-envelope inference from the disclosed numbers, not a figure TSMC reported.

The practical lesson is simple: pick a currency, then quote the growth rate for that currency.

> **Takeaway #2:** Before you compare two growth rates, check whether they're measured in the same currency.

---

## Part 3: Three Margins, Three Different Stories

TSMC reported three margins. They measure different things, so it's worth taking them one at a time.

**Gross margin: 66.4%.**
This is what remains after the direct cost of making wafers. For every NT$100 of revenue, about NT$66.40 was left after production costs.

**Operating margin: 58.7%.**
This also subtracts the cost of running the company, including research, development, sales and administration. About NT$58.70 of every NT$100 was left.

**Net margin: 51.1%.**
This is the bottom line, after everything else. A little over half of revenue became net income.

That is where the headline above comes from: **TSMC kept roughly 51 cents of every revenue dollar as profit.**

**Analysis:** That figure checks out. NT$763.06 billion divided by NT$1,494.24 billion is about 51.1%. For a business that runs enormous, expensive physical factories, keeping over half of revenue as net income is an exceptional result. It suggests customers are paying a premium for what only a few manufacturers can produce at scale.

> **Takeaway #3:** Revenue shows how much customers bought from you. Margin shows how hard it would be for them to buy it elsewhere.

---

## Part 4: Where the Money Actually Comes From

This part of the release is the one most likely to be misread.

TSMC broke down its **wafer revenue** by process technology:

| Process | Share of wafer revenue |
|---|---|
| 2nm | 8% |
| 3nm | 33% |
| 5nm | 28% |
| 7nm | 10% |
| **7nm and below (total "advanced")** | **79%** |

Two cautions apply before anyone builds a theory on this table.

**First, these are shares of wafer revenue, not chip counts.** If 33% of wafer revenue came from 3nm, that does not mean 33% of the chips TSMC made were 3nm chips. Advanced wafers cost more, so a smaller number of them can account for a larger share of the money.

**Second, "2nm" and "3nm" are names for process generations, not literal transistor sizes.** Nothing in a 3nm chip is necessarily three nanometers across. Treat the labels like model years.

Even with those cautions, the pattern is clear. **Nearly four of every five dollars of wafer revenue came from TSMC's most advanced processes.**

The newest node, 2nm, is already 8%. That is small next to 3nm's 33%, but every leading node starts small before it becomes the core of the business, and 3nm is the current example of that path.

**Analysis:** The money concentrates at the leading edge, which is also where competition is thinnest. That helps explain the margins in Part 3. Customers who need the most advanced process have the fewest alternatives.

> **Takeaway #4:** In this business, being one generation ahead is worth far more than being one generation cheaper.

---

## Part 5: What the CFO Said, and What He Didn't

According to the release, CFO **Wendell Huang** attributed the quarter's demand to TSMC's **leading-edge processes**.

That attribution matches the node breakdown above. It is also a narrower claim than the one many readers will want to make.

So, to be precise about the limits of the release:

- It **does not** state what share of revenue came from AI-related products.
- It **does not** say all, or most, of the profit came from AI.
- It **does not** reveal how capacity was split among individual customers.
- The source material includes **no verified share-price reaction** to the results.

You can believe AI demand played a large part in this quarter. Many people do, and it's a reasonable hypothesis. But the release doesn't quantify it, so this article won't either.

> **Takeaway #5:** "Leading-edge demand" and "AI demand" overlap, but they are not the same claim. Don't let one stand in for the other.

---

## Part 6: The Next 75 Days

Now we get to the tension in the story.

TSMC's management gave guidance for the fourth quarter of 2026:

- **Revenue:** US$52.3–54.3 billion
- **Gross margin:** 65–67%
- **Operating margin:** 56.5–58.5%
- **Exchange-rate assumption:** US$1 = NT$31.8

These are **forecasts**. They are not earned revenue, and they are not promised returns.

**Analysis (my arithmetic):** Compared with Q3's US$46.72 billion, the guidance range implies sequential growth of about **12% at the low end and about 16% at the high end**, or roughly **14% at the midpoint of US$53.3 billion**. Over a single quarter, that is a steep target.

The margin guidance is also telling. A gross-margin range of 65–67% puts this quarter's 66.4% inside the band, so management is signaling roughly steady profitability, not a sharp expansion. The operating-margin range of 56.5–58.5% sits mostly below this quarter's 58.7%. That doesn't mean something is wrong. It's a reminder that margins near record levels have limited room to keep rising.

Note the exchange-rate assumption as well: US$1 = NT$31.8. If the actual rate differs, NT-dollar results will differ too, even if the underlying business performs exactly as planned.

> **Takeaway #6:** A record quarter becomes the baseline the next one is measured against.

## Part 7: The Fine Print Is the Real Story

This is the part of the release that headlines usually skip.

TSMC's release warns about several risks:

- **Cyclicality.** The semiconductor industry has a long history of booms followed by downturns.
- **Customer orders.** Demand forecasts are not purchase orders, and orders can change.
- **Capacity.** Adding advanced capacity takes years and huge capital, and timing it against demand is difficult.
- **Technology.** Each new node has to be developed, ramped and produced at high yield.
- **Natural disasters.** Factories are physical, and so are the risks to them.
- **Foreign exchange.** As Part 2 showed, the currency alone can change how growth looks.

Put this list next to the numbers and the central tension becomes clear:

**What TSMC reported is real. What TSMC expects is not yet.**

The fabs ran, the wafers shipped and the revenue was recorded. That is the part already earned.

The next node ramp, the next round of customer demand and the next quarter's US$52–54 billion are all still in the future. They depend on customers continuing to order, on new processes ramping on schedule, and on factories operating without serious disruption.

**Analysis:** Strong results and real risk are not opposites. A company can report an exceptional quarter and still have results that depend heavily on a small set of advanced processes and on demand that has to keep showing up. In fact, the stronger the quarter, the more depends on the next one.

> **Takeaway #7:** Results tell you what has already happened. Guidance tells you what management expects. Treat them differently.

---

## Part 8: Why This Matters Beyond Taiwan

You may never buy a TSMC share. You almost certainly use products that contain its chips.

When a single manufacturer earns a 66.4% gross margin and gets 79% of its wafer revenue from its most advanced processes, it tells you where scarcity sits in the technology supply chain. Most of the hardware economy runs through a small number of extremely difficult manufacturing steps.

Scarcity creates pricing power, which supports margins and funds the next generation of technology. The cost is concentration: much of the industry depends on a small number of factories continuing to execute.

**Analysis:** That is why this earnings report reads as much like a map of the industry as a corporate result. It shows where value is captured, and it shows where the dependence lies.

## The Seven Lines Worth Remembering

1. **Profit grew faster than revenue.** Net income rose 68.7% against 50.9% revenue growth, both in NT dollars.
2. **Keep currencies separate.** 50.9% is NT-dollar growth. 41.2% is US-dollar growth.
3. **Margin reflects scarcity.** 66.4% gross, 58.7% operating, 51.1% net.
4. **79% of wafer revenue came from 7nm and below.** That is revenue share, not chip count.
5. **"Leading-edge" is not the same as "AI-only."** The release doesn't quantify an AI share.
6. **Q4 guidance of US$52.3–54.3 billion is a forecast,** based on an assumed rate of US$1 = NT$31.8.
7. **The record quarter becomes the new baseline.**
