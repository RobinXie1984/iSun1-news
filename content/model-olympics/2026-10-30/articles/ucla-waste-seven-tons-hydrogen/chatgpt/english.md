Suspenseful: UCLA’s Campus Waste Could Become Hydrogen. A Pilot Is Testing the Possibility.

Emotional Resonance: A Second Life for Campus Scraps: UCLA’s Waste-to-Hydrogen Experiment

Curious Question: Could UCLA’s Food Scraps and Plant Trimmings Help Power Its Campus?

At a university, food scraps and plant trimmings are part of the everyday work of feeding people and tending grounds. They also contain something useful: material made by living things, carrying energy and carbon gathered during growth. Engineers call this material biomass. Finding a practical way to use it could connect two familiar jobs—handling waste and supplying energy.

UCLA, the Los Angeles research university, estimates that its campus produces approximately seven tons of biomass waste a day. Its Samueli School of Engineering is working with Mote, a Los Angeles climate-technology company, to investigate what that stream could become. Their pilot-scale system aims to produce high-purity hydrogen, a fuel that can supply useful energy, while capturing carbon for storage.

The partnership brings a large question down to an approachable size: how much useful energy can ordinary campus waste provide, and what climate benefit would the whole process deliver?

Answering it starts with understanding what happens to the scraps.

Plants take in carbon dioxide from the air as they grow. Some of that carbon becomes part of their stems, leaves and wood. Food scraps also contain material that began with living things. That makes a pile of biomass both a potential energy resource and a temporary home for carbon.

The process UCLA and Mote are testing is called gasification. It converts biomass into a mixture of gases that can be processed to obtain hydrogen.

As a simple analogy, imagine taking apart a mixed box of building blocks and sorting the pieces for new uses. Gasification performs a chemical version of that task: the original material is broken down, and further processing separates useful components. Hydrogen is one desired product. Capturing carbon and directing it toward storage is another part of the project’s intended process.

The analogy explains the basic idea. The engineering lies in making the transformation work consistently with the material that actually arrives.

Campus waste varies. A batch rich in woody plant trimmings can behave differently from one containing more food scraps. Researchers need to understand how the incoming material affects the system and how to adjust its operation. The photograph accompanying UCLA’s reporting shows wood chips at Mote’s Los Angeles pilot plant—a concrete glimpse of the material behind the larger ambition.

Associate professor Dante Simonetti leads the UCLA team. Testing and evaluation have been underway since April 2026. UCLA’s engineering school described the project on October 22, 2026, followed by a campus newsroom report on October 29. The 18-month pilot is scheduled to continue through September 2027, with a possible extension.

That chronology matters. The October reports describe an experiment already in progress. Its current work involves studying how different inputs behave, improving the process and evaluating system performance. Those measurements will help determine how the idea could serve the campus.

UCLA’s projections offer several ways to picture the potential.

According to the team’s estimates, hydrogen from the campus biomass stream could supply about 2% of UCLA’s electricity. Another projected use would provide energy during periods of peak demand for several hundred hours each year. Alternatively, the hydrogen could fuel approximately 100 hydrogen vehicles.

Each figure describes a different possible use of the estimated resource. Choosing one would allocate hydrogen toward that purpose. The projections give the research a useful scale: a modest contribution to a large university’s electricity supply, a resource for selected high-demand hours, or fuel for a vehicle fleet.

Actual campus-wide output remains a subject for measurement. The pilot is the place where estimates meet equipment, variable materials and operating conditions.

The carbon story follows the same path from possibility to measurement.

Because plants absorbed carbon while growing, storing that carbon durably could keep some of it out of the atmosphere while hydrogen provides energy. This is the project’s central climate aim. Its success depends on the entire journey, including the resources used to process the biomass and the eventual handling and storage of the captured carbon.

Think of a household budget. A payment coming in tells you something useful; the amount left after expenses tells you what the household gained. Climate accounting similarly weighs carbon kept out of the atmosphere against emissions generated along the way.

For this project, independently audited totals covering the full process remain to be established. Verification of a complete campus-wide carbon-storage chain is also an open task. The university’s reporting describes the capture and storage ambition, while the eventual net climate benefit depends on those measurements and durable storage.

This gives the pilot a second job alongside producing hydrogen: discovering what the process delivers as a whole.

The partnership also has a very human scale. Mote is contributing $100,000 toward stipends, tuition and supplies for two chemical-engineering graduate students. Their research covers the behavior of incoming biomass, process improvements and system performance.

Two students, a working pilot and a stream of familiar waste make an unusually tangible research story. The work connects classroom chemistry with the practical questions that determine whether a system can become useful: how it behaves, how reliably it performs and what it takes to run.

The eventual cost of hydrogen, the amount produced from a given quantity of material, and arrangements for transport and storage remain open questions. Each will help shape the choice of where the hydrogen could do the most useful work. A campus electricity application and a vehicle-fueling application would place different demands on the same resource.

For now, UCLA and Mote have an ongoing experiment, a defined research period and a practical source of material to study. Their projected benefits explain why the experiment deserves attention. The measurements will establish how far that promise travels.

A university’s daily scraps are easy to see as the end of a journey. This pilot explores a further chapter: useful fuel, stored carbon and engineering knowledge drawn from material the campus already produces.

By the scheduled close of the pilot in September 2027, the question is how much of that second life the team can demonstrate—and which use would make the best contribution to the campus that supplied the waste.
