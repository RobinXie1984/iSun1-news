Curious Question: Can Seven Tons of Daily UCLA Waste Become Useful Hydrogen While Carbon Stays Stored?

At UCLA, a large research university in Los Angeles, the ordinary stream of campus waste is now part of an active engineering experiment. Food scraps, plant trimmings, and similar biomass—material that comes from living things—arrive each day. The Samueli School of Engineering, working with Mote, a Los Angeles climate-technology company, is running a pilot-scale gasification system that processes this material. The project seeks high-purity hydrogen together with carbon capture and storage. Associate Professor Dante Simonetti leads the UCLA team. The work links a university’s practical waste stream to research on energy options that keep captured carbon out of the air.

Testing and evaluation have been underway since April 2026. An engineering report appeared on October 22, 2026, and a campus newsroom story followed on October 29, 2026. The 18-month pilot is scheduled to continue through September 2027, with a possible extension. At Mote’s Los Angeles pilot plant, wood chips serve as one of the feedstocks, showing the kind of plant material the system handles under measured conditions.

Here is a simple analogy for the gasification step: picture a closed pot on a stove where wood chips heat slowly under carefully controlled air. In an open campfire the same wood burns fully into ash and smoke. In the closed-pot picture, useful gases leave first. Engineers then clean and separate those gases to obtain high-purity hydrogen, while the remaining carbon can be captured for storage. The real pilot operates at larger scale with precise controls, yet the idea of controlled heating that releases gases helps show the mechanism.

UCLA estimates its campus biomass waste at approximately 7 tons per day. Team projections describe several alternative uses for hydrogen from such a stream: it could supply about 2 percent of UCLA’s electricity, or provide peak-period energy for several hundred hours each year, or fuel approximately 100 hydrogen vehicles. These remain projections based on the estimated volume. The present stage centers on pilot testing of feedstock behavior, system optimization, and overall performance. Actual campus-wide output, net carbon removal, and adopted economics remain open for measurement.

Plant material captured carbon from the air as it grew. The project’s carbon-storage aim seeks to keep that carbon out of the atmosphere while the hydrogen supplies useful energy. Net climate benefit depends on the full process and durable storage. Independently audited lifecycle totals and a verified campus-wide storage chain remain open for measurement. Mote supports the effort with $100,000 toward stipends, tuition, and supplies for two chemical-engineering graduate students, enabling hands-on research on feedstock behavior, optimization, and system performance.

The ongoing pilot measures how ordinary campus waste behaves in the system and what hydrogen it can yield under controlled conditions. Exact yields, transport methods, storage sites, commercial prices, and guaranteed outcomes stay open. The measurements continuing through September 2027 will clarify the practical possibilities.
