Curious Question: What Does a Restaurant’s $0-a-Month Offer Really Cost?

A restaurant has two kinds of work happening at once. There is the meal itself: ingredients, cooking, service. Then there is the machinery of getting paid and keeping the business running: orders, card payments, software subscriptions and payroll.

Toast supplies technology for that second layer. Its point-of-sale system—the equipment and software used to take orders and payments—can sit at the center of a restaurant’s daily operations. The company also offers payment processing, additional services such as payroll, and financing through Toast Capital. Bringing these functions together can give an owner one connected system for several everyday jobs.

That connection also makes the price of the relationship more complicated. An owner may encounter one cost when signing up, another each time a customer pays, and further obligations when deciding to leave.

On October 29, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission, the U.S. agency responsible for consumer protection and competition enforcement, joined California and seven other states in filing a civil lawsuit against Toast and its affiliates. Their complaint alleges hidden or unauthorized fees, barriers to cancellation and misleading promises about support.

The case places a practical question before a federal court: how clearly did Toast explain the financial commitments attached to its services, and how did it obtain agreement to those charges?

For restaurant owners, that question reaches across the whole relationship—from the first attractive offer to the final bill.

The 71-page complaint was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. Its claims are allegations for the court to decide. The plaintiffs are seeking remedies through the lawsuit. The source record collected on October 30 leaves Toast’s response and the eventual ruling unknown.

To understand the dispute, start with a familiar distinction: a monthly bill can belong to a much longer agreement.

According to the complaint, Toast offered annual and two-year software subscriptions that customers paid for monthly. The complaint describes average monthly charges of roughly US$900, depending on the number of terminals and additional services. A terminal is the device staff use to enter an order or take a payment.

The billing rhythm matters because it shapes how people experience a purchase. A charge arrives each month, while the contractual commitment may run for a year or two. The amount on the monthly statement tells an owner what is being collected now. The agreement determines how long that obligation lasts.

Another arrangement, called Pay-as-you-Go, offered hardware and software at US$0 per month alongside higher transaction fees. Under that structure, more of the price is collected through payment activity.

The complaint alleges additional conditions associated with that offer. Restaurants whose payment processing failed to begin within 120 days could face a requirement to return the hardware or pay its retail value. A temporary closure could bring a US$85 monthly processing charge. Early termination could trigger a charge of US$150 for each remaining contract month.

Those charges concern different events. The hardware condition relates to getting started. The temporary-closure charge relates to a pause in operations. The early-termination charge relates to ending the agreement ahead of schedule. The plaintiffs describe them as obligations facing restaurant customers under the arrangements challenged in the complaint.

Here, the advertised monthly price is one part of a larger financial relationship. The lawsuit asks whether the rest of that relationship was presented and authorized properly.

A separate part of the case concerns a much smaller amount paid by diners: US$0.99.

According to the complaint and the FTC announcement, Toast retained a 99-cent fee on certain online orders above US$10 from September 2021 through late July 2023. The complaint describes the fee’s expansion by June 2023 and its placement within a “Taxes & Fees” category, with further disclosure available through a tooltip—an extra explanation a customer can open or reveal on the screen.

This was a historical fee from 2021–2023. The October 2026 event is the filing of the lawsuit examining that conduct.

The distinction between who paid and who received the money is central. Diners paid the fee on qualifying online orders, and Toast retained it. The dispute includes what both diners and restaurants understood and agreed to.

A checkout page has a small amount of space to explain several kinds of charges. Taxes go to public authorities. Other fees can go to a restaurant or a technology provider. Grouping amounts together may keep the screen compact, while the wording and extra disclosures determine how readily a customer can understand where the money goes.

In this case, the plaintiffs challenge the consent and disclosure surrounding the charge. The court will assess those allegations.

The cancellation claims bring the story back to the restaurant owner.

The complaint alleges a requirement for written notice at least 30 days ahead of renewal when a restaurant wanted its subscription to end. It also describes customers being passed between staff during cancellation attempts and charges continuing as they tried to leave.

One cited example involves a termination fee of US$5,318.87. That figure belongs to an individual allegation in the complaint; it illustrates a specific disputed experience.

Financing adds another connection. According to the plaintiffs, customers with Toast Capital balances could face accelerated repayment if they canceled their subscriptions or changed providers. Accelerated repayment means the remaining balance becomes due promptly, bringing a future obligation forward.

Think of the linked services as several appliances plugged into one power strip. A single connection makes the arrangement convenient. Switching off that connection affects everything attached to it. In a restaurant’s financial life, the links are contractual: changing the system used to take payments may also affect a software agreement or the timing of a financing repayment.

That analogy explains why an exit clause deserves attention alongside an opening price. Each service has its own purpose, while the agreements can join their consequences together.

From an owner’s perspective, moving to another provider could therefore become several decisions at once. There is the operational task of changing equipment and software. There is the subscription’s remaining term. There may also be money borrowed through the same provider, with repayment conditions tied to the relationship.

This is the broader significance of the case. A connected restaurant platform can reach from the counter where a diner pays to the account from which the owner meets business expenses. The meaning of its price unfolds across time: joining, operating, pausing, renewing and leaving.

The FTC and the states allege that Toast’s practices made important parts of those commitments deceptive or difficult to navigate. Those claims now await judicial assessment, and the remedies remain requests before the court.

For anyone trying to understand the business model, the underlying question is already clear: when one system handles so much of a restaurant’s working life, how clearly can the owner see the full financial commitment—from opening the account to closing it?
