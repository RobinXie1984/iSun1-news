Suspenseful: Federal and State Officials Open a Court Fight Over the Fees and Exit Terms Inside Restaurant Software Subscriptions

Emotional Resonance: Restaurant Owners and the Everyday Price of a System That Handles Orders, Payments, Payroll, and Loans

Curious Question: How Should Restaurant Owners Read the Entry Costs, Monthly Charges, and Exit Terms When One Platform Runs the Whole Operation?

Toast supplies many restaurants with the point-of-sale system that takes orders, accepts payments, tracks inventory, and connects to add-ons such as payroll and financing. Point-of-sale means the hardware and software that sit at the counter or in the kitchen and handle the transaction between a customer and the restaurant. The company also offers payment processing and Toast Capital financing products. On October 29, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission—the U.S. agency that enforces consumer-protection and competition rules—joined California, acting through the Los Angeles County Counsel and the San Francisco City Attorney, and the attorneys general of Colorado, Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Nebraska, Oklahoma, and Utah to file a civil lawsuit against Toast Inc. and affiliated companies. The Commission authorized the filing by a 2–0 vote. The 71-page complaint, Case 3:26-cv-12705 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, alleges hidden and unauthorized fees, barriers to cancellation, and misleading statements about customer support. The court will decide the allegations. The complaint requests remedies; as of the October 29, 2026 announcement and the filed complaint, a verified company response and the eventual ruling remain for later stages of the case.

The bigger picture is that a restaurant’s daily work often runs through one integrated platform. Orders, card payments, staff scheduling, payroll filings, and even a financing balance can sit inside the same subscription and the same hardware. That arrangement can simplify operations the way a single supplier might furnish a food truck with its ovens, registers, payment terminals, and a small loan for upgrades under one agreement. The lawsuit focuses on the terms that govern how that agreement begins, continues, and ends.

According to the complaint, restaurants enroll in auto-renewing annual or biennial software subscriptions that are billed monthly. The average cost runs around $900 a month, varying with the number of terminals and add-ons such as timekeeping or payroll processing. The complaint further describes a “Pay-as-you-Go” offer that advertises $0-per-month hardware and software in exchange for higher fees on each transaction processed through Toast. The plaintiffs allege that the offer includes conditions that appear in the agreement: if payment processing fails to begin within 120 days, the restaurant returns the hardware or pays its full retail value; a temporary closure still carries an $85 monthly processing charge; and early termination carries a $150 charge for each remaining month of the contract. These are pleaded claims about how the terms operate, and the court will determine their effect.

Separately, the complaint addresses a 99-cent fee that Toast retained on certain online orders above $10 from September 2021 through late July 2023. By June 2023 the fee sat inside a “Taxes & Fees” line with an additional tooltip disclosure. The lawsuit treats that historical fee as part of the broader dispute over disclosure and consent; it remains a past practice under the complaint, distinct from the subscription and cancellation terms now before the court.

Cancellation receives particular attention. The complaint alleges that ending the subscription requires 30 days’ written notice of an intent not to renew, followed by handoffs among teams, continued charges during the process, and, for customers with Toast Capital balances, accelerated repayment—meaning the remaining loan balance becomes due promptly if the restaurant cancels the subscription or switches providers. The complaint cites one example in which a customer faced a $5,318.87 termination fee. These details appear as allegations in the filed complaint, and the court will assess the evidence.

The October 29, 2026 filing therefore places the practical mechanics of an integrated restaurant platform under judicial review. Entry can look like a low or zero monthly hardware charge paired with transaction fees. Ongoing costs accumulate through the monthly subscription and any add-ons. Exit terms, as alleged, link the software subscription to hardware return conditions, continuing processing charges, early-termination amounts, and, where Toast Capital is involved, accelerated loan repayment. The case status is pending. Restaurant owners who rely on a single system for orders, payments, and financing now face the concrete question of how clearly those three layers—the cost to start, the cost to keep running, and the cost to leave—appear in the documents they sign and the bills they receive.
