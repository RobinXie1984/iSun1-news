# Every 113 Days or So, a Giant Star Erupts. Timed X-rays Point to a Hidden Partner

In a spiral galaxy called NGC 4559, roughly 30 million light-years away, a giant star keeps flaring. Each eruption drives its visible light toward the brilliance astronomers usually associate with a supernova, the explosion that ends a massive star's life. Then the star settles down and carries on. Astronomers catalogue the object as AT2016blu. On November 9, 2026, NASA and the Chandra X-ray Center, which runs science operations for NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, reported a likely driver of those flares: a small, extremely dense companion that appears to pull in gas from the giant star at regular intervals. The study behind the announcement, led by astronomer Mojgan Aghakhanloo, first appeared as a public preprint on August 20, 2026. NASA reports that it was published in *The Astrophysical Journal* on November 9. The crucial X-ray observations were made earlier, in March 2026.

A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion kilometers. The light that reached telescopes this year therefore set out from NGC 4559 roughly 30 million years ago. At that distance, a single star appears as a faint point inside a smudge of galaxy. Astronomers can follow it only through careful, repeated measurements of its brightness and through the different kinds of light it gives off.

The main star in AT2016blu belongs to a rare group called luminous blue variables, or LBVs. These are hot, extremely massive stars whose brightness changes, sometimes dramatically. The research team estimates that this one contains at least 33 times as much matter as the Sun. That figure describes mass, the amount of material in the star, and is separate from its size. Stars this heavy shed gas into space through strong winds and occasional violent eruptions. Some LBV eruptions grow as bright as supernovae while the star survives. Astronomers place these events in a category with a playful name: supernova impostors. The label describes the eruption itself. The star's eventual fate remains a separate question, and a star that has produced impostor events can still explode as a true supernova later in its life.

AT2016blu stood out because its eruptions kept a rhythm. According to the study, telescopes recorded 27 outbursts in visible light between 2012 and June 2026. Astronomers call visible light "optical" light, the same kind of light human eyes detect. The outbursts arrived roughly 113 days apart. Both numbers carry qualifications. The 27 are the outbursts that telescopes caught. Monitoring of any single galaxy happens in stretches, so some cycles may have slipped past between observations. The 113-day spacing is an approximate pattern, which the authors describe as quasi-periodic, meaning nearly regular. It gives astronomers an expected window for the next eruption and leaves room for variation from one cycle to the next.

A nearly regular beat suggests something mechanical, such as one object circling another. Visible light, though, leaves several stories open. A repeating flare could come from changes deep inside the star, or it could come from an outside influence such as a partner. The flares show *when* something happens. They reveal much less about *what* is happening. Astronomers therefore faced a practical question: could the rhythm itself help them catch the cause in the act?

## A schedule becomes a test

The long record of flares came from patient watching. The Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, a robotic telescope built to search for exploding stars, contributed brightness measurements. So did the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, which uses a network of telescopes around the world to follow stellar explosions. Observers working with the American Association of Variable Star Observers (AAVSO) added further measurements. NASA reports that more than 30 amateur astronomers took part. Year after year, these measurements built a timeline of the star's ups and downs. That timeline turned the flares into something astronomers could anticipate.

Anticipation mattered because X-ray telescopes are scarce and heavily used. X-rays from space are absorbed high in Earth's atmosphere, so X-ray telescopes have to operate in orbit. Chandra, launched in 1999, studies one small patch of sky at a time, and astronomers around the world compete for its observing hours. One tool for catching events that come and go is a Target of Opportunity request. A team arranges in advance to point the telescope at a chosen object once a specified trigger occurs. For AT2016blu, the trigger was the approach of another expected outburst. The team followed the star's brightness around the predicted eruption and activated Chandra observations in March 2026.

The dates show how tightly the pieces fit. In an image comparison released with the announcement, NASA presents a visible-light view of the star in a quiet state on March 14 and a view during the outburst on March 25. Chandra observed between March 24 and March 27. The preprint reports X-ray detections in five closely spaced observations during the campaign. These were five separate snapshots across a few days, so the X-ray record is a short series of measurements over that stretch. The published accounts give the observation dates. A moment-by-moment match between each X-ray change and each visible-light change lies beyond what they report.

The March detections gained added weight from an older comparison. Chandra had already looked at this part of NGC 4559 in 2001 and 2002. At the star's position, those exposures recorded a blank. From that blank, the team estimated an upper limit on the source's X-ray brightness at the time. According to the preprint, the team built this estimate with computer simulations. They placed an imaginary trial source at the star's position, simulated Chandra's observations of it, and lowered the trial brightness step by step until 95 percent of the simulated observations produced one X-ray count or less. That brightness became the upper limit. The calculation also adopts a representative 2002 instrument response, a standard model of how Chandra responded to incoming X-rays at that time. The limit is therefore an estimated observing constraint that rests on these statistical and instrument assumptions. In March 2026, Chandra clearly detected the source. Measured against that estimated archival limit, NASA reports that the new X-ray brightness was at least 100 times greater.

The phrase "at least" carries real information. The older observations supply a calculated threshold. If the authors' simulations and instrument model describe the 2001–2002 observations well, the source's brightness then sat near or below that threshold. The true brightness back then remains undetermined. A comparison against the threshold therefore gives a minimum increase under those assumptions, and the actual rise in X-ray brightness could be considerably larger than 100 times. The comparison concerns X-ray output alone; the star's mass and its brightness in visible light are separate quantities. Those 2001–2002 exposures also predate the visible-light record that begins in 2012, which adds to the uncertainty about the star's condition at that time.

Detecting X-rays was one challenge. Showing that they came from AT2016blu was another. A separate bright X-ray source sits close to the star on the sky. From 30 million light-years away, two such sources could easily blur into one glow, much as two distant car headlights merge into a single light at night. Chandra has unusually sharp vision, which astronomers describe as high angular resolution. That sharpness allowed the team to separate the two sources and assign the March X-rays to AT2016blu itself.

## Following the gas

Different kinds of light carry information about different conditions. A stove burner shows this in a simple way: as it heats, it shifts from dark to a dull red glow and then to a brighter orange. In space, X-rays come from matter far hotter than anything on a stove, heated to extremes typical of material falling toward very dense objects. Visible light comes from gentler conditions. During the March 2026 campaign, around the time of a visible flare, Chandra detected X-rays from the star. Observations of the same spot in 2001–2002 had yielded only upper limits. The five March snapshots record X-rays present during the campaign; the moment the X-ray output rose remains unknown. Together, the March detections and the older limits gave the team a clue about what was happening during an eruption.

The explanation the team favors unfolds in a series of steps. The giant star continually sheds gas into the space around it. A compact companion circles the giant on an eccentric orbit, meaning an oval path, so the distance between the two shrinks and grows during each lap. When the companion swings closest, it passes through denser gas and captures some of it. Astronomers call this process accretion: material gathers and falls inward under gravity. As the captured gas falls toward the companion, it speeds up, squeezes together and becomes intensely hot. That heated gas, located outside the compact object, gives off X-rays. Thick gas surrounding the system then absorbs much of that energetic radiation and releases part of the energy again as visible light. Telescopes on Earth register that visible light as the flare. In this picture, each close pass sets off a new episode, which would account for the roughly 113-day rhythm.

The last step, in which X-ray energy reappears as visible light, has an everyday parallel. This is an illustration only. On a sunny afternoon, dark pavement absorbs sunlight. After sunset, a hand held just above it can still feel warmth. Heat is the name for energy moving from warmer things to cooler ones, and it travels in several ways. Some of that warmth arrives as infrared radiation, a kind of light beyond what human eyes can see, which warm objects give off across a spread of wavelengths. Some arrives through convection, as air warmed by the pavement rises toward the hand. The radiation part carries the useful idea: energy that arrived largely as sunlight leaves partly as infrared light, a longer-wavelength form of radiation. In the proposed picture for AT2016blu, energy arrives in the surrounding gas as X-rays and leaves partly as visible light, so the flare that telescopes see on Earth is partly a transformed echo of the hidden feeding.

A compact object is the collapsed remnant of a star that has already died. Two kinds fit the evidence here. One is a neutron star, a ball of matter heavier than the Sun packed into roughly the width of a city. The other is a stellar-mass black hole, a remnant whose gravity is so strong that light inside its boundary stays trapped. In either case, the X-rays would come from hot gas swirling outside the object. The study treats both as live possibilities. The companion's identity remains open, and its mass remains unmeasured in the reported work. With both members introduced, the system can be placed in a familiar class. The authors describe it as a high-mass X-ray binary: a massive star that supplies gas, paired with a compact object that captures it and shines in X-rays.

The evidence comes in two layers. The first layer consists of measurements: years of timed visible flares, X-ray detections in five March observations, a sharp separation from the neighboring X-ray source, and a minimum rise compared with the older estimated limit. The second layer is interpretation. The oval orbit, the capture of gas and the conversion of X-rays into visible light form the model that ties those measurements together. The telescopes recorded light. The movement of gas between the two objects is an inference drawn from that light. The giant star survived every recorded episode while continuing to lose gas to space, and the companion takes only a portion of the gas the star sheds.

## A narrow first, and a wider lead

The authors describe AT2016blu as the first known supernova-impostor eruption from a luminous blue variable that is powered by intermittent accretion onto a compact object. The claim is specific. Astronomers have studied X-ray binaries for decades, and LBVs have their own long history of observation. The new element is this particular combination: an impostor-style eruption from an LBV, repeating on a schedule and traced to a partner that feeds intermittently. The words "first known" reflect the authors' reading of current records, and a similar system could still turn up in older data or future surveys.

NASA's headline speaks of finding the source of the outbursts. The underlying study offers strong, carefully timed support for one favored explanation, while keeping the companion's identity open. NASA's article and the Chandra X-ray Center release both describe the same paper. Their agreement therefore reflects one team's analysis. Independent tests would come from further observations and from other researchers working with the data.

NASA also calls AT2016blu a candidate ultra-luminous X-ray source, a label for unusually powerful X-ray emitters. The word "candidate" marks that classification as provisional. The study's proposed mechanism may help astronomers think about other erupting massive stars and other bright X-ray systems. For now, that connection is a research lead. Each of those systems would need its own evidence.

The method may prove as useful as the result. A long record of brightness measurements, built partly by volunteers, turned a puzzling series of flares into a forecast. The forecast allowed astronomers to request scarce X-ray time at the right moment. Chandra's sharp vision then pinned the signal to the right star. Each part answered a different need: the timeline supplied the timing, X-rays revealed extremely hot material, and high resolution identified its location. Together, they made an idea about invisible gas flows testable at a distance of 30 million light-years.

The reports focus on the flares. The giant star's eventual fate and any scheduled follow-up campaigns lie outside their scope. The roughly 113-day pattern describes the star's recorded behavior. If that pattern continues, each further flare would offer astronomers another opportunity to observe the system. If they can map the oval orbit precisely, weigh the hidden companion and track how much gas it captures from one pass to the next, will AT2016blu's partner turn out to be a neutron star or a black hole, and will every close approach follow the same chain of events?
