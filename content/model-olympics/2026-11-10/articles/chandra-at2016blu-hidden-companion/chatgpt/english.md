# A Star Keeps Erupting Roughly Every 113 Days. Its X-rays Point to a Hidden Companion

A massive star in the spiral galaxy NGC 4559 keeps brightening in episodes that resemble a supernova, then survives to brighten again. Astronomers studying the system, called AT2016blu, have now caught X-rays during one of those outbursts. Reports released by NASA and the Chandra X-ray Center on November 9, 2026, describe evidence for a compact companion whose gravity may help power the recurring display. The discovery connects two familiar parts of a star’s story—shining and shedding material—with a less visible process: gas falling toward a nearby object and becoming intensely hot.

The system lies roughly 30 million light-years away. A light-year measures the distance light travels in a year, so the signals reaching our telescopes began their journey long before human civilization. The principal star is a luminous blue variable: a hot, massive star whose brightness changes. Its estimated mass is at least 33 times the Sun’s mass. That figure describes how much matter the star contains; its physical dimensions are a separate measurement.

Such a star offers astronomers a difficult puzzle. An increase in visible light tells them that something has changed, while the cause can remain hidden within the unresolved system. The star’s own activity, material surrounding it, and interactions with a companion can all influence what reaches a telescope. Establishing which process supplies the energy requires observations that reveal more than brightness alone.

AT2016blu provided an unusually useful clue: repetition. Mojgan Aghakhanloo and coauthors report 27 detected outbursts in visible light between 2012 and June 2026, with an approximate recurrence of 113 days. The pattern is quasi-periodic, meaning that it has a recognizable rhythm with variations in timing. The count records the events observers detected across that interval; observing coverage leaves the total number of eruptions open.

That rhythm changed the practical task. Astronomers could watch for activity around an anticipated return and arrange another kind of observation while the system was brightening. A repeating mystery offered a chance to prepare.

The central question became more specific: could the visible eruptions be connected to hot material falling toward a compact companion?

Astronomers call the observed episodes “supernova impostors.” A supernova is a catastrophic stellar explosion; the impostor label describes an eruption with a similar appearance whose star survives the observed event. In AT2016blu, survival through repeated episodes is central to the mystery. The classification describes those episodes, while the star’s eventual fate remains a separate question.

Visible light supplies the long record of those appearances. X-rays offer access to a different part of the physics. They carry more energy than visible light and can reveal extremely hot matter. In the proposed explanation for AT2016blu, that matter lies near a companion with strong gravity. Observing X-rays during an optical outburst therefore gives astronomers a way to investigate the energy source behind the visible display.

An everyday analogy helps explain the observing strategy. Imagine hearing a recurring sound through a wall in a building. Its rhythm tells you when to listen closely. A second sensor, able to distinguish signals from neighboring machines, helps identify the equipment involved. You then use both records to infer how the machinery operates. This is an illustration: for AT2016blu, visible-light monitoring supplied the rhythm, and carefully timed X-ray observations supplied another physical clue.

Building that first record required years of attention. NASA credits the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, the Las Cumbres Observatory Global Supernova Project, and observations contributed through the American Association of Variable Star Observers, or AAVSO. More than 30 amateur astronomers contributed, according to NASA. Their measurements helped turn a series of brightenings into a pattern useful for planning.

That contribution matters because telescope scheduling is part of the scientific problem. An X-ray observation taken during a quiet interval answers a different question from one taken during an eruption. Long-term optical monitoring helps researchers identify the moment when a limited observing opportunity has the greatest chance of revealing the process they are studying.

Chandra had already observed the area in 2001–2002. In those archival exposures, AT2016blu’s X-ray counterpart remained below the detection threshold. Those earlier measurements supplied a reference point, with a clearly defined limitation: they described selected observing intervals more than two decades before the new campaign.

In March 2026, the team followed changes in visible light around a predicted outburst and triggered Chandra observations through a Target-of-Opportunity program, which allows observations in response to an unfolding event. The preprint reports X-ray detections in five closely spaced observing epochs. Here, an epoch simply means a particular observing time. All five belonged to the March campaign.

NASA’s image comparison places the chronology in concrete terms. An optical image from March 14 shows the system in a quiet state; an image from March 25 shows it in outburst. Chandra observed during March 24–27. These dates place the X-ray detections within the period of optical activity, giving researchers evidence at two kinds of wavelength during the same episode. The coverage establishes a shared observing window, while the detailed timing of individual changes remains a finer question.

The strength of the X-ray signal added another important clue. NASA describes the March emission as at least 100 times brighter than the level permitted by the 2001–2002 archival observations.

The meaning of “permitted” deserves care. When a telescope fails to detect a source, its data can still set an upper limit: the greatest brightness that could plausibly have escaped detection under those observing conditions. The older Chandra exposures supplied such a ceiling. The March measurement stood at least 100 times above that earlier limit.

This makes the reported increase a conservative comparison. The earlier source could have been fainter than the ceiling, which would make the actual difference larger. The precise earlier brightness remains unknown. The factor applies specifically to X-rays in these observations; the visible-light record and the star’s mass each describe separate properties.

Chandra also had to establish where the X-rays came from. A nearby bright X-ray source complicated the view. The telescope’s sharp angular resolution—its ability to distinguish objects that appear close together on the sky—helped separate AT2016blu from that neighbor. Timing associated the detected emission with an active period. Spatial separation helped assign it to the right system. Both were essential to the argument.

The researchers’ favored explanation brings those clues together in a binary, a system containing two objects bound by gravity. One is the massive luminous blue variable. The other is proposed to be a compact object: either a neutron star or a stellar-mass black hole. A neutron star packs matter into an extraordinarily dense stellar remnant. A stellar-mass black hole concentrates enough mass for gravity to trap light inside a boundary around it. The observations leave those two identities open, and the companion’s mass remains undetermined here.

The proposed orbit is eccentric, meaning elongated or oval. As the objects travel around one another, their separation changes. Near closest approach, the compact companion may capture material expelled by the massive star more readily. That offers a physical reason for an energy source to strengthen at recurring intervals.

The approximate 113-day optical rhythm is therefore useful evidence for an orbital explanation. Its role is to suggest a repeating encounter or change in conditions. The detailed orbital shape and the path taken by the gas remain inferred features of the model.

The next step is accretion: material gathering and falling toward an object under gravity. Gas approaching a compact companion gains speed. As moving material interacts, energy can become heat, raising temperatures enough to produce X-rays. In this account, the radiation comes from heated matter outside the compact object. The material being captured is gas expelled by the massive star, which remains present through the observed eruptions.

That mechanism explains how a comparatively small region around a companion could influence the appearance of a much larger stellar system. Gravity supplies energy as gas falls inward. The brightness then depends partly on how much material reaches that region and how the surrounding gas handles the energy released.

The same proposal also connects the X-rays to the optical outburst. Dense gas around the system can absorb energetic radiation and release some of that energy at visible wavelengths. Astronomers call this reprocessing. In plain language, energy first emerges in one form of light, interacts with matter, and emerges partly in another.

This gives the visible display a potentially indirect origin. Some of the light reaching an optical telescope could carry energy generated near the companion and subsequently released by surrounding gas. The star, its expelled material, and the proposed compact object would then form an interacting system whose appearance depends on several linked processes.

The March evidence supports that account because it combines recurring optical behavior with strong X-ray emission during an outburst. The earlier X-ray limit adds evidence of a substantial change in the system’s high-energy output. The proposed model explains why proximity, available gas, and heating could produce intermittent activity.

The strength of the explanation rests on the combination. Recurrence provides a possible schedule for encounters. X-rays provide a clue to energetic material. Chandra’s resolving ability ties that clue to AT2016blu. Each observation answers a different part of the question, allowing the physical interpretation to become more specific.

Several aspects remain open within that interpretation. The companion’s identity is unresolved. The orbit and transfer of material are inferred from the signals. Five closely spaced X-ray detections describe one observing campaign, so the relationship between optical and X-ray behavior across many eruptions remains a subject for further study. These boundaries define the reach of the result.

The authors conclude that AT2016blu is the first known luminous blue variable supernova-impostor case whose outbursts are driven by intermittent accretion onto a compact object. They describe it as a high-mass X-ray binary: a massive star supplying material to a compact companion. The novelty concerns that particular connection between a class of surviving stellar eruptions and a proposed source of their power.

The research also has a chronology distinct from the events in the sky. The decisive Chandra observations occurred in March 2026. The public author preprint was submitted on August 20 and marked accepted by *The Astrophysical Journal*. NASA and the Chandra X-ray Center released their accounts on November 9; NASA also reports journal publication that day. Those two public accounts describe the same underlying study.

This feature draws on those reports and the documented findings from the public preprint. The journal-final text was access-restricted during source collection, and its final wording remains independently unexamined here.

The next scientific question follows naturally from the proposed mechanism. If recurring close approaches help feed the companion, how consistently will the X-rays rise and fade alongside the visible outbursts? Observations across additional cycles could test that relationship and help constrain how material moves through the system. Similar comparisons might also show how widely this explanation applies among other erupting stars.

AT2016blu has already changed from a recurring flash into a more detailed physical puzzle. Years of brightness measurements supplied a rhythm; a targeted March campaign revealed energetic activity at the right location during an eruption. The hidden companion remains an inference, with its identity still open. As the system brightens again, the question becomes whether its next signals will trace the same movement of gas—and reveal more of the object waiting nearby.
