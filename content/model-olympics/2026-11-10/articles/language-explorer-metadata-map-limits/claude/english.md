# A Map of Thousands of Languages Lights Up in Berlin. What Lies Behind Each Pin?

On November 9, 2026, Google Research announced that its Language Explorer now appears as an interactive installation at the Museum für Kommunikation, Berlin's museum of communication. Language Explorer is a free public website for browsing facts about thousands of the world's languages. The museum's exhibition is called *REAL TALK. Language. Power. World.* Daan van Esch and Uche Okonkwo, two Google Research staff members who work on language technology, wrote the announcement. Language Explorer offers a world map dotted with pins, a search box and a data page for each language. Beneath those pages sits LinguaMeta, a research collection that van Esch and Okonkwo described in May 2024 with three colleagues, Sandy Ritchie, Shikhar Vashishth and Emily Drummond, in a paper at the LREC-COLING conference, a major meeting for language researchers. The fresh news of November 2026 is a new public stage for older research. A database built for scientists and engineers now stands in front of museum visitors.

The wider setting explains why that move matters. Google describes the world as home to more than 7,000 languages. It uses that 7,000-plus figure to convey the scale of human linguistic diversity and its long-running ambition to bring more languages online. The company's products reach parts of that range in different ways. According to Google, Translate handles nearly 250 languages, and the Gboard phone keyboard supports more than 900 language varieties. These three numbers measure different things. A Translate language has a working translation service. A Gboard variety is a typing setup, and a keyboard can list regional forms of one language as separate entries. The 7,000-plus figure estimates the world's overall diversity. Subtracting one number from another produces a tidy-looking "gap" that the evidence supports poorly. The figures work best side by side, as three separate snapshots of how far digital tools currently reach.

The museum frames *REAL TALK* around language and its connections to power, identity and society. Its public listing gives February 27, 2028, as the closing date and names a scheduled opening on Thursday, November 5, 2026, at 7 p.m. Google's post, dated November 9, places the opening within the week of its publication. The two accounts leave the timing unresolved: the museum's listing records a planned date, Google's wording points loosely to the days around November 9, and the actual date of the opening remains unconfirmed by either source. Google adds that the exhibition will travel to Frankfurt for a run that the company says lasts through September 2029. The exact Frankfurt dates still await confirmation from a second source.

The website documents a simple set of moves. A person can type the name of a language, a country or a region into the search box. A person can also pan across the map, click a pin and open a page about the language that pin represents. That page gathers several kinds of information into one neat panel. A natural question follows almost at once: what does that panel actually know, and where did each line come from?

## The card behind the pin

The answer begins with a word that sounds technical and turns out to be simple. LinguaMeta holds *metadata*, which means information about something. For a language, that information includes its names, the official codes that identify it, estimates of how many people speak it, the scripts used to write it, the places where it is used, any official status it holds and its level of endangerment. The collection describes languages. The languages themselves, with their conversations, songs, stories and grammar, live with the people who speak them.

An everyday comparison makes the idea concrete. Picture the card catalog in an old library. Each card tells a reader the title, the author, the year and the shelf number. The card helps someone find a book and tell two similar books apart, while the book itself sits on the shelf. If a card has a blank line for the publication year, the book still exists; the cataloger simply lacked that detail. If someone typed the card in 1980, its details reflect what the library knew in 1980. LinguaMeta works much like an enormous catalog of language cards. The comparison has a limit worth stating plainly. A book stays the same between visits, while a language keeps changing as its speakers live, move, marry, migrate and coin new words. A card for a living language is a snapshot of something in motion.

Those cards came from many hands. The site's FAQ says LinguaMeta *harmonizes* information drawn from 19 cited sources. Harmonizing means lining up records that use different names, codes and formats so that they describe each language in one consistent shape. The figure of 19 counts the reference collections that feed this merging process. Each of those collections carries its own history, with its own researchers, its own survey years and its own judgment calls. Nineteen inputs describe a process of assembly; they represent a mixture of older work, and the entries within them may differ in age and method.

LinguaMeta also records where each piece of information came from, a practice researchers call *provenance*. Provenance works like a stamp on a library card showing which older catalog supplied each detail. It lets a careful reader trace a number back to its origin and examine it. That trace reveals the origin of a value. Whether the value is still current, or correct, takes further checking against the original source and against newer evidence.

The collection also has deliberate borders. The repository that holds LinguaMeta, a public code-and-data store on the GitHub platform, says its focus is living, spoken languages. Many constructed languages, meaning languages deliberately invented by individuals or groups, many signed languages and many historical languages fall outside that focus. The map therefore shows the spoken side of present-day human language most fully.

Even inside those borders, the cards are filled in unevenly. The README, the guide document that explains the repository, reports that about 83 percent of the languages in LinguaMeta have a value for speaker population. That percentage has a specific base: the languages in the resource. It measures how often one field is filled in across the database's own entries. It describes the coverage of a field, separate from any share of the world's people or any score for census accuracy. For the remaining entries, roughly one in six, the speaker count is blank. A blank means unknown. It carries the same meaning as the empty year on a catalog card: a detail the compilers lacked, with the language and its speakers still out in the world. The README adds that some other fields are less complete than the speaker count.

Where numbers do appear, most arrive softened on purpose. The README explains that speaker figures come from the source records and are rounded in steps that grow with the size of the number. Counts up to 100 appear exactly as reported. Above that, the steps widen: a count between 100 and 1,000 is rounded to the nearest ten, a count between 1,000 and 10,000 to the nearest hundred, and so on up the scale, until counts above ten million are rounded to the nearest million. Each result is still a single number, with its precision matched to its size, which keeps a large estimate from looking more exact than its sources allow. On a catalog card, this resembles writing "about 300 pages" for a thick book while recording the exact count for a twelve-page pamphlet. The database also attempts to add up speakers of a language across several countries and regions into one total, while avoiding counting the same people twice. The README cautions that such calculations can magnify errors already present in the sources. If two underlying records each carry a small mistake, a combined total carries both mistakes forward.

Endangerment tags work in a similar way. According to the README, they use a six-point scale derived from UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger, and the README cites the Atlas's 2010 reference. UNESCO is the United Nations agency for education, science and culture, and its atlas grades languages along a ladder that runs from safe at one end to extinct at the other, largely according to how well a language passes from parents to children. The 2010 date identifies that cited Atlas reference. The observations behind any particular tag may come from a range of years, and the sources leave those individual dates open. A tag on the screen in 2026 reflects that reference and its underlying sources. Reading it as a fresh assessment completed in 2026 would mistake an inherited label for a new measurement.

A subtler issue concerns what counts as one language at all. LinguaMeta includes individual languages and also *macrolanguages*, a term for broader umbrella groupings that contain several individual languages. In catalog terms, a macrolanguage resembles a card for a multi-volume set, filed alongside separate cards for each volume. Both kinds of card are useful, and a count that adds set cards to volume cards will count some material twice. The README warns that counts mixing the two categories can overlap. Finer varieties within an individual language, such as local dialects, currently fall outside LinguaMeta altogether, according to the README's description of its scope; the catalog stops at the level of the individual language. Every such boundary reflects a practical filing decision. Speakers and scholars sometimes disagree about where one language ends and the next begins, and those disagreements can carry history, politics and pride. A database label records the filing choice its compilers made, while the wider conversation continues among the people concerned.

Two more terms help a reader make sense of a data page. A *script* is a system of written symbols, such as the Latin alphabet used for this article. A *locale* describes a geographic or cultural setting in which a language is used. The README illustrates these separate dimensions with Punjabi written in the Gurmukhi script in India. That example isolates one language, one script and one place to show how the database keeps the three dimensions apart. Punjabi itself is written in more than one script and spoken across more than one country; the example picks out a single combination for clarity.

The same care applies to the map. Each pin marks a recorded coordinate, a single point fixed by latitude and longitude. A point is a convenient way to place a language on a screen. Communities, meanwhile, spread across valleys, cities and diasporas, the scattered groups living far from an ancestral home. A pin locates a reference point. Questions about a community's full extent, its homeland or any national claim to a language belong to a different body of evidence.

For people who download the data, one more detail matters. The repository offers the information in two formats. The full version uses JSON, a structured file format that nests details inside one another. A compact overview comes as a TSV, a plain table with columns separated by tab characters, which opens easily in a spreadsheet program. According to the README, the compact table leaves out several details found in the full files, including some provenance, extended names, tags describing how scripts are used, researched official status and coordinates. A researcher who meets an empty column in the spreadsheet should check the fuller files before concluding that the whole resource lacks that information. Licensing is mixed as well. Google Research's own material carries a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license, which permits reuse with credit, while components from outside sources come under their own differing terms. Anyone planning to reuse the data has to check the terms for each part.

## A doorway and its fine print

Here the exhibition's larger theme meets the database. Placing thousands of languages on a glowing map in a public museum gives them visibility. The digital tools around a language each serve their own purpose. A research database organizes facts for people studying languages. A phone keyboard helps speakers type in their own language. A translation tool helps people carry meaning from one language into another. Each has its own requirements, and the sources describe these tools separately, leaving their relative workloads uncompared. The health of a language, which linguists call its vitality, rests on something else again: people speaking it at home, children learning it, and communities using it at work, in worship and in song.

The public sources examined here describe the installation and its tools. Questions about what visitors learn, or about any later change in how languages are taught or spoken, lie beyond what those pages report; any evidence on such outcomes would come from elsewhere. Google also mentions Project Vaani and Project Waxal, separate efforts that gather recordings of spoken language. They are distinct initiatives, and the public sources examined here leave open whether their audio appears in Language Explorer. Google describes an interest in collaborating with language communities. That statement describes an approach. Whether each represented community has reviewed and agreed to its entry remains an open question in those same public sources.

The tool does include a mechanism aimed directly at imperfect cards. The site invites users to send feedback backed by sources. The team reviews that feedback before making applicable changes to the repository or the website. The FAQ describes periodic updates whenever major developments in the dataset occur, which amounts to an occasional schedule and stops short of a continuous live refresh. In library terms, this resembles a slip at the front desk where patrons can report a wrong date on a card, with a librarian checking the claim before retyping it. The design makes sense in principle. The site and repository describe the process itself; figures on how many proposals arrive, how many succeed and how quickly changes reach the screen fall outside what those public pages report.

The Berlin installation therefore offers two things at once. It gives the public an inviting doorway into the vast variety of human speech. It also carries every simplification that comes with fitting thousands of living practices onto neat cards: rounded numbers, endangerment tags tied to a 2010 Atlas reference, filing choices about umbrella groups and the dialects beyond them, single points standing in for whole communities, and roughly one in six speaker counts left blank. The tools for examining those choices live in the same project, through provenance records, published documentation and a channel for sourced corrections.

What remains to be seen is whether the screen shows its gaps as clearly as it shows its pins. When a visitor in Berlin taps a dot and reads a tidy page, will that page also reveal which lines are estimates, which are old, which reflect a filing decision, and how a speaker who knows better can help set the record straight?
