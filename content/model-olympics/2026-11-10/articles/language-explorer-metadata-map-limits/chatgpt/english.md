# A Map of Thousands of Languages Opens a Question Behind Every Pin

Languages carry everyday life: conversations at home, lessons at school, stories, work and public affairs. Bringing information about thousands of them into one place means gathering records made by different people, at different times, for different purposes. Google Research’s Language Explorer gives those records a public map and search interface. On November 9, 2026, Google reported that the tool appears as an interactive installation in Berlin’s Museum für Kommunikation exhibition *REAL TALK. Language. Power. World.* A research resource has acquired a setting where visitors can encounter both linguistic diversity and the choices involved in describing it.

The attraction is easy to understand. Someone can search for a language, explore a country or region, and open a map pin to reach a page of related information. Names, writing systems and speaker estimates become easier to find together. For an educator preparing a lesson, a researcher following a reference or a speaker curious about how their language appears, this offers a possible starting point. These are potential uses; the inspected public sources describe the installation and its features, while leaving visitor experiences and educational results open for further reporting.

The larger challenge begins with the scale and variety of the subject. Language Explorer places its work against a background of more than 7,000 languages worldwide. That broad figure describes linguistic diversity. The underlying records have their own scope, categories and gaps. Even apparently simple questions—what a language is called, where people use it, how many speakers it has—can lead to several sources and answers shaped by different definitions. Gathering those answers makes them accessible. Presenting their origins and limits makes them interpretable.

Behind the interface sits LinguaMeta, described in a research paper published at LREC-COLING in May 2024. Its authors—Sandy Ritchie, Daan van Esch, Uche Okonkwo, Shikhar Vashishth and Emily Drummond—presented a unified resource of information about thousands of languages. The November 2026 announcement concerns a newer public setting for that earlier research. The museum lists the Berlin exhibition through February 27, 2028, and records an opening scheduled for November 5, 2026, at 7 p.m. That listing establishes the planned schedule; Google’s subsequent report establishes the company’s account of the installation.

LinguaMeta collects “metadata,” a technical word for information that describes something. Here, that includes language names and identifiers, speaker-population information, writing systems, geographical or cultural contexts, official status and endangerment information. A language entry organizes descriptions of a language. A collection of conversations, recordings or written works would serve a different purpose, giving access to examples of the language in use. Understanding this distinction helps establish what the map can offer before its polished presentation encourages broader expectations.

An everyday analogy is a library catalogue with a map attached. A catalogue record can help a reader find a book, understand how it has been classified and follow the source of its description. The book’s contents remain a separate object, and the catalogue’s usefulness depends partly on the care and age of its records. Likewise, Language Explorer can guide someone toward information about a language. The quality of that journey depends on what each entry contains, how its categories work and how far its sources can be followed.

According to the site’s FAQ, LinguaMeta harmonizes information citing 19 source collections. Harmonizing means arranging material from different origins into a shared structure so that it can be searched and compared. Recorded origins, often called provenance, allow a user to trace a value back toward the material behind it. The number 19 describes the resource’s cited collections. The support for any particular entry depends on the records associated with that entry. A common format brings information together; its accuracy and currency remain questions for examination.

This becomes concrete with the most immediately understandable number on a language page: its speaker population. The repository README says about 83% of languages in LinguaMeta have a speaker-population value. The unit being counted is a language entry in the resource. The percentage measures how often that field contains a value. It leaves the accuracy of those values as a separate matter, along with the much larger question of how comprehensively the world’s speakers have been counted.

For the remaining entries, the population value is unknown within that field. Keeping that status visible matters because a blank and a zero tell radically different stories. Zero makes a numerical claim; an empty field records a limit in the available information. Other fields also have incomplete coverage. A page may therefore contain a language name and location while offering less information about another aspect of its use. Each populated field adds something useful, and each unknown marks where further inquiry begins.

Even a filled population field requires a sense of proportion. LinguaMeta reports numeric counts using rounding steps that grow with the size of the population. Values from 0 to 100 are reported as supplied; larger counts are rounded in progressively larger steps, reaching the nearest million for counts above 10 million. This expresses estimates at a level intended to avoid excessive precision. The source records supply the underlying estimates, with their own dates and methods. A rounded value can communicate scale more honestly than a long string of digits. Its meaning still rests on the source: who was counted, which places were included and when the information was gathered. These are estimates assembled from records across time, with varying freshness.

Combining population records introduces a further difficulty. Regional and cultural descriptions can overlap, so adding every available figure risks counting some people more than once. The repository’s calculations seek to account for overlaps between regions and locales, while its README warns that calculation can magnify source errors. The resulting total therefore reflects both the original evidence and decisions about combining it. This is a familiar problem in data work: arithmetic can produce a clean result from inputs whose boundaries remain complicated.

Classification creates another kind of overlap. LinguaMeta includes individual languages and “macrolanguages,” broader groupings that encompass individual languages. A count that adds both levels can count overlapping descriptions of linguistic life. Finer varieties within individual languages are currently excluded from LinguaMeta. Consequently, a total number of entries becomes meaningful only when the reader understands which categories it includes. The database’s organizing scheme helps make searching possible; the scheme also shapes what a search reveals.

The resource’s scope is living, spoken languages. Many signed, historical and constructed languages fall outside that scope. An unsuccessful search can thus reflect the boundaries of the collection or the way a name is recorded. The language’s existence is a separate question. This matters especially in a public exhibition, where the apparent reach of a map may exceed a visitor’s familiarity with its selection rules. A useful overview gains clarity when readers can see what kind of overview they are exploring.

Language, writing system and locale also answer different questions. A writing system concerns the symbols used to write. A locale describes a geographical and cultural context. The README illustrates their relationship with Punjabi written in Gurmukhi in India: Punjabi identifies the language, Gurmukhi identifies the writing system, and India supplies geographical context. The example records one combination. Punjabi’s other writing practices and geographical settings remain part of its wider story. Keeping these dimensions separate allows a record to describe a particular use precisely while leaving room for other combinations.

The same care applies to the map itself. A pin marks a coordinate associated with a record. A community’s geography can extend across many places, and a point has limited power to express that reach. Reading a pin as a starting location for inquiry preserves its practical value. Treating it as a complete community boundary, an exclusive homeland or a claim of state ownership gives the coordinate meanings beyond what it establishes. The visual precision of a dot and the geographical complexity of language use belong to different scales.

Time is equally important, particularly when a label concerns a language’s future. LinguaMeta uses a six-point endangerment scale derived from the UNESCO Atlas, with a 2010 reference. That date identifies the framework’s reference point; the underlying observations may have their own dates. A reader opening a page in November 2026 is therefore encountering a classification whose current relevance depends on further source-level examination. The available documentation supports an explanation of the method. Assessing any particular language’s present condition requires its entry and supporting evidence.

An endangerment label also answers a different question from a speaker total. A population estimate addresses how many speakers a source records. Language vitality concerns continued use within communities and the circumstances sustaining that use. Putting these fields together can help a reader ask better questions, provided their distinct meanings remain clear. A large-looking number, a map location and a status label each contribute a piece of information. Their combination calls for interpretation, especially when their sources describe different moments.

The distinction extends to technology. A database entry makes information discoverable. A keyboard helps someone enter written characters. A translator attempts to convey meaning between languages. Google’s announcement places Language Explorer within its broader language work, citing nearly 250 Google Translate languages and more than 900 Gboard language varieties. Those company-reported figures count different units and functions. Their practical quality would require its own evidence. A place in a metadata resource establishes representation in that resource; effective writing tools, useful translation and sustained community use each involve further work.

The exhibition’s interest in language, identity, power and society gives these distinctions a public setting. Choosing a main name, assigning a category or selecting a location influences how people encounter a language. A speaker may recognize an entry immediately, or may see a description that calls for revision. A researcher may follow a population estimate to its origin. An educator may use a page to introduce the relationship between language and writing. The inspected documentation establishes these possibilities; direct accounts of such encounters would help assess their practical value.

Language Explorer offers a route for sourced feedback. The team reviews submissions before applicable changes reach the repository or site, and the FAQ describes periodic updates associated with major dataset developments. This gives users a documented way to challenge or improve a record. The effect of that process depends on what happens after a submission: how the evidence is assessed, how disagreements are handled and how a revised record appears. The inspected public materials describe the process; correction outcomes and community reception remain unknown within that evidence.

For researchers, following the evidence also means choosing the appropriate data format. The repository’s simpler tab-separated overview—a table stored as plain text—omits some details available in its fuller structured JSON data, including certain provenance and coordinate information. An audit using the overview alone would therefore have a narrower view of the record. Reuse has another boundary: the repository describes Google Research material under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence alongside external material carrying differing terms. Users need to follow the terms attached to the relevant material.

The map’s strongest promise is the journey it makes possible, from curiosity about a language to a record, then onward to the evidence behind it. Its public presentation brings that journey within easier reach. The repository’s documented limits give the journey substance: unknown values, rounding choices, overlapping categories and dated frameworks all affect what a reader can conclude. For the people whose languages appear on the map, the question now becomes practical: can they recognize the records, trace their origins and help improve them—and will those encounters make the overview more useful over time?
