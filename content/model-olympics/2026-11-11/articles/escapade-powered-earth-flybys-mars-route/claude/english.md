# Two Close Passes of Earth Have Sent ESCAPADE Toward Mars. The Braking Burns That Decide Its Fate Come Next September

In early November 2026, two small NASA spacecraft named Blue and Gold each swept close past Earth, fired their engines for several minutes, and headed out toward Mars. Together they form ESCAPADE, short for Escape and Plasma Acceleration and Dynamics Explorers. The University of California, Berkeley's Space Sciences Laboratory leads the mission. Rocket Lab built the two spacecraft. Advanced Space, a company that designs spacecraft routes, is among the partners. NASA funds the work through its Heliophysics Division, the part of the agency that studies the Sun and its influence across space, and through SIMPLEx, short for Small Innovative Missions for Planetary Exploration, a program for compact, lower-cost science missions. The pair's assignment is to watch, from two places at once, how the steady outflow of charged material from the Sun meets Mars and helps carry away part of its atmosphere. On November 9, NASA reported that both spacecraft had completed what it calls powered Earth flybys. Rocket Lab published its own account of the maneuvers on November 10.

Those two passes close an unusual first year. ESCAPADE launched from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station on November 13, 2025, and it has spent almost all of the time since then near Earth. The reason for that long first chapter is the shape of the whole mission, and the November flybys are the moment the plan turned toward Mars.

## A launch at the wrong moment, made useful

Earth and Mars both circle the Sun, at different distances and different speeds. Earth completes a lap in one year. Mars, farther out, takes nearly two. The fuel-efficient direct trip from one to the other works best when the two planets sit in a particular arrangement, so that a spacecraft leaving Earth arrives at the spot where Mars will be months later. That favorable arrangement comes around roughly every 26 months. Missions that depend on a direct route plan their launches around those dates.

ESCAPADE lifted off in November 2025, when, according to NASA, Earth and Mars were poorly aligned for a direct transfer. The mission team chose a patient route. After launch, the two spacecraft traveled out toward a region of space called Sun–Earth L2, roughly a million miles from Earth on the side facing away from the Sun.

L2 deserves a careful description, because the name sounds like a place on a map. It is a location defined by the combined pull of the Sun and Earth together with the motion of everything involved. A spacecraft near L2 can follow a looping path that keeps it in Earth's neighborhood as Earth travels around the Sun, with small engine firings to keep it on track. ESCAPADE's spacecraft traced a large, moving path around that region, which NASA describes as shaped like a kidney bean. Course-correction maneuvers took place along the way. The spacecraft were in motion the entire time, following a designed route that would eventually bring them back toward Earth at a chosen hour.

That return was the point of the loop. By the autumn of 2026, the geometry had become favorable for heading to Mars, and the spacecraft came home for a close encounter that would set them on their way.

Here is an everyday illustration of the scheduling idea, and it is only an illustration. Picture a traveler who arrives at the station when the direct train to her destination has already left for the season. She boards a local service that carries her around a loop line, staying in motion, and returns her to a major junction just as a connecting express pulls in. She steps aboard at the right minute and rides on toward her destination. The comparison captures two things about ESCAPADE: the mission kept moving while waiting for a better connection, and the connection itself demanded precise timing. The physics of what happened at the junction is a separate matter, and it involves Earth's own motion.

## How a planet can change a spacecraft's course

A gravity assist, sometimes called a slingshot, uses a planet's motion to change a spacecraft's path. NASA's educational material on spaceflight explains the principle, and it becomes clearer one step at a time.

Imagine standing on Earth and watching a spacecraft fly past. From that viewpoint, it comes in, curves around the planet under Earth's gravity, and leaves heading in a new direction. Now step back and watch the same event from the Sun's point of view. Earth itself is racing around the Sun at enormous speed. As the spacecraft curves around the moving planet, it ends up with a different speed and direction relative to the Sun, because it has shared a little of Earth's motion. Earth gives up an amount of its own motion so tiny that measuring it would be hopeless, and the spacecraft gains a meaningful change. Energy is exchanged; the planet's motion supplies it. The precise angle and timing of the approach determine the result.

ESCAPADE added a second ingredient. During each Earth encounter, the spacecraft also fired its own engine in a sustained departure burn. The new route toward Mars came from a combination of Earth's motion, the carefully chosen geometry of the pass, and the push from onboard propulsion. That combination is why NASA calls these powered flybys. NASA describes them as its first powered Earth flybys used to send spacecraft toward Mars, and the agency accomplished the maneuver twice, once for each spacecraft. The claim is specific: gravity assists themselves have a long history in spaceflight, and this mission's distinction lies in using a powered Earth pass as the launching point for a Mars journey.

## Two passes, two days apart

The first spacecraft reached its closest approach and performed its departure burn around 5 a.m. Eastern Standard Time on November 7, 2026. NASA places that pass about 1,000 miles above Earth's surface. The second spacecraft followed around 6 a.m. Eastern Standard Time on November 9, passing about 650 miles above the surface. For scale, the International Space Station orbits roughly 250 miles up, so both spacecraft swept through space a few times higher than the station's path. The published accounts identify the spacecraft as Blue and Gold; the question of which one flew on which date remains open in those reports.

NASA describes each departure burn as lasting roughly nine minutes. Rocket Lab rounds the figure to approximately ten minutes per spacecraft. Both are approximate descriptions of the same events.

Rocket Lab adds operational detail from its position as the spacecraft builder. According to the company, each spacecraft had to perform its departure burn as a single continuous firing; the mission design called for one uninterrupted maneuver. The spacecraft carried out those burns autonomously, with operators at Rocket Lab and UC Berkeley overseeing them. In this setting, autonomous means the spacecraft followed its onboard instructions on its own during the critical minutes, while people on the ground watched and held responsibility for the mission. The route itself came from human mission designers, led by Jeff Parker of Advanced Space.

The burns took place close to Earth, during the brief window when each spacecraft was near the planet. A long, continuous firing at that moment carries real stakes. A spacecraft that starts late, stops early, or points slightly off can leave Earth on a path that misses its target and then needs costly corrections later. NASA reports that both spacecraft completed their flybys successfully and are now on course for Mars. Rocket Lab also reported figures describing the accuracy of the burns, using measures the company leaves undefined in its release; this article sets those numbers aside and relies on the reported completion of both maneuvers.

Parker has described the broader design as giving a Mars mission far more freedom in choosing when to launch. That description explains the ambition behind the route. ESCAPADE's own journey still depended on planned geometry: the spacecraft waited near Earth until the planets reached a useful arrangement, and the departure happened at that chosen time. The published accounts describe the scheduling benefit in qualitative terms.

## Ten months of cruising, then a harder kind of burn

With the Earth flybys complete, the two spacecraft have begun a cruise to Mars that NASA expects to last about ten months. Small course corrections are planned along the way, the spaceflight equivalent of a sailor adjusting the heading during a long crossing.

The next decisive moments are scheduled for September 2027. NASA plans Mars orbit-insertion burns for September 7 and September 9, 2027, one for each spacecraft. Each is expected to last about 11 minutes. These burns have the opposite purpose from the November maneuvers. The departure burns sent the spacecraft onto a path toward Mars. The arrival burns must slow each spacecraft enough for Mars' gravity to capture it into orbit. A spacecraft arriving too fast continues past the planet. The capture burns are therefore the mission's second great test of timing and propulsion, and their outcome is still ahead.

After capture, NASA expects several months of orbit adjustment, as each spacecraft reshapes its path into the orbit the science plan requires. The agency expects science operations to begin in June 2028.

## Why two spacecraft matter

The scientific puzzle explains why ESCAPADE carries two identical spacecraft.

The Sun constantly blows out a thin stream of electrically charged material called the solar wind. NASA describes it moving at around a million miles per hour, a typical figure for a flow whose speed varies over time. The solar wind is a different thing from the wind that rustles trees on Earth. It is made largely of ions, which are electrically charged particles, and electrons. A gas made of charged particles like this is called plasma, which is where the mission gets the word in its name.

When the solar wind reaches Mars, it meets the planet's magnetic environment and its upper atmosphere. That encounter can energize and carry away charged particles from the top of the atmosphere, contributing to the slow loss of Mars' air into space. ESCAPADE aims to measure how this interaction works.

A single spacecraft faces a stubborn problem when studying something that changes. Suppose it measures one value at noon and a different value an hour later. The spacecraft has moved during that hour, and the solar wind may have changed too. The difference could come from a shift in time, a shift in place, or both. One observer moving through a shifting environment has trouble telling those apart.

Two spacecraft offer a way through. NASA's plan uses them in two arrangements. In the first, the pair follow one another along the same orbit, so the second spacecraft passes through the same region shortly after the first. Comparing their readings helps researchers see how conditions change with time at a given place, and how they differ from place to place. About six months later, the plan calls for the spacecraft to move into different orbits. One would travel farther out, watching the solar wind as it arrives. The other would fly closer to Mars, recording how the planet's surroundings respond. That second campaign is scheduled to last five months. Measuring the incoming flow and the planet's response at the same moment is like a coach watching both the pitch and the batter's swing, a pairing that links a cause to its effect. Those formations are plans for future measurements. The Mars results, and any new estimate of how quickly Mars loses its atmosphere, lie ahead of the mission.

The spacecraft have already done some scientific work. NASA reports that during the loiter period near Earth, they gathered observations as they passed through Earth's magnetotail, the long stretch of Earth's magnetic environment swept out behind the planet by the solar wind. Those measurements come from Earth's surroundings and stand apart from the planned Mars campaign. They show the instruments at work in space ahead of the main task.

The findings at Mars could eventually help people planning human missions there understand the space environment around the planet. That connection is a hope for the future, resting on measurements still to come.

## What the passes settled, and what waits beyond them

The November flybys answered a navigation question. Two spacecraft, launched at a poorly timed moment for a direct Mars trip, spent nearly a year looping through Earth's neighborhood, returned on schedule, and each completed a long continuous engine burn during a close pass, according to NASA and Rocket Lab. A route that looked like a detour has become a road to Mars.

That record of success rests on NASA's mission reporting and Rocket Lab's account as the spacecraft builder. The next checkpoints will add evidence of their own: course corrections during cruise, the braking burns planned for September 7 and September 9, 2027, the orbit adjustments that follow, and the first coordinated measurements expected in June 2028.

If those stages succeed, ESCAPADE will finally place two observers in different spots around Mars at the same time. The question then becomes concrete: when one spacecraft sees a gust of solar wind arrive and the other sees Mars' upper atmosphere respond, how much of the planet's slow loss of air will researchers be able to trace, step by step, from cause to consequence?
