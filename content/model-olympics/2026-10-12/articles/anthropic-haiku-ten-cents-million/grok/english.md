Three viral headline options

Suspenseful: The Ten-Cent Token Threshold: Anthropic’s Haiku 5.5 Just Redrew the Volume Map
Emotional Resonance: Cheap Tokens, Expensive Success: What Happens When the Meter Finally Drops
Counter-intuitive: Anthropic Made Input Almost Free. Your Actual Tasks Might Not Get Cheaper

On October 7, 2026, Anthropic introduced Claude Haiku 5.5. The company positioned the model for high-volume, cost-sensitive, and speed-sensitive work, and also as a subagent that can operate alongside larger Claude models. Those are the vendor’s own claims about intended use. They are not independent measurements of task success on live production workloads.

The launch price table is concrete. For prompts up to 100,000 tokens, input is listed at $0.10 per million tokens. Beyond that length the rate rises to $0.50. Output is $0.50 and $2.50 respectively. Cache reads sit at $0.01 and $0.05; cache writes at $0.125 and $0.625. These are separate line items. A million input tokens is a billing unit. It is not a promise that a single million-token prompt will be processed at the ten-cent rate, nor that any complete task will cost ten cents. Output tokens and cache operations are priced independently and can dominate the final bill.

Anthropic states that average run cost is roughly 75 percent below Haiku 4.5 and describes Haiku 5.5 as its fastest small model. The 75 percent figure is an aggregate claim tied to the company’s own workloads. It is not a guaranteed reduction for every user or every task. Haiku 5.5 also introduces adjustable effort settings. How aggressively the model works, and therefore how much it costs relative to the quality it delivers, remains dependent on the specific workload.

On the vendor’s OSWorld 2.1 offline-subset table, Haiku 5.5 is reported at 72.4 percent. Haiku 4.5 sits at 15.7 percent on the same table; Sonnet 5.5 is listed at 83.9 percent. These are the scores Anthropic published. They are offline-subset benchmark numbers. They are not measurements of success rates on live websites, they are not safety guarantees, and they are not evidence of reliable unattended operation. No independent reproduction of the table appears in the source material used here.

The pricing change alters the economics of volume. When the raw cost of feeding tokens into the model falls this far, the fixed overhead of spinning up more calls, more parallel agents, or longer context windows shrinks. Teams that previously rationed input because it was expensive can now experiment with denser prompts, more frequent checks, or larger batches without the same immediate penalty. That shift is real on the input side of the ledger.

It does not automatically translate into lower cost per successful outcome. Output still has to be generated and paid for. Retries, verification steps, and human or automated checking still consume tokens and time. Adjustable effort means an operator can choose to spend more compute inside a single call in the hope of reducing the number of calls overall; the opposite choice is also available. The final cost of a finished, correct result is therefore a function of input price, output volume, effort setting, failure rate, and the cost of whatever process confirms that the answer is usable. Cheap raw input changes one variable. It does not freeze the others.

This is the central tension the numbers leave unresolved. The ten-cent input tier makes high-volume experimentation cheaper to start. It does not, by itself, tell an operator whether the same volume will finish cheaper once every token that leaves the model and every correction that follows are counted. Anthropic’s own framing—that average run cost is substantially lower and that the model is especially suitable for speed- and cost-sensitive work—points toward workloads where the input reduction dominates. It does not constitute evidence that every existing pipeline will see the same percentage drop once real success criteria are applied.

The OSWorld numbers illustrate the same distinction at the level of capability. A jump from 15.7 percent to 72.4 percent on an offline subset is large. It is still an offline subset. Live environments introduce network latency, authentication, rate limits, changing page structure, and partial observability that the offline table does not capture. The gap between 72.4 percent and Sonnet 5.5’s 83.9 percent on the same table further indicates that Anthropic itself continues to rank larger models higher on that particular evaluation. Using the smaller model as a subagent therefore remains a design choice whose success depends on how cleanly the larger model can compensate for whatever the smaller one misses, and on whether the extra coordination tokens offset the per-call savings.

None of the source material supplies data on actual customer invoices, production error rates, or measured cost reductions on third-party workloads. Those quantities are simply not reported. The absence matters because the economic claim is workload-dependent by the vendor’s own description. An operator whose tasks are short, cache-friendly, and tolerant of occasional retries may see the input price dominate and capture most of the advertised reduction. An operator whose tasks produce long outputs, require multiple verification passes, or fail often enough to trigger fallbacks will see a different ratio. The table of prices does not resolve which profile is more common; it only makes the arithmetic transparent.

Adjustable effort adds another layer that is easy to under-weight. A lower effort setting reduces tokens spent inside a single generation at the risk of lower quality or higher downstream correction. A higher effort setting spends more inside the call in the hope of producing a result that needs less external repair. The optimal setting is not visible from the price sheet alone. It has to be measured against the cost of the repair process that follows. In environments where that repair process is itself an LLM call, the same pricing table applies recursively. In environments where repair is human review, the relevant cost is labor rather than tokens. The model card does not dictate which regime applies.

The subagent positioning follows the same logic. Pairing a cheap, fast model with a larger one can reduce the number of expensive calls the larger model must handle. It can also increase total token volume if the smaller model’s outputs must be summarized, filtered, or re-checked by the larger one. Whether the net bill falls depends on the fraction of work that can be offloaded cleanly and on the coordination overhead required to keep the two models consistent. The launch materials describe the intended pattern; they do not publish the measured overhead of that pattern on production traffic.

What the numbers do establish is a change in the relative price of volume. At ten cents per million input tokens for prompts under 100,000 tokens, the cost of simply trying more things—more prompt variants, more parallel agents, more intermediate checks—has dropped by a factor that is large enough to alter planning assumptions. Teams that once treated every additional thousand tokens as a budget line item can now treat many of those tokens as closer to rounding error. That does not make every experiment free, and it does not make every experiment successful. It does make the decision to run the experiment less sensitive to input length than it was under the previous small-model pricing.

The distinction between billing unit and completed task remains important. A million-token input at the ten-cent rate is not the same thing as a completed, verified answer for ten cents. Output, cache operations, and any follow-up calls sit on separate lines. If a task requires 200,000 output tokens and two retries, the input discount is real but no longer the dominant term. Operators who model only the input price will overestimate the savings; operators who model the full call stack will see the residual costs more clearly.

The same caution applies to speed claims. “Fastest small model” is Anthropic’s characterization. Independent latency measurements under production load, with realistic context lengths and concurrent traffic, are not part of the source material. Speed and cost are often traded against each other inside the same system; a model that finishes a single call quickly may still require more total calls if its first attempt is less reliable. The adjustable effort control is one explicit mechanism for managing that trade-off. Its practical effect remains workload-specific.

Taken together, the launch supplies a clear price schedule, a set of vendor performance claims, and one published benchmark table. It does not supply a universal cost-reduction factor, a live success rate, or an independent audit. The practical question for any given team is therefore narrower than the headline numbers: on the specific distribution of prompt lengths, output lengths, retry rates, and verification steps that team actually runs, how much of the input discount survives once every other line item is added? The price table makes that calculation possible. It does not perform the calculation.

The ten-cent input tier changes the slope of the volume curve. It does not flatten the cost of getting the answer right. That remaining gap—between cheaper tokens and cheaper verified outcomes—is the part of the ledger that still has to be measured rather than announced.

What task in your own stack would you actually reprice first if input really sat at a dime per million, and what part of the bill would you still have to watch?
