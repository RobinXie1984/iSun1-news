Suspenseful: The Final RFP Is Out: NASA’s Last Formal Chance to Keep a Continuous American Presence in Low Earth Orbit After the ISS

Emotional Resonance: Who Will Keep the Lights On in Orbit Once the ISS Goes Dark?

Counter-intuitive: NASA Is Not Asking for a Replacement Space Station. It Is Asking Companies to Prove They Can Build One That Doesn’t Need NASA Forever.

On October 9, 2026, NASA released its final request for proposals for commercial low-Earth-orbit destinations. The document does not announce a winner, a station, or a retirement date. It does something narrower and, in its own way, more consequential: it asks U.S. industry to submit complete plans for destinations that can keep a sustained American presence in low Earth orbit after the International Space Station is retired, while also building a customer base beyond NASA itself.

Administrator Jared Isaacman framed the release in terms of intent and requirements rather than accomplishments. NASA intends to retain a sustained U.S. presence after ISS retirement. It wants credible technical plans and workable economics. Those remain goals. Nothing in the announcement claims that replacement capacity already exists, that a gap has been closed, or that any company has demonstrated it can meet both the technical and the commercial tests at once.

Proposals are due December 8, 2026. Awards are expected in spring 2027. Neither date is a guarantee of selection or of later success. The request asks companies to design, build, test, certify, and operate destinations that offer end-to-end station and transportation services. The scope is therefore not limited to a pressurized module or a docking port. It covers the full chain from Earth to orbit and back, under the operating regime the company itself will run.

The planned contracting structure is firm-fixed-price, multi-award indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity. At least two contractors are expected through early development. Later competitive task orders would cover final design, test and evaluation, and certification and services, and could go to one or more of those contractors. This is the procurement architecture NASA has described. It is not a list of selected firms, not an announced total value, and not money already spent.

The clearest signal in the announcement is that NASA wants commercial destinations capable of serving the agency and of developing other customers. The agency is not offering to buy the entire output of a station in perpetuity. It is asking companies to show how their systems can meet NASA’s needs while also generating revenue from additional users. That dual requirement is the central commercial test. Reaching orbit does not, by itself, produce a working business. The request for proposals treats that distinction as real.

Several things remain unknown from this release. The selected firms are not named. No total award value is stated. No finished stations are described. No verified profitability figures appear. No precise retirement date for the ISS is settled by this document. Older transition timelines circulating elsewhere are not restated or confirmed here; importing them as newly settled facts would go beyond what the announcement provides. The release also contains no astronaut scenes, no private statements of emotion, and no claims that NASA has sold the ISS, abandoned orbit, or opened a space hotel.

The structure of the procurement itself carries practical consequences. A multi-award approach through early development keeps more than one technical path alive at the stage when major design choices are still open. Later competition for task orders allows NASA to select among those paths once more is known about technical maturity and cost. Firm-fixed-price terms shift more of the cost-growth risk onto the contractors than a cost-plus arrangement would. Whether that shift produces better discipline or simply deters capable teams is not answered by the announcement; it is a risk the market will price into the proposals that arrive by December 8.

The requirement for end-to-end services further raises the bar. A destination that cannot reliably transport crew or cargo, or that cannot integrate with existing and planned transportation systems, fails the request even if its modules are elegant. Certification is not a paperwork step that follows successful flight; it is part of the operating model the company must propose. Companies that have previously flown hardware or carried NASA payloads will still have to show how their full system, including the transportation piece, meets the sustained-presence goal under commercial economics.

Isaacman’s emphasis on workable economics points to a constraint that has limited commercial station concepts for years. NASA can pay for services, but the agency’s budget is finite and its needs are specific. A station whose only reliable customer is NASA is, from the commercial standpoint, still largely a government program with private contractors. The request asks for something different: destinations that can attract research organizations, industrial users, or other government agencies in addition to NASA, so that the fixed costs of operating in orbit are spread across a broader revenue base. Whether such customers exist at the volume and price points required is precisely what the proposals must argue and, later, what any awarded contractors must demonstrate.

The timeline is tight relative to the scale of the work. Between the December 2026 proposal deadline and an expected spring 2027 award decision, NASA will evaluate technical credibility and economic plans. Early development work under the multi-award structure would then begin. Final design, testing, certification, and the start of services would follow under subsequent task orders. Each of those steps has historically taken years for crewed systems. The announcement does not claim that the sequence will be completed before the ISS is retired, nor does it claim that continuous presence is already assured. It states an intention and a procurement path.

One practical implication of the dual-customer model is that NASA’s requirements and commercial customers’ requirements may not align perfectly. NASA may prioritize specific research facilities, safety margins, or schedule reliability. A commercial user might prioritize power, volume, data rates, or lower per-seat costs. The proposals will have to show how a single architecture can satisfy both sets of needs without making the system too expensive for either. That tension is not resolved by the request for proposals; it is delegated to the companies that respond.

Another implication is the treatment of risk. Firm-fixed-price development of crewed orbital systems has a mixed record. Cost growth and schedule slips have occurred even under fixed-price arrangements when technical unknowns prove larger than expected. The multi-award early phase gives NASA a chance to observe which teams manage those unknowns more effectively before committing to later stages. It also gives companies an incentive to keep their early bids realistic, because an unrealistically low early price could leave them unable to perform once the harder work begins. Again, the announcement describes the structure; it does not predict which teams will succeed under it.

The announcement is careful about what it does not claim. It does not assert that commercial destinations already exist that can replace the ISS. It does not name companies that have already solved the economics. It does not treat the spring 2027 awards as certain. Those restraints matter because public discussion of commercial space stations has sometimes moved ahead of demonstrated capability. By releasing a final request for proposals with explicit technical and economic criteria, NASA is asking industry to put specific plans on the table rather than continuing with concepts alone.

For the United States, the underlying policy goal is continuity of presence. A gap after ISS retirement would mean the loss of a national asset that has supported research, technology demonstration, and operational experience in orbit for decades. The request does not guarantee that continuity. It creates a formal mechanism through which companies can compete to provide it under commercial terms. Whether that mechanism produces one or more operational destinations in time depends on the quality of the proposals, the subsequent performance of any selected contractors, the availability of non-NASA customers, and the technical realities of building and certifying crewed systems.

The economics remain the open variable. A destination that can serve NASA and other customers must generate enough revenue to cover the costs of design, construction, certification, transportation, and ongoing operations, plus a return that makes the investment rational for private capital. NASA’s payments help, but they are not described as the entire business case. Companies will therefore need to identify additional users whose willingness to pay is high enough and stable enough to matter. Those users are not named in the announcement. Their existence and scale are part of what the proposals must substantiate.

The multi-award structure also creates an information advantage for NASA. By funding early development with more than one contractor, the agency can compare approaches on cost, schedule, technical risk, and commercial strategy before narrowing the field. That comparison is only useful if the early work is substantive enough to reveal real differences. Paper studies that look similar on the page may diverge once hardware, software, and customer commitments are tested. The later competitive task orders are the point at which those differences can be acted upon.

Nothing in the October 9 release converts intention into inventory. No stations have been certified under this solicitation. No transportation services have been demonstrated as part of an end-to-end commercial destination under these terms. No profitability has been verified. The request is an invitation to propose systems that could, if successfully executed, meet the dual requirements of sustained U.S. presence and broader commercial use. Execution remains future work.

The distinction between a government-owned station and a commercially operated destination that sells services to the government is therefore central. Under the model described, NASA would be a customer rather than the owner and operator. That shift changes incentives. The company must keep costs manageable for its full customer set, not only for NASA. It must also maintain the safety and reliability standards required for crewed operations while competing for non-NASA business. Those pressures can produce efficiency, or they can produce under-investment in margins that only become visible later. The proposals will have to address how the companies intend to manage that balance.

Transportation is inseparable from the destination in the request. A station that cannot be reached reliably, or whose crew and cargo transport is priced or scheduled in ways that make commercial use impractical, does not satisfy the end-to-end requirement. Companies may propose their own transportation, partnerships with existing providers, or some combination. The announcement does not prescribe the answer; it requires that an answer be part of the plan. That requirement forces proposers to confront the full cost stack rather than treating launch and return as someone else’s problem.

Certification adds another layer. Human-rating and operational certification for a commercial destination involve both technical demonstration and the establishment of processes that NASA and other regulators will accept. Those processes take time and money. A company that underestimates them in its early fixed-price proposal risks being unable to complete later stages. The multi-award approach gives NASA visibility into how different teams are planning that work before larger commitments are made.

The announcement’s restraint on dates and outcomes is itself informative. By declining to treat spring 2027 awards as certain and by omitting any claim that replacement capacity is already in hand, the release keeps the focus on the work still required. Continuous presence after ISS retirement is stated as an intention. The request for proposals is the instrument NASA is using to pursue that intention through commercial means. Success is not asserted; it is left as the outcome of competition, performance, and market response.

For industry, the December 8 deadline creates a forcing function. Teams must assemble technical designs, cost estimates, customer strategies, and transportation plans into coherent proposals under firm-fixed-price assumptions. Those proposals will be evaluated against the criteria NASA has set: credible technical approaches and workable economics for destinations that can serve the agency and others. The evaluation will determine which teams, if any, receive early development awards. Later performance will determine which of those teams, if any, receive the task orders that lead to certification and services.

The broader commercial question remains open. Low Earth orbit has hosted government stations for decades. Converting that environment into one that supports multiple customers at prices that cover full costs has not yet been demonstrated at station scale. The request asks companies to show how they would do so. Their answers, and NASA’s subsequent decisions, will indicate whether the dual-use model can move from policy goal to operating system.

The October 9 release therefore marks a formal step rather than a completed transition. It defines what NASA is willing to buy, how it intends to buy it, and what additional commercial activity it expects the destinations to support. It does not name the providers, set the final price, or guarantee the schedule. Those elements depend on the proposals that arrive in December and on the work that follows any awards in 2027.

What remains is the test the announcement itself sets: whether U.S. companies can put forward plans that are technically credible and economically workable for destinations that serve NASA while also attracting other customers. The answer will not be known when the proposals are submitted, nor even when early awards are made. It will be known only if and when certified systems are operating, transporting crews and cargo, and generating revenue beyond NASA’s payments. Until then, the sustained presence the agency intends to retain is a requirement written into a solicitation, not a capability already on orbit.
