# The Atacama Has Burst Into Flower. What Will Remain After the Colours Fade?

In northern Chile’s Atacama Desert, rain has brought a hidden part of the landscape into view. Dormant seeds produced flowers after a wet Southern Hemisphere winter, colouring parts of this exceptionally dry region red-purple, green and white during September and October 2026. NASA’s Earth Observatory described the bloom on November 19, drawing on satellite images and observations by scientists from Chile’s Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso. Their work connects a brief seasonal spectacle to a longer scientific task: understanding where desert life persists, how it responds to water, and what those responses might reveal about its future.

A desert’s visible surface gives only a partial account of its living systems. Seeds can remain dormant through dry conditions, carrying the possibility of growth into a later season. A bloom makes some of that capacity visible. Flowers provide evidence that plants have responded to a particular opportunity, in particular places. The larger question concerns what happens across the intervals between those opportunities, when the same ground can appear bare.

That gives this season’s colours two kinds of interest. They are an event worth observing in their own right, and they offer researchers a chance to investigate processes that spend much of their time out of sight. The Atacama remains an exceptionally dry desert. Its temporary flowers open a window onto life adapted to that setting; the duration and reach of the view depend on the season and on how carefully observers follow it.

The weather sequence began months before NASA published its account. According to the agency’s explanation, a strengthening El Niño in the tropical Pacific accompanied changes in the atmospheric pressure systems that influence storm routes. A subtropical Pacific high weakened, while a blocking high farther toward southern South America helped shift storm tracks toward the equator.

A high is a broad region where atmospheric pressure is relatively elevated. Such regions help shape the paths along which weather systems travel. Their position and strength matter because a storm’s route determines which stretch of land receives its moisture. Here, NASA describes an arrangement that helped bring winter storms into areas where water is usually scarce.

From mid-July, storm fronts supplied by an atmospheric river brought substantial rain along Chile, from Atacama to Los Lagos, according to Chile’s weather service as cited by NASA. An atmospheric river is a long, concentrated stream of moisture moving through the air. August storms then brought rare snow to parts of the Atacama. The weather account covers a much broader area than the individual flower scenes later shown in satellite images.

This is NASA’s explanation of the season’s atmospheric setting. The precise share of the rainfall attributable to El Niño remains an open question in this account, as does the event’s relationship to future climate conditions. The clear sequence is winter moisture followed by spring vegetation. Because Chile lies in the Southern Hemisphere, September and October fall in spring: the “austral spring” used in descriptions of the bloom.

For dormant seeds, that seasonal change created an opportunity to grow. Yet a flower is one visible stage in a longer life history. Germination, growth, flowering and the production of another generation of seeds each concern a different part of that history. Observing flowers establishes that some plants reached the flowering stage. Understanding how their populations endure requires following more of the sequence.

The satellite record provides dated views of this awakening. Landsat 8’s Operational Land Imager acquired images on September 14; an imager aboard Landsat 9 acquired images on October 24. One illustrated location lies about 30 kilometres southwest of Copiapó, near the eastern part of Desierto Florido National Park. That distance helps place the scene geographically. The bloom’s total area is a separate measurement.

In the September view of the other illustrated area, about 50 kilometres southwest of Copiapó, white flowers belong to *Nolana*, plants commonly called *suspiros*. Andrés Moreira-Muñoz, a scientist who studies the geographical distribution of plants, considers them probably *Nolana baccata*. A ground photograph of *Nolana* dates from September 24, ten days after the satellite image. The interval matters: the two observations bring complementary evidence from different days.

The October view of the location about 30 kilometres southwest of Copiapó carries red-purple *Cistanthe* flowers. Remote-sensing scientist Roberto Chávez identifies the likely species as *Cistanthe longiscapa*, while retaining the possibility of another member of the same plant group. Chávez and colleagues photographed *Cistanthe* on October 24, matching the date of the Landsat 9 observation. Both Chávez and Moreira-Muñoz are associated with Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso.

Together, those observations give the season biological detail. A white patch has a plausible plant identity; so does a red-purple patch. The scientific names distinguish groups and possible species, while the scientists’ qualifications preserve the precision of their judgments. The dates also keep the story anchored. September and October provide glimpses of variation across places and time; tracing the fate of an individual plant would require observations directed at that plant.

The distinction becomes clearer when considering what an orbiting instrument actually measures. NASA’s overview of the Operational Land Imager describes measurements of reflected radiation in visible, near-infrared and short-wave infrared bands. Visible light is the portion human eyes can see. The infrared bands extend the instrument’s view into other portions of the same spectrum. A band is simply a selected range of that radiation.

These measurements help distinguish patterns across the land. The instrument records light reflected from the surface, and researchers interpret those signals. A coloured area in an image therefore begins as an observation about reflected light. Connecting it to a particular plant requires biological evidence, including the closer views available through fieldwork. Satellite observation and plant identification meet at that interpretive step.

Imagine, as a hypothetical illustration, studying a large garden from a few overhead photographs and visits to two flower beds. The overhead views show where colour spreads and how patches relate to one another. Walking into a bed lets an observer inspect the plants responsible. Each view answers questions at its own scale. Extending a finding from two beds across the whole garden requires a reason to believe the visited beds represent the rest.

Time creates another challenge in the same imaginary garden. A photograph taken in the morning and a visit ten days later describe different moments. Both can be accurate, and both can help explain the garden. The confidence of the connection depends on what happened during the interval. This is why the September 14 satellite acquisition and September 24 ground photograph deserve their own dates in the Atacama story.

The research project described by NASA brings together satellite data, drones and digital cameras to study biodiversity across space and time. Biodiversity means the variety of living things; here, a central task is understanding how that variety changes from place to place and through the season. The combination of observing methods is intended to connect broad patterns with closer biological detail and eventually inform land-use and conservation managers.

The word “eventually” carries substantive meaning. The work described is a research effort with a practical aim. Its value will depend on how reliably observations answer questions that managers face. A picture can direct attention to a place. A dependable account of that place’s living systems requires a stronger connection between the picture, the plants on the ground and their behaviour over time.

Moreira-Muñoz regards this bloom as potentially the most significant in extent, intensity and duration since the team began its remote-sensing observations. The assessment gives the event weight while retaining its scope: a scientist’s qualified judgment within the team’s observational experience. A numerical ranking of the 2026 bloom remains unestablished in NASA’s account.

Those three dimensions describe different features of a bloom. Extent concerns how much ground it covers. Intensity concerns the strength of the observed signal. Duration concerns how long it lasts. Keeping them separate allows a more useful comparison between seasons. A bloom could occupy a wide area for a short interval, for example, while another could remain concentrated in a smaller area over a longer period. Those are hypothetical possibilities, and they illustrate why the dimensions deserve individual attention.

Species variety adds another question. A large, strongly coloured patch could be dominated by one kind of plant; a less visually striking patch could contain several. Again, these are illustrative possibilities. They show why the strength of a colour signal alone leaves the biological composition open. Measuring a spectacular bloom and describing its biodiversity are connected tasks, each with its own evidence.

The same distinction applies beneath the surface. A seed bank is the store of seeds present in the ground. This season’s flowers show that a portion of the desert’s dormant life responded successfully to the available conditions. The condition of the wider seed bank, and its ability to support future generations, remain longer-term questions. A bright season supplies a valuable observation within that investigation.

There is historical depth to the effort. NASA mentions a 2014 study that used Landsat observations and reports that a 2019 study identified 13 blooming events during 1981–2015. That count comes through NASA’s account of the study; the underlying paper’s full text was unavailable for independent examination here. Its useful contribution is to place the current work within a history of attempts to observe desert flowering over decades.

Comparing events across decades introduces questions about consistency. An observer needs to know what qualified as a bloom, which places were covered, and how observations from different years were made comparable. The historical count describes events identified within a particular study period. A forecast of the next bloom would require a separate understanding of the conditions that bring flowering about.

For conservation, the most interesting possibility may lie in learning which visible patterns correspond to enduring ecological differences. Consider an explicitly hypothetical comparison between two desert hillsides. Both flower after the same wet winter. One repeatedly supports a particular mixture of plants across several observed seasons; the other shows a different mixture or a different seasonal response. Repeated field observations could establish whether those differences persist, giving managers a firmer basis for understanding each place.

The first photographs would begin that inquiry. Later visits could reveal which plants flower at different times, which locations repeatedly respond, and how closely the satellite signals track those changes. Such findings could help researchers decide where further investigation would be useful. Their relevance to a particular land-use decision would then depend on the decision and on the ecological evidence gathered there.

This prospective value explains why a temporary bloom can matter beyond the weeks when flowers attract attention. Its transience creates an opportunity to observe a living system in an unusually visible phase. The scientific task is to carry knowledge from that phase into a longer account of the landscape. The project’s stated purpose points toward that account; validated maps and demonstrated management benefits remain outcomes for future assessment.

For now, the strongest story is also the most concrete. Winter moisture reached parts of an exceptionally dry desert. Plants flowered in spring. Satellites recorded selected places on known dates, and researchers added local photographs and qualified identifications. Those observations reveal a seasonal response with enough complexity to reward close study.

When the colours fade, the dates and images will remain available for comparison with another season. Their lasting value may emerge through those comparisons, as researchers connect brief appearances above ground with the persistence of life below. The Atacama’s flowers have made that life visible. How much of its enduring story can scientists learn before the next interval of waiting begins?
