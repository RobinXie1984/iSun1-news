# A Rare Bloom Lit Up the Atacama. What Will Remain When the Flowers Fade?

During September and October 2026, parts of the Atacama Desert in northern Chile turned red-purple, green and white. On November 19, NASA's Earth Observatory described the display in an article by Kathryn Hansen. The Earth Observatory is a NASA website that publishes satellite pictures of our planet with plain explanations. The report combines views from the Landsat satellites with photographs taken on the ground. It also draws on two scientists at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso, a university in the Chilean port city of Valparaíso. Roberto Chávez works in remote sensing, which means studying the land with measurements from aircraft or satellites. Andrés Moreira-Muñoz studies plant geography, the science of where plants grow and why. The Atacama is an exceptionally dry desert. The colour arrived after an unusually wet winter.

The timing needs a moment of orientation. Chile lies in the Southern Hemisphere, so its seasons run opposite to those of Europe, North America and most of Asia. Scientists use the word *austral* for Southern Hemisphere seasons. The austral winter falls around the middle of the calendar year, and austral spring follows from about September. The September and October flowers therefore belong to a desert spring. They appeared a few months after the winter rain that made them possible.

In a place this dry, plant life spends long stretches waiting. Seeds rest in the soil in a dormant state, alive while growth stays paused. When enough water arrives, some of those seeds sprout, grow and flower. NASA describes the 2026 colour as this kind of response: rain woke dormant seeds, and the resulting plants flowered in spring. The bloom covered particular places for particular weeks. The Atacama remains an exceptionally dry desert, and the flowers form a seasonal event within it. That brief, local quality sits at the centre of the story. A short display offers a rare chance to see where living seeds lie hidden. It also raises a harder question: how much can a few weeks of colour reveal about the life that persists through the long dry stretches in between?

## Where the water came from

NASA traces the wet winter to changing weather over the Pacific Ocean. El Niño is a recurring warming of surface waters in the tropical Pacific, and it can reshape weather far from where it forms. It was strengthening during 2026.

According to NASA, two areas of high pressure also shifted in ways that mattered for Chile. A high-pressure system is a large region where the air presses down slightly harder than in surrounding areas. Storms tend to travel around the edges of such regions, so their position and strength help decide where storms go. NASA describes a weaker subtropical high-pressure system over the Pacific. It also describes a blocking high, a persistent area of high pressure, positioned toward southern South America. NASA says these changes helped push storm tracks toward the equator. For Chile, that means northward, toward the Atacama.

The water arrived through an atmospheric river, a long, narrow band of concentrated moisture flowing through the sky. NASA cites Chile's national weather service, which reported storm fronts fed by an atmospheric river from mid-July. A major July rain event stretched along Chile from the Atacama region in the north to Los Lagos far to the south. August storms then brought rare snow to parts of the Atacama.

This weather sequence is NASA's explanation, built partly on the Chilean weather service's account. It describes a plausible route by which water reached the desert. NASA presents this route as a descriptive account of causes and events, in qualitative terms. The sound reading links the bloom to an unusually wet winter that NASA associates with these patterns. The precise weight of each cause remains a question for meteorologists. This article itself rests on NASA's public report and its instrument overview. The underlying Chilean weather report, the raw satellite files and the full text of an older study mentioned below lie beyond its reach.

## The view from orbit

Landsat is a long-running series of Earth-observing satellites operated by NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey, a U.S. government science agency. NASA's November report uses two satellite acquisitions. The Operational Land Imager on Landsat 8 captured a view on September 14. An imager on Landsat 9 captured another on October 24.

NASA's instrument overview explains what the Operational Land Imager measures: sunlight reflected from the ground, recorded in several bands. Some bands cover visible light, the colours human eyes can see. Others cover near-infrared light, just beyond red, and short-wave infrared light, farther along still. Both kinds of infrared are invisible to people. Different surfaces reflect these bands in different proportions. Patterns in the measurements therefore help researchers distinguish and compare kinds of land cover across wide areas.

NASA locates its illustrated scenes in relation to Copiapó, a city in the Atacama region. One area lies about 30 kilometres southwest of the city, near the eastern side of Desierto Florido National Park. The park's name means "Flowering Desert" in Spanish. Another area lies about 50 kilometres southwest, in a zone identified as a priority for biodiversity. A kilometre is a little more than half a mile. These distances tell a reader where to look on a map. They describe position. The total area covered by the 2026 bloom remains outside NASA's public account.

The dates deserve equal care. September 14 and October 24 sit forty days apart. Each image is a single snapshot of its own scene on its own day, and the pair shows two moments in a longer season. Neither image records the daily life of any individual flower.

Here an everyday comparison helps, offered purely as an illustration. Picture someone looking out an upstairs window at a neighbourhood after heavy rain. From that height, they can see which yards have turned green and which remain brown, and they can choose which street deserves a closer walk. Several details belong to street level: the names of the plants, how many there are, and whether they will still be thriving months later. Satellite imagery plays a similar role in the Atacama. It shows where the ground has changed across a wide region and points researchers toward places worth visiting. Naming the flowers takes people on the ground.

## Names on the ground

Chávez and his colleagues supplied that closer look. In NASA's account, the red-purple colour in the October view comes from *Cistanthe*, a group of flowering plants. Chávez identifies the flowers as most likely *Cistanthe longiscapa* or another species in the same genus. A genus is a group of closely related species; its name forms the first half of a two-part scientific name. His team photographed *Cistanthe* in bloom on October 24, the same date as the Landsat 9 acquisition.

The September view shows white flowers. Moreira-Muñoz identifies them as *Nolana*, known in Chile as suspiros, and probably *Nolana baccata*. The ground photograph of these white flowers dates from September 24, ten days after the September 14 satellite image. The photograph and the satellite view come from the same month and support each other. They also record different days, and that gap stays part of the evidence.

The Latin names do real work. They turn a general impression of desert flowers into specific proposals that other botanists can check against specimens, photographs and plant records. Each name also carries a stated level of confidence. Chávez offers *longiscapa* as the most likely species within *Cistanthe*, and Moreira-Muñoz offers *baccata* as the probable species of *Nolana*. Those qualifiers mark expert identifications that further fieldwork could confirm or refine.

A tempting story waits in these two colours: a hillside that turned white in September and then purple in October, one flower giving way to another. The evidence supports a simpler picture. Different views on different dates show different flowering plants. Whether particular slopes shifted from *Nolana* to *Cistanthe*, or whether one species replaced another, remains an open question for repeated observations of the same ground.

## A big claim, carefully framed

The most striking statement in NASA's report belongs to Moreira-Muñoz. He regards the 2026 event as potentially the most significant since his team's remote-sensing observations began. He judges it on three measures: extent, intensity and duration. Extent asks how much ground shows the bloom. Intensity asks how strong the bloom's signal appears, which roughly reflects how dense and vigorous the plant cover looks from above. Duration asks how long the display lasts.

NASA adds some history. It reports that a 2019 study identified 13 blooming events between 1981 and 2015. According to its citation, Chávez led that study. Its title says it used a long satellite record of plant greenness to measure the extent, duration and intensity of past blooms, the same three measures Moreira-Muñoz names. The count of 13 describes events found under that study's methods across those decades. It shows that blooms have appeared repeatedly in the satellite era. Any regular cycle, or a yearly chance of a bloom, lies beyond what this figure describes.

Testing the 2026 assessment would require the team's defined observation period and the boundaries it uses to mark where a bloom begins and ends. It would also need comparable satellite measurements for each year and the method for calculating each of the three measures. Those details lie outside NASA's public account. The assessment therefore stands as an informed expert judgement. It is ambitious, clearly qualified, and limited to the satellite era of the team's own observations. It signals something unusual, and a measured confirmation lies ahead.

Even a confirmed ranking would answer one particular kind of question. Extent, intensity and duration describe the display. Three further questions concern the living system underneath it. The first is diversity: how many kinds of plants, insects and other organisms share the bloom. The second is the seed bank, the store of seeds resting in the soil for future rains. A bloom spends some of those seeds and can also produce new ones. Whether this year's flowering refilled the store depends on how many plants set seed, and that measurement belongs to fieldwork. The third is resilience: the capacity of desert life to recover across many dry and wet years. A wide, vivid, long-lasting bloom could involve many species or a few. It could come from a deep seed bank or a strained one. The satellite views alone leave those possibilities open.

This distinction gives the larger question its shape. A spectacular display shows that living seeds survived somewhere and that rare water reached them. The health and persistence of the community they belong to call for different evidence, gathered closer to the ground and over longer periods.

## From colour to understanding

That longer view is the purpose of Chávez's field project. According to NASA, the project combines satellite observations, drones and field photography with digital cameras. Drones are small remotely piloted aircraft that can photograph the land from low altitude. The project studies how biodiversity varies from place to place and over time. Its aim is to inform land-use and conservation managers, the people who decide how land is used and which areas receive protection. NASA's account describes the study as ongoing and sets out its tools and aims. Judging any completed findings, maps or management outcomes would take separate evidence from the project itself.

A hypothetical example shows how the pieces could fit together. Imagine two hillsides that look different in a satellite image during a bloom year: one shows a strong colour signal, and the other a weaker one. Researchers could send a drone over both to photograph the plant cover in finer detail. Field teams with digital cameras could then record which flowers grow on each slope and when they open. If the same contrast reappeared in a later bloom year, researchers could begin asking what the stronger hillside holds: richer soil, a deeper seed bank, more water after rain, or a wider mix of species. Each layer of observation would test the one above it. This sketch illustrates a possible method; NASA's report describes the project's tools and aims, and a comparison of this kind remains a prospect.

The approach also suits the bloom's temporary nature. Brief events reward a monitoring system ready to look when they happen. A satellite passing overhead on a regular schedule can catch the colour across a wide region. Drones and field photography can then document details within the short window while flowers are open. Over several bloom years, such records could reveal which places flower repeatedly, which flower rarely, and which hold the greatest variety of life. For conservation managers, that pattern could help identify places where desert life persists between rains. Whether it will guide real decisions depends on the project's results and on how managers use them.

The 2026 bloom has already shown something worth knowing. After a wet winter, water reached parts of an exceptionally dry desert, and seeds waiting in the soil answered with red-purple, green and white. Satellites recorded the colour from orbit on two dates. Scientists photographed the flowers on the ground and named them with stated confidence. One of them, Moreira-Muñoz, judged the event potentially the most significant in extent, intensity and duration since his team's remote-sensing observations began. The flowers are seasonal, and their brevity belongs to the finding itself.

The question now lies beneath the colour. When the petals have dried and the hillsides return to their usual browns and greys, the seeds in the soil and the records gathered from above and on the ground will hold the rest of the story. How much of the Atacama's enduring life can a few weeks of flowers help scientists find?
