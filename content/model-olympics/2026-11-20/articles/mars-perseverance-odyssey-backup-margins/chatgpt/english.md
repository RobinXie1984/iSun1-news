# Two Mars Missions Still Answer Earth. What Will It Take to Bring Their Science Back?

Exploring Mars depends on machines that can collect information, point their equipment and send their findings home. NASA, the United States civil space agency, reported on November 19, 2026, that two of those machines had encountered separate technical problems: Perseverance, a rover working on the surface, had trouble with its radios; Odyssey, a spacecraft circling the planet, had trouble with equipment that controls its orientation. Both remained in contact with Earth. Their science activities were paused while engineers investigated how to restore the capabilities that make that contact useful.

The two missions occupy different places in a larger system of exploration. A rover examines Mars close up, gathering observations from the ground. An orbiter studies the planet from above and can also carry messages between surface spacecraft and Earth. Scientific work depends on those activities fitting together. A measurement needs a working instrument, a spacecraft able to support it and a route through which the resulting data can reach researchers.

That connection gives these incidents their significance. Protective systems and alternative operating methods have preserved control of both spacecraft. The next stage involves recovering enough performance to resume useful work. For Perseverance, the immediate constraint is the flow of data. For Odyssey, it is the fuel being consumed to keep the spacecraft pointed correctly.

NASA’s November 19 account describes investigations still under way. The rover’s radio event began on October 30; Odyssey entered a protective state during November 4–6. Each has its own engineering investigation, with the causes still open. Their shared story concerns the distance between keeping a spacecraft manageable and restoring its daily scientific role.

Perseverance’s difficulty first appeared through a small, regular signal inside the vehicle. Its primary radio for sending data normally supplied the main computer with an electronic heartbeat: a recurring message indicating that the radio was responsive. On October 30, that heartbeat stopped.

After 25 missed heartbeats, spanning about 100 seconds, the rover’s automatic fault protection acted. It switched off the unresponsive radio, selected the backup and placed Perseverance in safe mode. This protective state suspends nonessential activity so the spacecraft and its operators can concentrate on maintaining stability and diagnosing the problem.

The sequence illustrates a basic requirement of remote exploration. A spacecraft needs rules for handling trouble as it develops. Here, the main computer had a defined signal to watch and a defined response when that signal disappeared for long enough. The heartbeat was an internal health check between pieces of equipment. Its disappearance told the computer that the radio required attention; the underlying reason remained a separate question for engineers.

By November 19, Perseverance had left safe mode and was stable. Its backup radio, however, presented another concern: output power measured about half the expected level. The team suspended use of both radios while assessing the causes and conditions for safe operation.

The quantity matters. Radio output power describes the strength of the transmitted signal. The reported measurement concerns that power relative to its expected value. Turning it into a percentage of normal data delivery would require further information about the communications link. Engineers therefore face two related tasks: understanding the backup’s performance and continuing work toward recovery of the primary radio.

Perseverance still has another way to communicate. Its high-gain antenna, which concentrates a radio signal toward a chosen direction, can reach Earth directly using X-band, a range of radio frequencies. NASA’s Deep Space Network receives that communication. The network is an international system of large antennas, with complexes at Goldstone in California, Madrid in Spain and Canberra in Australia, supporting communication with spacecraft across interplanetary space.

The rover’s customary route uses UHF, or ultra-high-frequency radio, to send data to an orbiter over the relatively short distance between the surface and Mars orbit. The orbiter then forwards the information across the much longer distance to Earth. Orbiters can carry larger radio equipment and have more power available for communications than rovers. That division of work gives a surface explorer access to a more capable onward delivery system.

An everyday illustration helps explain the present difficulty. Imagine a delivery business whose parcel van becomes unavailable while its telephone remains usable. Staff can discuss the fault, coordinate repairs and exchange essential information. The daily volume of parcels still depends on restoring a suitable delivery method. In this hypothetical example, the telephone represents the ability to remain in touch, while the van represents the capacity to move a larger load. The actual spacecraft use radio for both purposes; the comparison concerns how much work each available route can carry.

NASA says Perseverance’s direct Earth link has a significantly lower data rate than its usual orbiter relay. Data rate is the amount of information a connection can carry in a given time. The direct route preserves a valuable channel for commands and reports about the rover’s condition, while imposing tighter limits on the information that can flow home.

The team has reserved that capacity for diagnosis. Science and instrument activities are paused, and the cameras are taking no new images. That acquisition pause also affects the public raw-image feed, where people ordinarily follow the rover through its latest pictures.

This is a concrete consequence of a communications problem. The rover’s ability to take an observation and the mission’s ability to handle the resulting information belong to the same operational chain. During recovery, measurements about the spacecraft itself take priority because they help engineers decide what the hardware can safely do next.

For people following the mission, the pictures provide a particularly visible measure of activity. Their current absence has a specific explanation: image-taking is paused. Meanwhile, the direct radio connection continues to support the less visible work of investigating the fault. The mission is active in an engineering sense while its usual scientific output remains suspended.

Odyssey’s problem concerns another requirement that becomes easy to overlook when a spacecraft is described simply as being “in orbit”: it must control which way it faces.

Travelling around Mars and turning to face a particular direction are different motions. An orbiter can continue along its path while changing the direction of its instruments, antennas or solar arrays. Reliable orientation allows its equipment to serve its intended purpose.

Odyssey normally performs this work with reaction wheels. These are spinning devices inside the spacecraft. When a motor changes a wheel’s spin, the spacecraft responds by turning in the opposite sense. Engineers use this exchange of rotational motion to adjust the spacecraft’s orientation. The wheels consume electrical power, and routine wheel-based pointing conserves the propellant that a thruster would expend.

Odyssey carries four reaction wheels. One failed in 2013. NASA says its wheel-based orientation arrangement requires at least three, so the recent stoppage of another wheel has taken the spacecraft below that requirement. It is now using thrusters supplied with hydrazine fuel to control its orientation.

The spacecraft was in safe mode from November 4 to November 6. By NASA’s November 19 update, it had exited that state, while its instruments and science activities remained offline. The thrusters provide a working means of pointing the craft as the investigation proceeds.

They also draw on a finite supply. The team estimates that Odyssey has enough fuel for one to two months if it relies solely on thrusters for orientation, and engineers are refining that estimate.

The condition attached to the number carries much of its meaning. Fuel use depends on how the spacecraft is operated. A period of exclusive thruster-based pointing has one consumption pattern; recovering a dependable wheel could change that pattern. The estimate describes the endurance of a particular operating method under the team’s current assessment. The spacecraft’s longer-term prospects remain tied to the recovery work and the revised fuel calculation.

One test supplied a reason to continue investigating the wheel: it moved in response to a command. That is a useful observation. It establishes a response under the conditions of that test, while reliable service requires evidence of sustained, repeatable performance.

This distinction is central to the recovery effort. A wheel participates in ongoing control of the spacecraft. Its value comes from responding predictably as orientation needs change. Engineers must evaluate whether the observed movement can develop into dependable operation and whether the spacecraft can then support its intended work.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory leads the recovery work. JPL is a California-based mission engineering laboratory managed by the California Institute of Technology, or Caltech. Lockheed Martin built and operates Odyssey. Together, the teams are evaluating a machine whose service history extends across a quarter of a century.

Odyssey launched in April 2001 and entered Mars orbit that October. Its original mission was designed for just over two and a half years. Around 25 years later, NASA describes it as its longest-running Mars mission.

That history gives substance to the current stakes. Odyssey has mapped the composition of the Martian surface, helped assess landing locations and investigated water or buried ice near the surface. It has also served as a communications relay for other Mars missions. Its contribution therefore includes both observations gathered by its own instruments and information carried home on behalf of other spacecraft.

A long-lived orbiter becomes part of the working infrastructure around a planet. Its scientific instruments have one role; its communications equipment provides another. Restoring Odyssey would return both kinds of service, giving the recovery effort consequences beyond its own next observation.

Its age supplies context for the care involved in that effort. The spacecraft has already operated far beyond its original design duration, and the earlier wheel failure changed the equipment available for handling a further problem. The cause of the present stoppage remains under investigation. Twenty-five years of service records an extraordinary operating history; a diagnosis requires evidence about this particular fault.

Across the two missions, backup arrangements have provided different kinds of margin. Perseverance’s direct Earth connection preserves communication while limiting data delivery. Odyssey’s thrusters preserve pointing while consuming fuel. Each alternative gives engineers room to work, and each comes with a cost that shapes the investigation.

Those costs also explain why leaving safe mode is one stage of recovery. Safe mode addresses the immediate need for a stable, manageable spacecraft. Scientific operation asks more of the system: instruments must work, orientation must be dependable, and information must reach Earth at a useful rate. A spacecraft can satisfy the first requirement while engineers continue testing the others.

The distinction helps give the announced schedules their proper weight. The Odyssey team aims to restore science and relay activities during the last week of November, following tests and analysis. Perseverance’s team hopes to resume science by the end of November. As of NASA’s November 19 report, both dates are goals whose achievement depends on the recovery work.

For Perseverance, the next meaningful change would connect safe radio operation and usable communications capacity with renewed instrument activity. For Odyssey, it would connect dependable pointing and a workable fuel outlook with the return of science and relay service. The details differ, but each mission needs its surviving capabilities to support a complete task again.

Earth can still reach both spacecraft, and their teams retain ways to investigate the problems. The coming tests must show how far those preserved capabilities can carry them. When a spacecraft answers a command, what further evidence will establish that it can once again do the work it was sent to Mars to perform?
