# More Than 100 AI "Experts" and the Most Important Role Is Still Yours

**The short version:** On October 23, 2026, Tencent published part two of its WorkBuddy series. The story describes an AI agent workbench where a "team lead" breaks a task into pieces, hands the pieces to specialist personas, and returns a single unified draft for a human to review. Tencent says there are more than 100 built-in "Experts" covering areas such as finance, law, market research and copywriting.

The idea is appealing because it lets you organize AI work the way a well-run team organizes people. But this is Tencent describing its own product. It is not an independent evaluation, a scientific breakthrough, or evidence that these features first launched on October 23. Tencent itself says no agent is 100 percent perfect and presents the specialists' output as a starting point for human review.

My conclusion: the specialist label is a useful way to organize work, but it does not prove the work is right. A coordinated team of AI roles may produce a cleaner draft. It does not remove the need for someone to check whether that draft is true. The human reviewer is still the most important seat at the table, and the product shifts what that person does more than it shrinks their responsibility.

The rest of this piece covers what Tencent actually said, what it didn't, and what I'd want to know before trusting the result.

---

## The pitch: from one assistant to a small firm

Most of us have used AI the same way for a while. You open a chat, ask one assistant to do everything, and get back one answer. Need financial analysis, a legal read and a polished summary? You either ask for all of it at once and hope, or you split the task yourself across several prompts and stitch the results together.

Tencent's October 23 story describes a different setup. Based on Tencent's account, it works like this:

1. **You describe the task.**
2. **A team lead splits the work.**
3. **Specialists work at the same time**, sharing information as they go.
4. **You get back one unified draft**, not a stack of separate notes.
5. **You monitor, review and decide** which improvements to accept.

You can also skip the delegation: Tencent says users can browse the catalogue and pick a specific Expert directly.

Anyone who has run a small company will recognize the structure. It is a project manager with a bench of specialists, except the specialists are software personas and the manager is also software. The organizing idea is the familiar one: experts on the parts, a lead to coordinate, a single deliverable at the end.

Tencent's story title says it plainly: your new AI colleague brought a few friends.

---

## What "more than 100 Experts" actually means

This is the number most likely to be misread, so it's worth being precise.

According to Tencent, there are **more than 100 built-in Experts**. Each is a ready-to-use role with defined work methods and tools for an area such as finance, law, market research or copywriting.

That is a catalogue of roles. Based on the brief, it does **not** mean:

- 100 separate foundation models,
- 100 human employees,
- 100 licensed professionals, or
- guaranteed expertise in any subject.

The report does not identify which models sit underneath these roles, and it does not establish how accurate any Expert has been when tested.

**My analysis:** In AI products, the word "Expert" works more like a job description than a credential. It tells the system how to approach a task: which method to follow, which tools to use, what format to produce. That is genuinely useful, because structure often improves output. But a job description is not a licence, and a persona labelled "contract review" has not passed a bar exam. Given what Tencent has published, the honest reading is that these are **pre-built specialist workflows**, and how good each one is remains an open, testable question.

## The examples are illustrations, not case studies

Tencent's story uses scenarios to explain the concept: a **Friday proposal**, a **financial-analysis agent** and a **contract-review agent**.

These are explanatory examples in a vendor's story. They are not reported customer incidents or verified client projects. The brief provides no named customers, no revenue figures, no hours saved, no jobs replaced, no mistakes caught, and no privacy incidents. I won't invent any.

The choice of examples is still revealing. Financial analysis and contract review are areas where:

- the work is structured enough to divide into parts,
- the output looks professional and authoritative, and
- **a confident mistake can be expensive.**

So these examples show both why the idea is attractive and why caution matters.

## The tension: a label organizes, evidence verifies

Here is the central issue in the story, and it applies well beyond Tencent.

**A specialist label helps organize a workflow. The reader still needs evidence that the unified answer is correct.**

Consider what the unified draft does to the person reading it. Under the old one-assistant model, you could often see where the work was weak. One response might have a solid summary and shaky numbers, and the inconsistency was visible.

A coordinated system aims to remove that visible inconsistency. Specialists share information, a lead combines their work, and you receive one coherent document. That coherence is the product's appeal. It also means a polished draft is not the same as a verified draft.

**My analysis, as a hypothetical:** Imagine a proposal where a financial persona produces a projection, a market-research persona produces a sizing estimate, and a copywriting persona turns both into a persuasive narrative. If the sizing estimate rests on a bad assumption, a good copywriting step may make that assumption more convincing, not less. Sharing information helps the parts agree with each other. Agreement and correctness are different things. Tencent's description, as reported, does not establish that the specialists independently check each other. It establishes that they share information and that the output is unified.

To be clear, this is a question, not an accusation. The brief does not say the system lacks cross-checks. It says the source does not establish them. **Missing information is not proof that a safeguard is missing.** It is a reason to ask.

## What Tencent admits, and why it matters

Tencent's own framing is more careful than the usual "AI does it all" pitch. According to the story, Tencent acknowledges that **no agent is 100 percent perfect** and describes the specialists' work as **a starting point for human review**. Throughout the description, the user monitors, reviews and decides which improvements to accept.

That candour is worth noting. A vendor putting human review at the centre of its own product story is setting realistic expectations.

But "a starting point for human review" carries a lot of weight. It means the value of the system depends on the quality of the review it gets. A great draft reviewed carelessly can be worse than an average draft reviewed carefully, because the great draft is easier to trust too quickly.

## What we don't know

Here is the gap between the description and the evidence I'd want as an operator or investor. According to the brief, Tencent's story does **not** provide:

- **An independent controlled benchmark.** There is no comparison against a single assistant or a human team.
- **An error rate.** We don't know how often the unified draft is wrong, or in what ways.
- **An accuracy gain or cost saving.** No measured productivity, latency or cost figures are reported.
- **Permissions and data security details.** It is not established what the agents can access or how data is handled.
- **Cross-agent correlation.** We don't know whether specialists built on similar underlying models tend to make the same mistakes together. That matters, because five agents sharing a blind spot are not five independent opinions.
- **Legal licensure and liability.** Nothing establishes who is responsible if a contract-review persona misses something.

Each of these is unknown, not known to be bad. Product stories often leave this material for documentation, contracts and later evaluations. But until it appears, the responsible reading is: **promising structure, unproven performance.**

## A practical test: how to evaluate a "team of AI experts"

This is my own framework, not Tencent's. If you are considering any coordinated-agent tool, Tencent's or another, these are the questions I'd ask, with concrete tests for each.

**1. Can I see the seams?**
A unified draft is convenient. Can you trace which specialist produced which claim? If a number appears in the final proposal, can you find where it came from? If not, your review is checking a finished surface, not the reasoning behind it.

*Concrete test:* Pick three factual claims in the output and try to trace each to its source. Time how long it takes. If it's slow, review will be slow too, and that eats into any claimed productivity gain.

**2. Do the specialists disagree?**
Real expert teams argue. A finance lead questions the market sizing, and legal raises a clause the copywriter wanted to soften. If the system only shows agreement, ask whether disagreement is being smoothed away during unification.

*Concrete test:* Give it a task with a planted inconsistency, for example two input figures that can't both be true. See whether the draft flags the conflict or quietly picks one.

**3. Are the specialists actually independent?**
Several personas on similar underlying models may share failure modes. Diversity of labels is not diversity of judgment.

*Concrete test:* Ask the same question through the full team and through one general assistant. If the answers match closely, the team may be adding formatting rather than independent checking.

**4. Who signs off?**
In finance and law, someone remains accountable. A persona's label does not move that accountability anywhere. Know before you start whose name goes on the final output.

**5. What does the system touch?**
Before sharing a client file or contract with any agent workbench, find out its permissions, retention and data boundaries in writing. The brief establishes none of these for this product, which means you need to check, not that you should assume the worst.

## Why I still find this interesting

I don't want to come across as only sceptical, because the underlying idea is good.

Breaking work into roles is one of the oldest productivity methods we have. Consulting firms, newsrooms and trading desks all run on specialization plus coordination. Applying that pattern to AI agents is a natural next step, and it may well produce better-structured first drafts than one generalist assistant working alone.

There's a quieter benefit too. A catalogue of more than 100 defined roles works like a **menu of methods**. Even if you never fully trust the output, seeing how a "market research" role is set up to approach a problem can make your own thinking sharper. The role becomes a checklist you can borrow.

The open question is not whether coordinated specialists are a sensible design. It's whether **this** coordination produces answers that are **reliably correct**, and how much of the review work the human still has to carry. That is an empirical question, and Tencent's October 23 story doesn't answer it.

---

## The shift for the human at the desk

**My analysis:** If tools like this spread, the reviewer's job changes in a specific way.

The reviewer used to be the editor of a rough draft: fixing grammar, filling gaps, adding structure. The coordinated system handles much of that. What's left is harder. The reviewer becomes an **auditor of a polished draft**: checking claims, testing assumptions, and resisting the pull of a document that already sounds finished.

That calls for different skills, including domain knowledge, scepticism, and the habit of going back to primary sources instead of trusting a confident summary. The tool can raise the floor on structure. It does not raise the floor on judgment, which still comes from the person reviewing.

---

## Bottom line

Tencent's October 23 WorkBuddy story describes an attractive design: more than 100 built-in specialist roles, a team lead that splits the work, specialists running in parallel and sharing information, and a single draft returned for human review. It's a recognizable model of how good teams work, applied to AI.

It is still a vendor's description of its own product. There is no independent benchmark, no error rate, no measured saving, and no established detail on permissions, security, cross-agent independence or liability. Tencent itself says no agent is perfect and frames the output as a starting point.

So the takeaway is simple: **the label organizes the work, and evidence verifies it.** More than 100 Experts can give you a better first draft. Deciding whether that draft is true is still the human reviewer's job.
