# €204 Billion Below the Commission's Plan, Horizon Europe Commitments 57% Above the 2021–2027 Allocation in 2025 Prices: Can Costa's Budget Bargain Hold?

On 21 November 2026, António Costa put a new set of numbers on the table in Brussels. Costa is President of the European Council, the forum where EU national leaders meet to set the Union's political direction. His proposal concerns the EU's next long-term budget, formally called the Multiannual Financial Framework, which would run for seven years, from 2028 to 2034. It is an offer for negotiation. EU leaders are scheduled to meet on 26 and 27 November to debate it, and as of 22 November that meeting is still ahead. The amounts for 2028–2034 in this story are proposals: they await agreement, and their money remains unspent. The figures for the current 2021–2027 framework and today's average national contribution rate of 0.80% describe arrangements already in place, and they serve as the comparison points.

Several EU bodies share this story, and their names sound alike. The European Commission is the EU's executive. It drafts proposals and runs programmes, and it made the first proposal for this budget. The Council's own explainer describes that earlier plan as worth close to €2 trillion. The European Council, which Costa chairs, gathers the leaders. The Council of the European Union is a separate institution that gathers ministers from national governments and formally adopts EU laws. The European Parliament also has a vote. Costa's task at this stage is to find a package that every national government might accept.

The stakes reach well beyond Brussels. A seven-year framework sets spending limits for large programmes. They include Horizon Europe, the EU's main fund for research and innovation, and Erasmus+, which pays for study and training abroad. Other parts of the budget cover security and defence, climate and social goals. The EU pays for its budget through what it calls "own resources", the revenue sources assigned to the Union. One of these own resources is a contribution from each national government, calculated on the country's gross national income, so governments pay roughly in line with the size of their economies. The Union also has other existing own-resource streams, and Costa proposes adding five new ones. Each government therefore weighs two questions: how much it pays, and what the Union buys with that money. Research funding, which can shape laboratories and careers for years, sits inside that larger bargain.

## A smaller total and a bigger research line

Costa's package contains what looks like a contradiction. A two-page summary table published by the Council on 21 November gives the overall ceiling in millions of euros, counted as "commitments", a term explained below. In current prices, the Commission's plan totals €1,984,894 million, or almost €1.98 trillion. Costa's plan totals €1,780,880 million, about €1.78 trillion. The gap is €204,014 million: roughly €204 billion, or about 10.3% of the Commission's figure. By that measure, Costa has trimmed the plan.

Yet Costa also presents his package as a strong one for research. In the same table, measured in 2025 prices, Horizon Europe receives €89,317 million in the current 2021–2027 budget. The Commission proposed €154,882 million for the next period. Costa proposes €140,000 million. Compared with the programme's current allocation, his figure is about 57% larger. Costa also says Erasmus+ would grow by 25% compared with the 2021–2027 period, measured in constant prices.

So the proposed total shrinks while the proposed research line grows. Both claims come from the same official documents, and both hold up arithmetically. Reconciling them requires a closer look at how the numbers are measured.

## Two measuring sticks and two comparisons

The first key is the comparison point. The €204 billion reduction compares Costa's plan for the next budget with the Commission's plan for the next budget. The 57% research increase compares Costa's plan for the next budget with the budget in force today. Each statement compares against a different baseline.

Seen against the Commission, Costa's research figure is actually lower, by €14,882 million, or a little under 10%. Seen against today's programme, it is a large increase. Horizon Europe shows the pattern in a single line of the table. A reader who hears "Costa cut the budget" and "Costa boosted research" is hearing two accurate statements, each tied to its own comparison.

The second key is the price basis. The Council's table gives every figure twice: once in current prices and once in constant 2025 prices. Current prices count the euros assigned to each future year, including the effect of expected price rises. Constant prices remove those rises, so every year is measured in euros with 2025 purchasing power. That is like measuring with a ruler that keeps its length. Over seven years, a basket of goods usually costs more each year. Constant prices ask how many 2025 baskets the money could buy.

This matters because mixing the two rulers gives misleading results. Costa's €1.78 trillion is a current-price figure. The current budget's €1,270,457 million, about €1.27 trillion, is a constant-price figure. Setting one beside the other and calling the difference "growth" mixes inflation-boosted euros with fixed-value euros. The fair comparison stays on one ruler. In 2025 prices, the table lists €1,270,457 million for 2021–2027, €1,763,056 million for the Commission's proposal and €1,582,516 million for Costa's proposal.

On that shared basis, Costa's ceiling sits roughly a quarter above today's framework and about €180 billion below the Commission's plan. Those two figures are simple arithmetic from the table's constant-price column. The same rule applies to research. Costa's Horizon Europe line also appears as €158,187 million in current prices. That number belongs in the current-price column, and the 57% increase comes from comparing constant-price figures with each other.

The defence figures need the same care. Costa cites an increase for defence, while the summary table groups spending under a broader heading: resilience, security, defence and space. Defence is one part of that larger group. A defence-only percentage therefore requires its own category definition, which the summary table leaves for more detailed documents.

## What a "commitment" is

The third key lies in the word "commitments". The Council's summary table states its amounts in commitments. The draft conclusions, a separate 53-page Council document prepared for the November summit, propose a different total for payment appropriations: €1,553,665 million in 2025 prices.

**An illustrative analogy:** think of a family planning several years ahead. It sets a ceiling on everything it may agree to buy over that period: a kitchen renovation, school fees, a new car. That ceiling is like a commitment. It is permission to sign contracts. The family then pays the bills over time, some this year and some in later years. The cash leaving its account each year is like a payment. When the family plans carefully, it can lower its overall ceiling compared with an earlier, more ambitious plan and still reserve more for school fees than it spends today. The two totals are linked, but they measure different things.

The analogy helps with the accounting only. The EU's legal rules for commitments and payments are its own, and a household budget offers a rough picture of them. The main point carries over. If the proposal is approved, a commitment of €140,000 million for research would give the EU authority to take on research obligations up to that level across the seven years. Awarding grants or signing contracts, paying scientists and producing discoveries are separate later steps. The gap between the commitment and payment totals reflects two different measures; judging any shortfall, or how much money has reached recipients, requires a payment schedule and later spending records.

## Who pays for the package

With the arithmetic resolved, the harder question comes into view. If the overall plan is larger than today's framework in constant prices, and research grows sharply, where does the money come from? Costa's answer has two parts: the income-based national contributions and new own-resource streams.

On national contributions, Costa says the average contribution would rise from today's 0.80% to a proposed 0.82% of member states' gross national income. Gross national income, or GNI, measures the total income earned by a country's residents and businesses in a year. The change is 0.02 percentage points. In relative terms, that is an increase of about 2.5% in the average rate. This rate applies to whole national economies. It is separate from a household tax rate and from a share of all government spending.

An average also hides variation among countries. Some governments may pay more and others less. Each country's bill in euros also depends on the size of its economy and on other budget arrangements. Costa's figure gives the overall level. The country-by-country picture belongs to the negotiations ahead.

The second part is revenue from other sources. Costa proposes five new own-resource streams for the Union. One is linked to the digital-service revenues of certain large digital companies. A second comes from the carbon border adjustment mechanism, or CBAM, which places a carbon cost on certain imported goods. A third relates to electrical and electronic equipment that goes uncollected as waste. A fourth concerns tobacco products. A fifth comes from the emissions trading system, or ETS, under which companies buy and trade permits to emit carbon dioxide.

The detailed draft describes the electronics stream precisely. It would be calculated on the weight of uncollected electrical and electronic equipment, at a proposed rate of €2.25 per kilogram. Costa's public remarks describe it more loosely. The documents examined for this article set out the proposed basis and rate; who would ultimately bear that cost, whether governments, producers or consumers, lies beyond their scope.

Costa says these proposed streams, combined with the EU's existing revenue streams, can generate about €60 billion a year. That is his estimate for the combined set, looking ahead to future years. His remarks give the combined figure; a separately verified yield for the five new streams, and a full reconciliation of EU revenue, would require fuller evidence. Costa also proposes phasing in the ETS and tobacco elements gradually, to soften their uneven effects on different member states. Revenue from crypto-assets and from radio spectrum appears in the draft as a subject for further work, outside the five proposed streams.

Taken together, the financing works like this. Costa reduces the total compared with the Commission, so he chooses which programmes give up part of their Commission-proposed amounts. He protects or raises priorities such as research and student exchange compared with today. He keeps the average national contribution close to its present level. New revenue streams would help close the gap. The success of that plan depends on the new revenue being approved and then collected at roughly the expected level.

The package also contains targets. Costa proposes funding for housing from the start of the period. He proposes that 35% of spending across programmes support climate and environmental goals. He also proposes that 14% of national and regional allocations go to social goals. These percentages use different bases and may overlap, so adding them together gives no meaningful share of the whole budget. They are intended spending rules. Whether they are met would only become clear years from now.

## The long road from offer to law

The planned timetable is tight. The stated aim is a political agreement by the end of 2026, followed by the legal acts in 2027, so that the new budget can begin in January 2028. That is a goal, and the dates depend on governments reaching agreement.

The legal steps are demanding. According to the Council's explainer, the budget regulation needs the consent of the European Parliament and then a unanimous vote in the Council of the EU. Every member government therefore holds a veto. The decision on own resources, which would create the new revenue streams, also has to be approved by each member state under its own constitutional rules. In some countries, that can involve national parliaments. The proposed digital resource depends on a separate EU directive with its own timetable. The draft conclusions still contain bracketed alternatives on some provisions concerning value-added tax and the corrections some countries receive on their contributions. Those brackets mark unresolved points.

The sources also have limits. Costa's press conference sets out his case for the package. The draft conclusions contain the negotiating text. The summary table supplies the numbers. The Council's explainer describes the procedure. All four come from EU institutions, so their agreement shows internal consistency; confirmation from governments or outside analysts would have to come from separate sources. This account draws on the draft's opening sections and its revenue sections; the draft's full spending and legal provisions run across its 53 pages. The documents examined here establish the proposal, its arithmetic and its procedures; specific reactions from member governments, and who would ultimately bear the costs, lie beyond this review.

The numbers that first seemed contradictory have an explanation. The proposed total is smaller than the Commission's plan, the proposed Horizon Europe commitment is larger than the programme's current allocation, and each claim uses its own baseline and price basis. The open question is political and financial. Can the member governments agree on how to divide a 0.02-point rise in the average contribution among themselves, approve five new revenue streams through their own national procedures and accept the programmes that receive less than the Commission proposed, in time for the budget to begin in January 2028? The next test is the leaders' meeting scheduled for 26 and 27 November, and its outcome still lies ahead.
