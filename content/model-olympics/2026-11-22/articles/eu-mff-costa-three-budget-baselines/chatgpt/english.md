# Europe’s €204 Billion Budget Trim Has a Research Promise Inside—Can the Bargain Hold?

Europe’s next seven-year budget is becoming a negotiation over how much governments will commit to shared ambitions, and where the money will come from. On 21 November 2026, António Costa, President of the European Council, the forum of EU national leaders, proposed a smaller spending framework for 2028–2034 than the European Commission had sought. His offer still gives Horizon Europe, the EU’s research and innovation programme, substantially more than its current allocation. The combination puts a revealing question before governments: how can a budget shrink against one proposal, expand research against today’s programme, and keep average national contributions close to their present share of national income?

The EU’s long-term budget, formally called the Multiannual Financial Framework, sets spending limits over seven years. That extended horizon matters for activities whose planning reaches across several annual budgets. Research programmes, for example, need room to support work that takes time to organise and carry out. The framework establishes the financial space within which subsequent decisions can happen. Its size also expresses a political judgment about which responsibilities governments want to finance together.

Several institutions have different jobs in making that judgment binding. The European Commission, the EU’s executive, put forward the earlier budget proposal in July 2025. Costa’s role is to help national leaders find common political ground. Member-state ministers sit in a separate institution, the Council of the EU, which has a formal legislative role alongside the European Parliament. These distinctions explain why a proposal from the European Council President can move negotiations forward while its spending and revenue provisions remain subject to agreement and legal approval.

As of 22 November 2026, Hong Kong time, the special leaders’ meeting scheduled for 26–27 November was still ahead. Costa’s text was a negotiating offer. Its immediate significance lay in the choices it placed before governments: a lower total than the Commission requested, substantial proposed research funding, and a revenue bargain intended to limit the change in average national contributions.

The arithmetic becomes clearer once three objects are kept in view: the current 2021–2027 framework, the Commission’s proposal for 2028–2034, and Costa’s proposal for that same future period. A comparison between the two future proposals measures the scale of Costa’s trim. A comparison with the existing framework measures the change from the earlier seven-year settlement. Each answers a useful question.

The Council’s summary table puts the Commission’s proposed total at **€1,984,894 million** and Costa’s at **€1,780,880 million**, both in current-price commitments. Subtracting the second from the first gives **€204,014 million**, or about €204 billion. That is approximately 10.3% of the Commission’s figure. The reduction therefore describes how much Costa has lowered the proposed future ceiling relative to the Commission’s opening position.

“Current prices” means the amounts reflect the prices used for the years concerned. For a comparison across different seven-year periods, the table also supplies figures expressed in 2025 prices. This holds the price measuring stick steady, helping readers separate changes in the budget’s stated size from changes associated with the price level. The choice of column matters as much as the choice of programme.

In those **2025 prices**, the current framework totals **€1,270,457 million**, the Commission’s future proposal **€1,763,056 million**, and Costa’s **€1,582,516 million**. On that basis, Costa’s total stands €312,059 million, or about 24.6%, above the earlier framework. The comparison establishes an increase in the table’s overall constant-price total. Assessing the resources available for individual activities also requires attention to their allocations and to any changes in programme scope.

An everyday illustration helps. Imagine a household planning seven years of renovations. An architect proposes a large package; the household then adopts a cheaper version. Within that revised plan, the amount reserved for insulation could still exceed what the household allocated during its previous renovation cycle. The new total is smaller relative to the architect’s proposal, while the insulation allowance is larger relative to the earlier plan. Both descriptions are accurate because each names its own starting point.

Horizon Europe makes this distinction concrete. In 2025 prices, the current programme’s allocation is **€89,317 million**. The Commission proposed **€154,882 million**, and Costa proposes **€140,000 million**. His research line is therefore €50,683 million, about 57%, above the current programme, while falling €14,882 million below the Commission’s request. The proposed research expansion and the reduction from the Commission’s ambition coexist within the same programme.

For researchers, those two comparisons describe different stakes. The earlier programme provides a reference for the scale of support already planned across the current period. The Commission’s proposal provides a reference for the larger ambition placed on the negotiating table. Costa’s offer preserves a substantial increase against the first benchmark and reduces the amount sought under the second. Whether that allocation becomes available for particular projects depends on the eventual settlement and subsequent funding decisions.

There is another distinction inside every large budget number: a commitment and a payment occur at different stages. A commitment ceiling sets the limit for financial obligations that can be undertaken. Payments concern the money used to meet obligations as they fall due. Returning briefly to the hypothetical renovation, authorising contracts and paying contractors over time are separate steps. The illustration explains the timing distinction; EU appropriations follow their own legal rules.

Costa’s draft separately proposes **€1,553,665 million in payment appropriations, measured in 2025 prices**. That figure belongs to the payment side of the framework. The summary table’s totals belong to commitments. Keeping the two measures separate preserves a basic fact about the story’s present stage: a proposed ceiling describes potential financial authority. Grants awarded, cash transferred and research completed are later events, each requiring its own evidence.

The harder political question begins with financing. Costa proposes moving average national contributions from **0.80% to 0.82% of member-state gross national income**, or GNI, a measure of national income. The change is **0.02 percentage points**. This is a claim about the average contribution share, with each country’s eventual euro amount also depending on its income and the allocation arrangements.

A percentage of national income can remain close to its earlier level while the euro amount changes. If the income base grows, the same share produces more euros; if it falls, that share produces fewer. This is a mathematical relationship, rather than a forecast for Europe’s economy. It helps explain why a contribution ratio alone supplies only part of the financing picture. Country-level bills and the ultimate distribution of costs remain unresolved in the material available.

Costa’s revenue proposal supplies another part. He identifies five new streams connected to specified services of large digital companies, the carbon border adjustment mechanism, electrical and electronic equipment left uncollected, tobacco products, and the emissions trading system. In EU budget language, “own resources” are revenues assigned to financing the Union’s budget. Their design determines how money enters the shared budget; their wider economic effects depend on who pays and how businesses and governments respond.

The two carbon-related streams concern different mechanisms. The carbon border adjustment mechanism concerns carbon pricing at the border. The emissions trading system concerns allowances associated with emissions. Placing revenue from these mechanisms in the budget links part of its financing to the systems used to price carbon. The revenue estimate consequently depends on the rules adopted and the activities to which they apply.

The equipment provision shows why precise wording matters. Paragraph 145(iii) of the draft specifies a measure based on the **weight of electrical and electronic equipment left uncollected**, with a proposed rate of **€2.25 per kilogram**. Collection and recycling describe different stages in handling discarded equipment. The draft’s collection-based measure gives a particular calculation base; the eventual distribution of its cost would depend on the final legal and implementation arrangements.

Costa estimates that the proposed streams, **together with existing revenue streams**, could generate about **€60 billion a year**. That combined scope is essential. The estimate concerns a financing system containing both proposed and existing elements. Actual receipts would depend on adoption, timing and the performance of the relevant revenue bases.

It would also take a fuller financing account to connect that annual estimate to the entire seven-year spending offer. An annual flow and a seven-year commitment ceiling measure different things. Phased implementation can change the amount available from one year to the next, while commitments can lead to payments over time. The headline revenue estimate therefore helps describe Costa’s intended bargain, with the detailed funding path still dependent on legal design and subsequent decisions.

The distribution of that burden is already visible in the proposal. Costa suggests phasing in the emissions-trading and tobacco streams to ease unequal effects among member states. The draft also retains bracketed alternatives for some value-added-tax and correction provisions. These brackets mark choices still open in negotiations. Cryptoassets and spectrum revenues remain subjects for further work, giving them a different status from the five proposed streams.

This makes the revenue discussion inseparable from the spending discussion. Two governments could favour the same research allocation and assess its financing differently because the revenue rules affect their economies differently. That is a general implication of combining shared spending with varied revenue bases; the available documents establish the proposed mechanisms rather than individual governments’ positions. Costa’s package asks leaders to consider the total, the priorities inside it and the distribution of the financing burden together.

Other provisions show the breadth of that political negotiation. Costa proposes housing funding from the framework’s start, a **35% climate and environment spending target across programmes**, and a **14% social-goals target within national and regional funding envelopes**. These are proposed directions for spending. Their percentages use different bases, and individual spending can serve overlapping aims.

For example, a hypothetical housing project might improve living conditions and reduce energy use at the same time. That illustrates how policy purposes can overlap; eligibility and accounting would follow the adopted rules. Adding 35% and 14% into a single budget share would obscure both the different bases and the possible overlap. Evidence of success would eventually require a further step, from amounts assigned to outcomes such as completed housing or measured environmental improvements.

The remaining path is institutional as well as financial. The stated ambition is an agreement by the end of 2026, followed by legal acts in 2027, allowing the new framework to begin in January 2028. The MFF regulation requires unanimity in the Council of the EU after the European Parliament gives its consent. The own-resources decision also requires national adoption under each country’s constitutional requirements. The proposed digital resource has a separate underlying directive and applicability timetable.

A leaders’ agreement could therefore provide a major political milestone, followed by several distinct legal steps. The schedule matters because spending authority and the revenues intended to support it must become operational through their respective procedures. A planned January 2028 start remains an objective whose achievement depends on those decisions.

The official documents make the arithmetic of Costa’s offer understandable. They also leave its political durability open. A framework can be €204 billion below the Commission’s proposal while placing research about 57% above its current allocation, because the comparisons use different baselines. Keeping average national contribution shares close to their present level adds a further bargain over revenue, with the costs and approvals spread across several mechanisms. When leaders meet on 26–27 November, can they agree on those linked choices in time to turn a seven-year proposal into usable funding?
