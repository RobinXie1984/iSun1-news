# A Tiny Glass Chip Joined Two Telescopes’ Starlight. How Much Further Can It See?

On Mount Wilson in California, astronomers use telescopes spread across the mountain to study details of stars that appear extremely small from Earth. The CHARA Array, operated by Georgia State University, has six telescopes. An experimental instrument called CHARIOT has now combined light from two of them through a glass component measured in centimetres. The US National Science Foundation highlighted the work on November 19, 2026. It addresses a practical challenge in astronomy: how to bring separately collected starlight together precisely enough to extract information about distant objects.

The division of labour matters. The telescopes collect light, their separation helps reveal fine detail, and a substantial network of equipment carries and controls the beams. The chip takes over part of the optical task where those beams meet. Its small size belongs to one component inside a large observatory. The mountain’s long light paths, collecting mirrors, detectors and supporting equipment remain essential.

That arrangement gives the experiment its interest. Astronomy often advances through changes deep inside an instrument, at the point where collected light becomes a useful measurement. A smaller component can offer a different way to guide and combine that light. Its usefulness depends on what survives the journey: enough light, enough stability and enough information about the star. CHARIOT’s real-sky observations allow researchers to begin judging those qualities together.

The immediate question is simple to state: can this compact glass combiner preserve the delicate patterns in starlight well enough to make reliable measurements, particularly as the targets grow fainter?

To understand the challenge, start with the difference between collecting more light and seeing finer detail. A larger collecting surface catches more of the light arriving from a distant source. That helps astronomers measure faint objects. Resolving power concerns a different property: the ability to distinguish structures separated by a very small angle in the sky. A star can deliver plenty of light while its apparent size remains too small for a particular telescope to measure clearly.

Interferometry uses separated telescopes to investigate those tiny angular scales. Light behaves as a wave, with a repeating pattern that rises and falls. When carefully controlled beams meet, their waves reinforce or cancel one another according to how their repeating patterns line up. The resulting variations in brightness form interference fringes. Those fringes carry information about the source that produced the light.

An everyday illustration helps explain why the routes matter. Imagine two small devices tapping the surface of a tray of water in the same rhythm. Ripples spread from each device and overlap. Where crests arrive together, the combined movement grows; where a crest meets a trough, it shrinks. Move one device and the pattern shifts because the ripples travel different distances. This imagined tray illustrates how travel distance affects the alignment of waves. CHARIOT works with light, precision optical equipment and measured fringe contrast.

In the astronomical instrument, the routes from the telescopes must be carefully matched so that useful interference survives. The telescope separation establishes the scale of detail being probed. A wider separation can give access to finer angular structure, while the actual mirrors determine how much light the system collects. A pair of separated mirrors still has the collecting area of those mirrors. The space between them contributes the separation that makes the measurement sensitive to fine detail.

Fringes also require interpretation. Astronomers measure their contrast: how strongly the bright parts stand out from the dim parts. This quantity is called visibility. Light arriving from different parts of an extended source contributes overlapping patterns, affecting the contrast of the combined signal. By examining that contrast, researchers can constrain how the source’s brightness is distributed across very small angles.

A fringe measurement therefore provides a particular piece of information about an astronomical object. Building a fuller account of its structure requires suitable observations and analysis. CHARIOT’s reported achievement is a measurement of stellar interference, with future applications extending toward more demanding astronomical targets.

Inside its glass chip, microscopic channels called waveguides steer the incoming light. The researchers made these channels using ultrafast laser inscription: very short laser pulses create guiding structures inside the material. Their chosen glass was low-OH Infrasil quartz, whose low hydroxyl content helps limit a source of absorption. The instrument operates in the K-band, a range of near-infrared light beyond human vision. This is a photonic component, meaning its working medium is light.

Putting light paths inside glass changes the physical arrangement of the combining optics. The routes become part of a fabricated component that can be installed and tested. That offers researchers a way to explore alternative designs while using the observatory’s existing ability to collect and deliver starlight. The decisive test comes when the component receives light through the complete observing system, with the atmosphere and equipment contributing their own variations.

The observations unfolded well before the latest announcement. CHARIOT obtained its first real-sky fringes in May 2024. An October 2024 engineering run involved optical changes and hardware difficulties. By July 2025, a revised chip had improved throughput, and the team achieved its strongest reported precision results.

Those dates describe an instrument developing through successive trials. First fringes establish that the optical chain can produce the sought-after signal. Later runs investigate how well that signal can be measured and how changes to the equipment affect performance. Hardware trouble belongs to this engineering history: it can interrupt observations or alter measured signals, creating problems that researchers must distinguish from the behaviour of the star.

The written record followed later. Aline N. Dinkelaker and colleagues submitted their preprint on September 28, 2026. Heriot-Watt University described the research on October 27, and NSF adapted that account for its November 19 update. The two institutional reports share a reporting lineage. The experimental detail discussed here comes from the authors’ preprint; comparison with the final journal text remains pending.

The strongest precision result needs a little unpacking. For sufficiently bright stars, roughly those with K magnitudes below 2, the latest run produced raw squared-visibility measurements with relative standard errors below 1%. Squared visibility is the fringe-contrast measure multiplied by itself. Relative standard error expresses the estimated statistical uncertainty of a reported measurement as a fraction of its measured value.

Here, “raw” identifies the measurement stage before full correction for the observing system’s effects. The sub-1% figure describes the statistical precision of that particular quantity under the stated conditions. Its meaning stays attached to the bright targets and the measurement being made.

A familiar distinction is useful. Repeated readings from a measuring instrument may cluster closely together, allowing a precise estimate of their average. A shift in the instrument’s response can still move that entire cluster. Assessing the spread within a set of readings and checking the consistency between sets address different questions. In astronomy, those questions become especially important when the desired signal is small.

The CHARIOT paper reports between-measurement variation of a few percent alongside the smaller statistical errors. These figures can coexist because they describe different aspects of performance. The close grouping within a measurement is encouraging. The larger variation between measurements shows why calibration, the process of accounting for the instrument’s response, remains central to the scientific result.

Brightness introduces another boundary. K magnitude expresses how bright an object appears in this near-infrared band, with larger numbers indicating fainter sources. A bright star supplies more light for estimating fringe contrast. As the available light decreases, random fluctuations become a larger part of the measurement, and extracting a precise value becomes harder.

The team detected fringes from the fainter star HD 210824, at K = 5.212, with about 4.61% relative standard error under poor-to-moderate seeing. Seeing describes the atmospheric conditions that disturb incoming starlight. This result extends the demonstrated reach to a dimmer target, with a larger statistical uncertainty than the bright-star measurements.

There are consequently two useful milestones: finding the interference signal and measuring it closely enough for a particular scientific purpose. A detectable fringe shows that information survived the optical journey. The required precision depends on the question astronomers want to answer. A subtle contribution from material around a star may demand tighter control of the measurement than a stronger feature.

Light transmission adds another part of the picture. The authors report about 40% throughput for the fibre-connected CHARIOT beam combiner. That percentage refers to light passing through this defined subsystem. Overall observatory efficiency includes the other stages through which starlight travels.

Throughput and measurement precision answer separate questions. Throughput asks how much light emerges from an optical assembly. Precision asks how tightly a quantity can be estimated from the measurements obtained. A bright source can provide enough light for a precise statistical result even when the system loses a substantial fraction along the way. For a faint source, those losses make an already scarce signal harder to measure.

This relationship explains the importance of improving transmission. More useful light reaching the measurement stage gives researchers a stronger basis for estimating fringe contrast. The benefit still interacts with atmospheric conditions, instrument stability and calibration. Each affects how effectively the surviving light can support an astronomical conclusion.

The earlier instrument, JouFLU, provides a useful reference. According to the CHARIOT paper, JouFLU achieved sub-percent precision for stars around K < 3 and had a limiting magnitude of about 4.5. CHARIOT’s K = 5.212 example reaches a fainter fringe detection, while better precision on faint stars remains a development goal.

That comparison resists a single ranking because the measures serve different purposes. One describes precision over a brightness range; another describes the faintness at which a signal can be detected. Observing conditions also influence the result. For an astronomer planning an observation, the relevant issue is whether an instrument can measure the desired feature with adequate reliability at the target’s brightness.

CHARIOT’s other practical contribution is its role as an experimental platform. The beam combiner can be exchanged in a few hours, with optical adjustments. Researchers can therefore install a different component and investigate how its design behaves inside the observing system.

The adjustment time is part of the engineering reality. Light must enter the intended paths, emerge in a usable form and reach the measurement equipment correctly. A replaceable chip creates room for experimentation, while the surrounding optical work determines whether a particular replacement performs well. Its value lies in making alternative designs accessible to real-sky testing.

Combining more beams is one prospective direction. Each telescope pair offers information associated with its separation and orientation, so additional beams can support a richer investigation of a source’s structure. They also expand the combining task. The present demonstration establishes two-beam operation for this laser-inscribed approach in the near-infrared K-band.

The researchers’ longer-term interests include stellar companions and exozodiacal dust, the fine material around other stars, with eventual planet-related science among the possibilities. Such work depends on separating small contributions to the measured light from the much stronger stellar signal and from changes introduced by the observing system. Reliable precision on dimmer targets would make the platform more useful for those demanding questions.

The achievement already has a clear physical form: light collected by two telescopes crossed an established observatory, entered microscopic paths in glass and emerged carrying measurable stellar fringes. Successive engineering runs improved the device and exposed the boundaries of its present performance. The small component has earned a place in a much larger instrument.

Its next test follows naturally from that record. As researchers change the glass pathways, add beams and turn toward fainter stars, how much of the precision demonstrated on bright targets can they carry with them?
