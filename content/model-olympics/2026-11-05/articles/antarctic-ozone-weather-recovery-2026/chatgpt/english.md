# Antarctica’s Ozone Hole Swelled in 2026. What Is Happening to Its Recovery?

High above Antarctica, a thinly spread gas helps protect life from the Sun’s damaging ultraviolet radiation. Ozone in the stratosphere, the atmospheric region above the main weather systems, absorbs some of that radiation before it reaches Earth’s surface. Each southern spring, a combination of extreme cold, returning sunlight and human-made chemicals severely depletes this protective ozone. On November 4, 2026, US scientists reported an unusually large expanse of that damage—even as they continued to project recovery later this century.

The update came from NOAA, the US agency responsible for oceanic and atmospheric science and weather services, and NASA, the US space agency. Their joint monitoring found that the Antarctic ozone hole’s average area from September 7 through October 13 was about 25 million square kilometres, or 9.6 million square miles. For that peak-depletion period, the 2026 seasonal average ranked seventh largest in 48 years of monitoring.

That is a substantial area of weakened protection. It also raises a question with significance far beyond a single Antarctic spring: how can scientists recognize a slow environmental recovery when one year produces such a dramatic result?

The answer begins with what the map measures. It then leads through the winds surrounding Antarctica, the chemistry inside that circulation, and an international agreement whose effects continue to unfold nearly four decades after its adoption. Each part operates on a different timescale. Their combined influence explains how a shrinking supply of destructive chemicals can still produce an exceptionally large seasonal hole.

The word “hole” describes severe ozone depletion. Ozone remains within the mapped region, dispersed among other gases. NASA’s Ozone Watch draws the Antarctic hole’s boundary using a threshold of 220 Dobson units, a measure of the total ozone in a vertical column of air. Locations with column amounts below that threshold contribute to the mapped area.

This definition gives scientists a consistent way to follow the hole’s changing outline. It also establishes the meaning of a large number such as 25 million square kilometres: a broad horizontal region crossed a specified depletion threshold. The amount of ozone remaining can vary from place to place within that region. A map’s outline and the ozone measured at an individual location reveal different aspects of the same event.

For an everyday illustration, picture a large piece of protective fabric with a thinned patch. One measurement records how far the patch spreads across the cloth. Another examines how much material remains at a particular spot. A wider patch and an especially worn spot are separate characteristics. This is an analogy: atmospheric ozone is dispersed gas, and instruments measure its abundance through air. The fabric simply helps separate the extent of damage from its severity at one location.

The distinction matters throughout the 2026 report. Alongside the seasonal average, the agencies recorded a greatest single-day extent of 27.1 million square kilometres, or 10.5 million square miles, on September 18. That figure describes the year’s widest mapped hole. The seventh-place ranking belongs to the average over September 7–October 13. In the agencies’ historical comparison, 2000 remains the year of the largest daily area.

An average and a daily maximum answer different questions. The daily figure captures the widest reach. The average describes the hole’s extent across the designated weeks of peak depletion. Keeping both dates and definitions attached to the numbers allows a reader to understand the season’s scale while preserving the distinction between a brief extreme and conditions sustained over time.

To understand why the depleted region spread so widely, the story moves upward into the Antarctic stratosphere. During the cold season, a large circulation of winds surrounds the continent. Scientists call it the stratospheric polar vortex. It helps isolate frigid polar air, maintaining conditions in which unusual clouds can form high in the atmosphere.

According to NOAA scientist Amy Butler, planetary-scale waves rising from the lower atmosphere were especially weak during August and September 2026. These are vast atmospheric disturbances that ordinarily help disrupt the vortex. Their weakness allowed the circulation to remain stronger and larger, with its strongest winds farther from the South Pole. A wider region consequently remained cold enough to favour the processes that destroy ozone.

Butler’s explanation links the year’s atmospheric behaviour to the unusually large hole. It identifies a physical mechanism; the update leaves the precise share of the area attributable to weather unquantified. The important connection is that winds influence the size and persistence of the cold setting where destructive chemistry takes place.

That chemistry begins with human-made compounds containing chlorine or bromine. Released into the environment, these compounds can travel into the stratosphere. There, ultraviolet radiation breaks them apart, releasing chemical components that can take part in ozone destruction. The Antarctic vortex supplies a favourable setting for those reactions by helping maintain cold, isolated air.

Cloud particles in the exceptionally cold polar stratosphere provide surfaces where reactions convert chlorine-containing chemicals into forms that become highly destructive when sunlight returns. Southern spring therefore brings a dangerous combination: air prepared by the winter’s cold, reactive chemicals, and light that drives ozone loss. Chlorine can participate repeatedly in the destruction process, becoming available again through a cycle of reactions.

The sequence explains why several conditions matter together. Cold affects cloud formation and chemical preparation. The vortex helps preserve those conditions over a large region. Returning sunlight enables the springtime destruction. Human-made compounds supply the chlorine and bromine involved. A change in the weather can strengthen a season’s depletion even while the long-term chemical supply declines.

The altitude of the ozone also matters. In the stratosphere, ozone provides protection by absorbing damaging ultraviolet radiation. Near the ground, ozone is a harmful air pollutant. The recovery discussed in the November 4 update concerns the protective ozone high in the atmosphere. Its location gives the same molecule a very different significance for life below.

A second set of 2026 observations brings the focus from the broad map to the air directly above the South Pole. There, the lowest overhead ozone amount recorded during the year was 105 Dobson units on October 17. The station’s record low was 92 Dobson units in October 2006. The 2026 minimum therefore contained more ozone than the station’s most depleted recorded column.

A Dobson unit makes a dispersed gas easier to count. Imagine collecting all the ozone in a vertical column and compressing it into pure ozone at 0°C and a pressure of one atmosphere. Under those stipulated conditions, one Dobson unit would correspond to a layer 0.01 millimetres thick. The South Pole’s 105-unit reading would become 1.05 millimetres in that artificial comparison.

In the real atmosphere, those molecules remain spread through air across a considerable range of heights. The millimetre conversion is a way to express their combined amount. It helps explain why a gas present in such small quantities can be described as a protective layer while occupying a much larger atmospheric region.

The South Pole comparison provides meaningful evidence about that location. Its geographic reach is the overhead column at the station, and its date is October 17. The largest mapped area occurred almost a month earlier, on September 18. Together, these measurements show a very broad seasonal hole and a South Pole minimum above the station’s historical low. Conditions elsewhere within the hole require observations of those locations.

Scientists gather these different views with complementary instruments. NOAA launches balloon-borne ozone sensors, called ozonesondes, from the South Pole to sample how ozone changes with height. A ground-based optical instrument, the Dobson spectrophotometer, measures the overhead column. Satellite instruments aboard NASA’s Aura, NOAA-20, NOAA-21 and the jointly operated Suomi NPP contribute the broader mapping needed to follow the hole’s horizontal extent.

The instruments give the story its scale: a profile through the atmosphere, a column over a station, and observations across a much wider region. NOAA’s and NASA’s November 4 accounts describe the same joint monitoring effort. Their shared findings belong to one coordinated body of evidence, with each measurement interpreted according to its location, timing and method.

The slower part of the story begins in 1987, when countries adopted the Montreal Protocol. The agreement committed them to replace ozone-depleting substances, including chlorofluorocarbons, usually called CFCs. These compounds had been widely used in refrigerators, air conditioners and insulating foam. Their usefulness in everyday products had created a chemical legacy that extended far beyond those products’ working lives.

Some of that legacy persists in the atmosphere. Additional chemicals can escape from old equipment, products and landfills over many decades. Changing what industry uses affects future releases, while substances already released continue through their atmospheric lifetimes. This delay connects a treaty adopted in the twentieth century to Antarctic conditions measured in 2026.

NOAA scientist Stephen Montzka reports that chlorine attributable to ozone-depleting substances in the Antarctic stratosphere has declined by about one-third from its peak. His discussion also references levels reached in the 1980s. The finding concerns a particular chemical measure in a particular atmospheric region. It provides evidence that the supply feeding destructive ozone chemistry has diminished, even though a substantial legacy remains.

That decline helps explain the agencies’ continuing assessment of recovery. Winds can expand the area favourable to depletion during a particular season. International controls act over decades on the chemicals available within that area. The 2026 observations bring both processes into view: weather supported an exceptionally extensive hole, while the chemical burden had fallen from its peak.

NASA ozone scientist Paul Newman offers an estimate of how much that chemical change may have mattered this year. He estimates that the 2026 hole could have covered roughly 3.5 million square kilometres more if ozone-depleting substances had remained as prevalent as at their year-2000 peak.

This is a counterfactual: an estimate of how the season might have developed under alternative chemical conditions. The observed 2026 hole provides the real-world outcome; the larger figure describes an inferred alternative. The update presents the estimate at an approximate scale, with its detailed method and uncertainty range unspecified. Its value is to make the effect of a reduced chemical supply easier to grasp during a year dominated by a strikingly large map.

Newman’s year-2000 comparison and Montzka’s chlorine assessment also use distinct chemical descriptions and historical references. Preserving those distinctions matters. Together they support the broad explanation that controls have reduced the chemicals responsible for depletion, while each retains its own scope and baseline.

Scientists in the 2026 update continue to project recovery by the 2070s as legacy chemicals break down. They also identify later seasonal formation in recent years as a sign of healing. A later start, a large peak area and a changing chemical burden can all occur within the same evolving system. Each describes a different feature of the recovery.

The 2070s remain a projected horizon, with the intervening seasons subject to atmospheric variability. The unusually large 2026 hole records continuing damage. The decline in Antarctic stratospheric chlorine records progress in reducing one of its essential causes. Understanding the trajectory requires carrying both findings forward.

There are therefore three clocks in this story. Atmospheric disturbances reshape conditions over weeks. Cold, cloud chemistry and returning sunlight govern the annual depletion season. International controls and the gradual breakdown of legacy compounds alter the chemical setting over decades. A single year’s map captures the combined result at one point along those overlapping timelines.

The November 4 update makes recovery a more demanding idea than a steadily shrinking outline. It means assessing how the underlying chemical burden changes, when depletion begins, how widely it spreads and how much ozone remains at measured locations. As the next Antarctic springs arrive, the question is how those observations will accumulate into a clearer account of healing—and how much damage will persist along the way.
