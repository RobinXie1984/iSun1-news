## Verified claim spine

C1 — The 2026 hole’s mean area over September 7–October 13 is about 25 million square kilometres (9.6 million square miles), ranking seventh largest in 48 years of monitoring. The greatest single-day extent is 27.1 million square kilometres (10.5 million square miles) on September 18. The rank applies to the seasonal mean; the daily maximum has its own date and denominator.

C2 — The ozone-hole boundary uses a total-column threshold of 220 Dobson units. The term describes severe depletion with ozone still present. Area describes a horizontal extent, while column amount describes how much ozone lies above a location. Stratospheric ozone absorbs damaging solar ultraviolet; ground-level ozone is a harmful pollutant.

C3 — NOAA scientist Amy Butler attributes the unusually large vortex partly to weak planetary-scale wave activity rising from the lower atmosphere during August and September. The stronger/larger stratospheric circulation sustained broader cold conditions favourable to destructive chemistry. A seasonal weather effect differs from the long-run chemical trend.

C4 — Human-made compounds containing chlorine and bromine reach the stratosphere and are broken down by ultraviolet radiation. Cold polar stratospheric cloud surfaces help activate ozone-destroying chemistry; returning sunlight drives spring depletion. Chlorine participates repeatedly in catalytic destruction. The simplified fabric analogy in the article explains separate measurements; real atmospheric ozone is dispersed gas.

C5 — The South Pole overhead minimum was 105 DU on October 17, compared with that station’s record 92 DU in October 2006. This is distinct from the minimum anywhere in the Southern Hemisphere. One DU corresponds to 0.01 millimetres of pure ozone at 0°C and one atmosphere; 105 DU would be 1.05 millimetres under those artificial compression conditions. The conversion expresses column amount rather than the physical depth or altitude of the real ozone layer.

C6 — Balloons carrying ozonesondes, a ground Dobson spectrophotometer and satellites complement each other’s measurement footprints. The update names NASA Aura, NOAA-20, NOAA-21 and joint Suomi NPP. Their observations support monitoring over time; the two agencies’ news releases represent one joint assessment.

C7 — The 1987 Montreal Protocol committed countries to replacing ozone-depleting substances. NOAA scientist Stephen Montzka describes about a one-third decline in ozone-depleting-substance-derived chlorine in the Antarctic stratosphere from peak levels. The release references earlier chlorine comparisons and a separate year-2000 ozone-depleting-substance counterfactual; their baselines and chemical measures remain distinct. Legacy refrigeration, air-conditioning and insulating-foam chemicals can persist for decades and continue escaping from old products and landfills.

C8 — Paul Newman’s estimate that the 2026 hole could have been roughly 3.5 million square kilometres larger under year-2000 peak ozone-depleting-substance prevalence is a counterfactual estimate. It is separate from observed area and actual measured repair. The single kilometre figure avoids treating the source’s rounded mile range as an exact conversion.

C9 — The agencies project recovery by the 2070s as legacy chemicals break down. This is a forecast, with year-to-year variability continuing. Later seasonal formation is identified as a healing sign, while 2026’s broad depleted area documents continuing damage. Neither a single map nor a single year fully describes long-term recovery.
