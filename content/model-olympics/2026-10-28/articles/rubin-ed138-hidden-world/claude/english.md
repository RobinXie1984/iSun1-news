The World Hiding in Plain Sight

The short version: On October 27, 2026, the Rubin Observatory announced its first dwarf planet candidate, an object designated 2026 ED138, sitting beyond Pluto about 46 astronomical units from the Sun. Rubin caught it 30 times across 20 nights between March and July 2026. Researchers then found it in archival images going back to 2016, which pinned down more than a decade of its path. Its size is still a wide estimate, somewhere between 600 and 1,500 kilometres across, and it has not been confirmed as a dwarf planet. It is also not Planet Nine.

What strikes me most isn’t the object. It’s the method. A modern survey spotted a faint moving point in one of the most crowded parts of the sky, and then, in effect, went back in time through old data to prove the point was real. That combination, new eyes plus old archives, looks like a repeatable way to find things. And repeatable discovery is far more valuable than a single lucky find.

Here’s how it happened, what we actually know, and what we don’t.

A dot, not a photograph

Start by setting the mental image straight, because the internet will get this wrong.

Nobody has a picture of 2026 ED138’s surface. There’s no crater, no icy ridge, no colour portrait. What Rubin recorded is a point of light. A very faint one. Everything we know about this object comes from watching where that point shows up, and how bright it looks, across many images taken over time.

That’s not a weakness of the discovery. That’s what discovery looks like at this distance.

According to Rubin’s announcement, the candidate was identified by Matthew Holman, using search code developed together with Kevin Napier. The object lies past Pluto, currently about 46 astronomical units from the Sun. One astronomical unit is the average distance between the Earth and the Sun, so this thing is roughly 46 times farther out than we are. At that range, sunlight is weak, the object is dim, and it creeps across the sky slowly.

Slow and faint is a hard combination to catch. Now add a crowd.

The needle in the Milky Way

The brief from Rubin highlights a detail I find genuinely fascinating: the object was sitting in a crowded field of the Milky Way. That means the images were packed with stars.

Picture searching for one specific firefly in a stadium where thousands of other lights are glowing. Now imagine that firefly barely moves between snapshots, and that it’s dimmer than most of the lights around it. A single image tells you almost nothing. Any one dot could be the firefly, or a star, or noise.

The trick is time.

Stars stay put relative to one another over these timescales. Something orbiting the Sun doesn’t. So if you take many images of the same patch of sky on different nights, and then ask a computer to look for points that shift in a consistent, physically sensible way, the moving object starts to separate from the background.

That’s what Rubin did. The observatory recorded 2026 ED138 30 times on 20 different nights, from March 4 to July 11, 2026. Algorithms then linked those separate detections together, recognising that the scattered dots on different nights were all the same object, moving along one path.

This is the quiet heroism of modern astronomy. The human insight is in designing the search. The search code is what makes it scale.

The ten-year time machine

Here’s the part that turned a promising detection into something much stronger.

Once you know roughly where an object is and how it moves, you can run the clock backward. You can predict where it should have been in older images, and then go check.

Researchers did exactly that, and found the object in archival images from DECam, the Dark Energy Camera, going back to 2016. Those older images extend the record so that more than ten years of the object’s orbit is now constrained.

Why does that matter so much? Because an orbit measured over a few months is fragile. Objects this far from the Sun take a very long time to complete a lap, so a few months only shows you a tiny sliver of their path. Many different orbits can fit a short arc. Stretch the observations across a decade and most of those alternatives fall away. The path becomes far more certain.

My read on this: this is the most important idea in the whole story. The archival images were sitting there for years. Nobody had flagged this particular faint dot in them, at least not as this object. The data didn’t change. What changed was that a new survey gave researchers a reason, and a prediction, to look in exactly the right place. Old data became newly valuable because new data told it what question to answer.

As an entrepreneur, I’d call that a compounding asset. Every archive of the sky gets more useful each time a new instrument comes online, because the new instrument hands it fresh questions.

A quick word on dates, because they matter

It’s easy to read “announced October 27” and assume something happened on October 27. It didn’t, at least not in the sky.

The timeline, per the brief:

2016 onward: The object appears in archival DECam images, unrecognised at the time as this candidate.
March 4 to July 11, 2026: Rubin records it 30 times over 20 nights.
October 27, 2026: Rubin announces the dwarf planet candidate.

The observations came first. The object, of course, has been out there far longer than any of us. The announcement is just the moment the story became public. That distinction is small, but it’s the kind of thing that gets blurred in headlines, and blurring it makes science look more like a sudden event and less like the patient process it really is.

How big is it? Honestly, we don’t know yet

Now the uncertainty, which deserves more space than it usually gets.

The estimated diameter is 600 to 1,500 kilometres. That’s a huge range. The top of it is two and a half times the bottom. So why so wide?

Because nobody measured the size directly. Astronomers inferred it from three things:

How bright the object looks from here.
How far away it is, which the orbit tells us.
How reflective its surface is assumed to be.

That third item is the soft spot. Brightness depends on both size and reflectivity. A small object with a bright, shiny, icy surface can look just as bright as a larger object with a dark, sooty one. If you don’t know which you’re dealing with, you can’t pin down the size from brightness alone. The reflectivity is estimated, and so the diameter inherits all that uncertainty.

Think of it like judging the size of a car at night by how much light bounces off it. A small white car and a big black car might glow about the same under a streetlight. You need another clue.

For 2026 ED138, that clue should come from infrared follow-up observations, which the brief says are needed to narrow the size and confirm what kind of object this is. The basic logic is that objects also give off faint heat, and measuring that alongside reflected light helps break the tie between “small and bright” and “large and dark.” Until that work is done, the honest answer to “how big is it?” is “somewhere in a wide range.”

What it isn’t

Two clarifications, because they’re where hype tends to creep in.

It is not a confirmed new dwarf planet. Rubin calls it a dwarf planet candidate. The brief is explicit that it is not a confirmed sixth recognised dwarf planet. Whether it qualifies depends partly on its size and nature, which is precisely what the follow-up observations are meant to establish. “Candidate” is doing real work in that sentence.

It is not Planet Nine. Planet Nine is the name for a proposed large, undiscovered planet far out in the solar system. 2026 ED138 is not that. It’s a distant, possibly sizeable icy body beyond Pluto, which is interesting on its own terms. It doesn’t need to be something more dramatic to be worth our attention.

I’d go further. Resisting the temptation to inflate this find is part of what makes it trustworthy. A candidate with a clearly stated size range and a clear list of next steps is more credible than a breathless claim with nothing behind it.

Why this is good news, even with all the caveats

So we have a faint dot, an uncertain size, and an unconfirmed classification. Why get excited?

Because of what the find demonstrates. The brief frames the positive story around two things: Rubin showed it can make this kind of discovery, and reusing older observations opened a useful new avenue for research. Let me unpack why I think both points carry weight. (To be clear, what follows is my interpretation, not a claim from the announcement.)

First, it’s a proof of capability. A survey that can pull a faint, slow-moving object out of a dense star field and link 30 detections across 20 nights has shown its search pipeline works under hard conditions. Crowded regions of the Milky Way are exactly where faint movers are easiest to miss. If they can be found there, the method has passed a tough test.

Second, it turns archives into hunting grounds. The DECam recovery shows that old images aren’t just history. Given a good prediction from new data, they can extend an orbit by years almost instantly. That’s a powerful lever. You don’t have to wait a decade to measure a decade of motion if someone already took the pictures.

Third, it’s a pattern, not a one-off. If the pipeline worked once, it can presumably be pointed at more of the sky. I can’t tell you how many more objects like this are waiting, and the brief makes no such claim, so I won’t invent a number. But the logic suggests this is the start of a workflow rather than the end of a lucky search.

From a builder’s perspective, that’s the real headline. The interesting thing about a new tool is rarely its first output. It’s whether the first output proves the tool can produce many more.

The explorer’s lesson

I keep coming back to one image: a faint point of light, present in photographs since at least 2016, waiting for someone to ask the right question of it.

There’s something humbling in that. We tend to imagine discovery as seeing something nobody could see before. Often it’s the opposite. It’s realising that what we already had contained an answer we hadn’t recognised.

That idea travels well beyond astronomy. Companies sit on years of records they’ve never revisited. Researchers have old datasets that new methods could reread. The lesson of 2026 ED138 is that the combination of fresh observation and patient reuse of old material can reveal things neither would find alone.

What to watch next

If you want to follow this story, here’s what will actually move it forward:

Infrared follow-up. This is the key next step named in the brief. It should help narrow the 600–1,500 km range and clarify what the object is.
Classification. Only once the size and nature are better understood can anyone say whether “candidate” can be dropped.
More finds from the same approach. The strongest sign that this method matters would be additional discoveries made the same way: new survey data, linked by algorithms, strengthened by archives.

Until then, 2026 ED138 is best understood as exactly what Rubin says it is: a promising, distant, possibly large world beyond Pluto, tracked carefully, sized loosely, and not yet confirmed.

The bottom line

Rubin’s first dwarf planet candidate is a faint dot about 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth, caught 30 times in 2026 and traced back through images to 2016. It could be 600 kilometres across or 1,500. It might turn out to be a dwarf planet, or it might not. It is not Planet Nine.

But the method that found it, linking scattered detections in a crowded sky and then mining old images to lock in the orbit, is the real discovery here. One faint dot proved the approach works. The sky is large, and the archives are deep. I suspect this won’t be the last thing hiding in pictures we already have.
