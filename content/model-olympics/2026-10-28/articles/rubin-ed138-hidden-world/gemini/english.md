The Perimeter at Forty-Six Astronomical Units

To understand where candidate 2026 ED138 drifts, one must first reset their sense of distance. An astronomical unit (AU) represents the mean distance between the Earth and the Sun—approximately 150 million kilometers. Neptune, the outermost recognized major planet, carves its orbital track at an average distance of 30 AU. Pluto travels an eccentric highway ranging from roughly 30 AU at perihelion to 49 AU at aphelion, averaging nearly 39.5 AU.

At approximately 46 AU, 2026 ED138 is situated well beyond Pluto’s mean position, lingering in the trans-Neptunian realm where the Sun’s gravitational dominance weakens into a slow, multi-century ballet. Sunlight at 46 AU is depleted by the inverse-square law; by the time photons travel roughly 6.9 billion kilometers out to the candidate and reflect along another 6.9-billion-kilometer journey back to Earth's telescopes, the returning optical signal is exceedingly faint.

Negligible Parallax and Motion: Unlike near-Earth asteroids, which streak visibly across exposures over the course of minutes, an object at 46 AU creeps across the celestial sphere at an imperceptible crawl, shifting mere arcseconds over multiple weeks.

The Needle in the Galactic Swarm: Algorithmic Cartography

Discovering an outer Solar System body typically requires targeting sparse, quiet patches of sky where background stellar clutter is minimal. In empty galactic latitudes, detecting a moving body is conceptually straightforward: take an image on Monday, take another on Tuesday, subtract the static stars, and examine the remaining moving dot.

Candidate 2026 ED138, however, was detected directly within a crowded Milky Way field.

In dense galactic regions, digital subtraction algorithms frequently struggle. The density of background stars creates severe blending, diffraction artifacts, and millions of stationary light sources that swamp faint moving signals. A single faint point wandering across thousands of background stars produces a catastrophic signal-to-noise challenge. The moving point is constantly occluded, merged, or lost behind background stellar flux.

Resolving this signal required the search code developed by Matthew Holman and Kevin Napier. Rather than relying on simple visual inspection or traditional subtraction pipelines that fail in crowded starscapes, their linking algorithms evaluate vast combinatorial networks of transient, faint point detections. The software models plausible orbital trajectories through time, asking a core mathematical question: Which subset of otherwise disconnected, ambiguous optical detections aligns with a single, gravitationally bound Keplerian trajectory around the Sun?

Between March 4 and July 11, 2026, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory recorded the object 30 times across 20 distinct nights. This observational cadence was critical. A few detections across two or three nights yield an underdetermined orbit; without a sufficiently long observation arc, an apparent slow-moving object can be mathematically mimicked by closer, slower asteroids or noise artifacts. The 20 distinct nights provided by Rubin built a firm foundation, confirming that this faint point of light was not a sensor defect or an erratic artifact, but a real, coherent physical body following a predictable trajectory through the deep Solar System.

The Ghost Arc: Mining Ten Years of Archival Photons

While the formal announcement of 2026 ED138 took place on October 27, 2026, the discovery was neither born nor finalized on that date. Nor did the physical observations begin solely with Rubin’s 2026 observing run.

Once Holman and Napier’s pipeline established the preliminary trajectory from the four-month Rubin dataset, researchers turned toward a powerful, underutilized operational lever: precovery. Precovery involves calculating an object's past positions and searching for its signature in historical, archival datasets where it went uncataloged at the time of exposure.

The researchers traced the orbit back into archival exposures captured by the Dark Energy Camera (DECam)—an instrument mounted on the Víctor M. Blanco 4-meter Telescope at Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory in Chile. The archival DECam data extended all the way back to 2016.

The Reflectivity Dilemma: Sizing a World You Cannot See

One of the most vital distinctions to make regarding 2026 ED138 is that scientists have not directly measured its physical size. No instrument has seen its edges, cast a shadow across its limb, or measured its angular diameter.

Instead, the candidate's size is estimated indirectly, spanning a broad potential range of 600 to 1,500 kilometers in diameter. This range is calculated from three primary variables:

Its observed optical brightness (apparent magnitude).

Its heliocentric distance (approximately 46 AU).

An assumed surface reflectivity, known as albedo.

This dynamic introduces what astronomers call the albedo degeneracy. Optical telescopes record only the total flux of reflected sunlight. A small object covered in pristine, highly reflective volatile ices (high albedo) reflects the exact same amount of optical light as a massive, charcoal-dark object covered in complex, space-weathered organic compounds or tholins (low albedo).

While optical instruments measure reflected sunlight, space-based infrared telescopes (such as the James Webb Space Telescope) detect thermal emission—the heat absorbed by the object and re-radiated into space. Because a dark surface absorbs more sunlight and radiates more heat, while a bright surface reflects sunlight and stays colder, combining simultaneous optical and infrared measurements untangles the albedo degeneracy. Until those radiometric infrared measurements are conducted, the true physical dimensions of 2026 ED138 remain strictly bounded between 600 and 1,500 kilometers.

Categorical Boundaries: Neither Planet Nine nor Crowned Dwarf

In reporting frontier science, precision requires delineating what an object is not. The discovery of a major trans-Neptunian body routinely triggers sensational speculation, but two critical boundaries must be maintained:

1. It is not "Planet Nine"

"Planet Nine" is a specific, theoretical hypothesis: a proposed major planet with an estimated mass roughly 5 to 10 times that of Earth, orbiting hundreds of astronomical units away on a highly eccentric, inclined orbit. It is hypothesized to explain the apparent orbital clustering of extreme trans-Neptunian objects.

Candidate 2026 ED138 shares none of these characteristics. Even at its absolute maximum estimated diameter of 1,500 kilometers, its mass would remain a minuscule fraction of Earth’s mass—far closer to the scale of Pluto (2,376 km) or Charon (1,212 km). It does not possess the gravitational heft required to act as the hypothesized perturber of the outer Solar System.

2. It is not yet a confirmed sixth recognized dwarf planet

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) currently recognizes five official dwarf planets: Ceres in the asteroid belt, and Pluto, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake in the trans-Neptunian region. (Several other large outer bodies, such as Sedna, Quaoar, and Gonggong, are widely considered dwarf planets by researchers but have not received formal classification).

To be classified as a dwarf planet under IAU definitions, a celestial body must meet specific physical criteria:

It must orbit the Sun (which 2026 ED138 does).

It must not be a satellite of another planet.

It must not have cleared the neighborhood around its orbit (distinguishing it from the eight major planets).

Crucially: It must have sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces, assuming a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape.

Whether 2026 ED138 satisfies that final requirement remains unknown. If follow-up measurements determine that its diameter sits near the lower bound of 600 kilometers, its composition becomes the deciding factor. An icy body at 600 kilometers may achieve hydrostatic equilibrium, whereas a rocky body might retain an irregular, fractured shape. If its diameter lies near the upper bound of 1,500 kilometers, plastic relaxation under self-gravity is almost certain, making dwarf planet status highly probable. Until infrared observations narrow its diameter and provide clues to its density and composition, 2026 ED138 remains strictly a candidate dwarf planet.
