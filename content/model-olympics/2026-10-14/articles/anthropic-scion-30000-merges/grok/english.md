Emotional Resonance: Seven Engineers, One Overloaded Monorepo, and the Quiet Rebuild That Kept the AI Working

Counter-intuitive: Anthropic Replaced Git With Something Faster—And Then Told Everyone Not to Copy It

In May 2026 Anthropic was merging roughly 2,500 code changes a day. By September the same internal source-control team reported the figure had climbed past 30,000. Those are the company’s own numbers, published in an engineering blog post on 13 October 2026; they are not independent benchmarks. The growth was large enough, the team said, that the infrastructure itself became the bottleneck.

The volume came, in significant part, from Claude. The same model family that Anthropic sells was also being used inside the company to generate code at a rate the existing Git-based monorepo setup was not built to absorb. A seven-person team responded by designing and building an in-house system called Scion, together with a companion review tool named Antique and related supporting pieces. Over five months they completed a full migration of the monorepo off Git. Claude helped create the original surge of changes and also assisted in writing parts of the replacement. Human engineers still set the architecture and reviewed the intent of the changes that landed.

Scion’s design choices are described as online clients, server-side operations, and sparse, on-demand data rather than full local mirrors of the repository. Anthropic reports that the new arrangement produced a 200× smaller CPU and memory footprint for the infrastructure that had previously mirrored the monorepo, and a separate 25 % improvement in available CI CPU capacity. These are distinct measurements. They do not mean the system is 200× cheaper overall, nor that every operation is 200× faster for every user.

Anthropic is explicit about the limits. Scion is not presented as a general-purpose Git replacement. The company’s workload and its internal token spending are described as unusually large; the same design is not claimed to be suitable for typical organizations. No public drop-in release is established in the post. Higher merge volume, the post notes, is not evidence of higher code quality, of profitability, or of a reduction in the number of engineers required.

The episode is therefore narrow and carefully caveated. It is also a clean illustration of a shift that is becoming harder to ignore: when AI accelerates the production of code, the systems that accept, review, store, and integrate that code can become the new constraint. The writing of changes is no longer the slowest step. The machinery that has to remain correct while absorbing those changes is.

Git was never designed for a continuous flood of machine-generated patches arriving at the rate Anthropic observed. Its distributed model, its expectation of reasonably complete local clones, and the cost of keeping mirrors and CI systems in sync all worked well for human-scale contribution rates. Once the daily merge count moved from low thousands into the tens of thousands, those assumptions produced measurable pressure on CPU, memory, and build capacity. The source-control team’s response was not to ask developers to slow down. It was to replace the storage and access layer underneath them.

The five-month migration by a small team is notable for its speed relative to the size of the change. A monorepo migration is ordinarily a multi-year, high-risk project. Here the team could lean on Claude both as a source of the problem and as a drafting assistant for the solution. The post is careful to keep human judgment in the decisive seats: architecture decisions and review of change intent stayed with engineers. That division of labor is stated rather than dramatized; it is simply the boundary the company drew.

What Scion appears to trade is the classic distributed-Git assumption of “every clone is complete and autonomous” for a more centralized, demand-driven model. Clients stay online, operations happen on the server, and data is fetched only as needed. The reported infrastructure savings follow from that choice: far less redundant copying of history and trees across many machines. The 25 % CI capacity gain is a separate operational outcome, not a direct consequence of the 200× footprint claim. Both figures are Anthropic’s; neither has been independently reproduced in the public record as of the post’s date.

The disclaimers matter because the numbers are easy to over-read. A 200× reduction in one infrastructure footprint does not automatically translate into a 200× reduction in total cost of ownership, nor into faster local workflows for every developer. A system optimized for Anthropic’s particular mix of internal token use, review volume, and monorepo scale may be inefficient or brittle under different conditions. The post itself warns against treating Scion as a drop-in successor that other companies should simply adopt. Higher throughput of merges also does not, by itself, demonstrate that the resulting codebase is better, that the business is more profitable, or that fewer people are needed. Those outcomes depend on review quality, product decisions, and economics that sit outside the source-control layer.

The larger pattern is the relocation of friction. Early AI coding tools were often evaluated by how quickly they could emit a function or a pull request. Once that emission rate rises by an order of magnitude inside a single organization, the limiting factors become the ones that were previously background: storage growth, index and mirror costs, CI queue depth, review bandwidth, and the human capacity to assess intent at scale. Scion and Antique are attempts to push some of those costs down by redesigning the substrate. They do not remove the need for judgment; they change where the judgment has to be applied and how much machine assistance can sit beside it.

Whether the same pressure will appear elsewhere depends on variables the post does not quantify for other organizations: how heavily AI is used for code generation, how large and how monolithic the repository is, how expensive mirroring and CI have become, and how much review capacity already exists. Some companies may continue to absorb the growth inside conventional Git setups for a long time. Others may hit comparable infrastructure ceilings and decide that a custom system, or a different commercial product, is cheaper than continued scaling of the old mirrors. The Anthropic case shows that the ceiling can be reached and that a focused internal team can move the monorepo past it in months rather than years. It does not show that every team should attempt the same replacement, or that the replacement will look the same.

Uncertainty remains on several points the post leaves open. The performance claims have not been independently verified outside Anthropic. The full economic picture—engineering time spent on the migration, ongoing operational cost of Scion versus the prior Git infrastructure, effects on developer velocity beyond the cited CI metric—is not detailed. Applicability to repositories that are smaller, more distributed, or less saturated with AI-generated changes is explicitly disclaimed. No statement is made about reductions in engineering headcount, about outages that forced the change, or about any broader obsolescence of Git. Those absences are part of the record.

What the record does contain is a concrete sequence: a rapid rise in daily merges, an infrastructure bottleneck identified by the team responsible for source control, a five-month internal rebuild that used the same AI systems contributing to the load, measurable reductions in certain infrastructure footprints, and a clear refusal to present the result as a universal solution. The sequence is narrow. The implication it supports is broader and still provisional: AI-driven increases in code production can shift the binding constraint from generation to acceptance and integration. How widely that shift appears, and how organizations choose to respond when it does, will be settled by later measurements, not by any single engineering post.
