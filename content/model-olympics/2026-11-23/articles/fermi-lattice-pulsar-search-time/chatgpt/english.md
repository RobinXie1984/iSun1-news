# Fifteen Years of Sky Data Held a Pulsar. What Finally Revealed Its Beat?

Astronomers have identified a rapidly spinning stellar remnant in roughly 15 years of observations from NASA’s Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope. The object, called PSR J1622−7204, is a pulsar: the compact remains of an exploded star, whose rotation brings pulses of radiation into view. It turns about 261 times each second. The discovery, reported on 19 November 2026, grew from a broader scientific opportunity: observatories accumulate measurements that later generations of researchers can examine with new methods. In this case, a different way of searching made a long-recorded signal recognizable.

Fermi observes gamma rays, a highly energetic form of light. Its Large Area Telescope, usually shortened to LAT, records individual particles of that light, called photons. Those records can reveal powerful activity across the sky, although identifying the object responsible sometimes takes years of analysis. A source may appear in a catalogue because gamma rays arrive from its direction, while its physical identity remains an open question.

That was the starting position here. The team examined an already catalogued source, 4FGL J1622.2−7202, whose properties made it a promising pulsar candidate. Researchers had a place to look and a reason to look there. Their challenge was to recover a regular beat from the recorded photon arrivals and establish which kind of object produced them.

The six authors—Pook-Kolb, Gazith, Clark, Agrell, Zackay and Allen—reported their discovery in *Physical Review Letters*. Their result identifies an isolated gamma-ray pulsar, meaning one whose timing can be studied as a star outside a binary system. Its measured spin frequency is about 260.896 hertz, where one hertz means one cycle per second. Each rotation takes approximately 3.833 milliseconds, or just under four thousandths of a second.

A star turning that quickly sounds like an excellent clock. The difficulty lies in how little of its ticking reaches the detector.

A pulsar is a neutron star, an extremely compact remnant left after a massive star explodes. As it rotates, radiation can sweep across Earth’s direction, creating a repeating pattern. Yet LAT captures only a tiny, irregular fraction of the emitted photons. The observation record also contains unrelated background photons. The result is a sparse mixture whose underlying rhythm requires reconstruction.

For an everyday analogy, imagine trying to work out a lighthouse’s turning rate from a few timestamped glimpses, mixed with flashes from other lights. Nearly all the lighthouse’s turns pass unseen. You would have to decide which glimpses belonged together and how many complete turns separated them. This is an illustration of the timing problem; the actual observations concern gamma-ray photons from a distant stellar remnant.

The long archive makes that problem both richer and harder. More years provide more opportunities to collect useful photons. They also allow small errors in an assumed rotation rate to accumulate. A slightly mistaken rate eventually puts the predicted pulse at the wrong part of the star’s turn. A gradual change in spin adds another complication.

The detector moves, too. Fermi travels around Earth, and Earth travels around the Sun. Those motions change the distance that light travels before reaching the spacecraft. Correcting the arrival times therefore depends on the source’s position in the sky. Uncertainty about that position becomes uncertainty about the clock.

To recover the signal, a search has to find a consistent account of all these effects. It must connect widely separated arrivals to the same rotating object while allowing for background contamination. The many plausible combinations of spin, spin change and position create an enormous computational task.

The new method approached that task through the number of whole rotations between photons.

A whole rotation comes in integer steps: one turn, two turns, three turns, and so on. Across a long gap, the number can be enormous, but it still has to be a whole number. The researchers used that structure to turn the timing problem into a mathematical arrangement called a lattice.

Here, a lattice is an arrangement generated by integer steps in a mathematical space. Its many dimensions hold relationships among possible rotation counts and a timing model. Certain relatively short vectors—mathematical displacements through that space—can point toward combinations that fit together well. In plain terms, the geometry helps the computer find promising ways to connect the scattered observations into a common rotational history.

The team used a lattice-sieving algorithm called G6K to search for many such short vectors. Their formulation also handles five continuously varying quantities analytically, reducing the numerical search burden. These are computational changes applied to existing measurements; the telescope hardware and the recorded photons remain the same.

Lattice methods have a history in cryptography, where researchers study difficult mathematical problems involved in protecting information. This astronomy work borrows classical computational ideas from that field and runs them on ordinary processors. Its reported achievement is the recovery of a celestial timing signal.

The geometry supplies candidates, each of which still needs scrutiny. The researchers rejected physically implausible candidates and checked the survivors for statistically significant pulsation. The successful solution appeared among the candidates the algorithm produced; it occupied a place beyond the shortest physically plausible vector. That detail matters because it shows the role of the later checks. A mathematically attractive answer earns its astronomical meaning through agreement with the observations.

The practical search began with a deliberately small selection of photons. The researchers assigned weights reflecting how likely each photon was to belong to the target source, then took the 120 highest-weighted records. Each trial used a randomly selected subset of 80.

Those weights express probabilities. Even a highly ranked photon can come from the background, and troublesome background events can spoil a trial. Trying different subsets gives the method further opportunities to find a combination that exposes the underlying rhythm.

In 1,000 test trials, the researchers estimated that a single 80-photon subset had about a 1% chance of success for this pulsar. One successful subset happened to occur within the first 20 trials. That early success was fortunate: using the estimated per-trial probability, roughly 300 trials would offer about a 95% chance of at least one success.

The repeated attempts help explain the timing figures associated with the discovery. On an otherwise idle single core of an AMD EPYC 7351P processor, the paper reports about 90 seconds for one trial. With the processor under full load, a trial took about five minutes. The authors give about 10 CPU hours for the case overall.

Thus, the five-minute figure describes one attempt involving one small subset. A practical search includes repeated attempts, candidate assessment and further analysis. Its success depends on the particular source and the quality of the selected photon combinations.

Once a promising timing solution emerged, the team expanded the evidence. They performed separate pulsation checks, including a statistical measure called the H-statistic, and fitted the timing model against a larger collection of observations. Such a check asks whether the photons, arranged according to the proposed rotational clock, show a convincing recurring pattern.

This later stage used 15,905 weighted photon records. Their weights added up to an effective contribution of about 828 photons attributable to the pulsar. The two numbers describe different aspects of the data: nearly 16,000 records entered the analysis, while their combined probabilities indicated a much smaller effective contribution from the star.

That distinction connects the exploratory search to the discovery claim. Eighty selected records could generate a candidate. The larger data set and additional statistical checks supplied the evidence used to establish its periodicity and refine its timing.

The research paper is the primary account of those results. NASA’s accompanying report provides mission and public-data context, while the Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics, also known as the Albert Einstein Institute, presents the research institution’s account. Together, these sources describe one research result; independent reproduction remains outside the evidence presented here.

The scale of the computational change becomes clearer beside an earlier attempt on the same pulsar. According to the paper, a previous state-of-the-art search failed to detect it despite consuming about 600 CPU years of computation. The new approach succeeded using about 10 CPU hours.

A CPU year measures aggregate processor work: the amount one processor would perform while running for a year. Many processors can share that work, shortening the elapsed calendar time. The earlier figure therefore describes the accumulated computation across a search, rather than centuries spent waiting for a machine.

Dividing the two reported workloads gives a ratio of roughly 526,000 to one. The paper describes a reduction of five to six orders of magnitude, meaning a factor in the broad range from one hundred thousand to one million. The Albert Einstein Institute’s announcement rounds the message to a million-fold improvement.

The comparison is dramatic, and its scope is specific. The older procedure failed to detect this object; the newer one found it. The workloads consequently describe approaches with different detection outcomes on a difficult candidate. They establish a powerful result for this case, while performance across other pulsars remains a question for further searches.

The deeper consequence concerns access to the signal itself. A source that had defeated a substantial previous computation became identifiable with a different mathematical formulation. The advance changed what researchers could recover from the archive.

That archive exists because Fermi has been observing for a long time and because its measurements are available for reuse. NASA says the mission launched in 2008 and surveys roughly the entire sky every three hours. Following an initial one-year calibration period, its science data became public, with later observations released as they were processed. NASA also provides Fermitools to help researchers prepare and work with the data.

Repeated surveys and public access serve different purposes. Repeated observations accumulate the photons needed to reveal faint or difficult patterns. Public access allows researchers beyond the original observing effort to bring their own methods to those records. Preparation tools make the measurements more usable, while published analysis methods create a route for checking a claimed signal.

PSR J1622−7204 illustrates how those pieces can work together. The photons were already recorded. The source was already promising. A new search method connected the arrival times to a coherent stellar clock. The discovery date marks that identification, while the observations belong to the preceding years.

Further progress still faces substantial computational limits. The paper reports that lattice-sieving cost grows steeply, approximately exponentially, as more photons enter the candidate problem. Increasing the input can therefore make the search rapidly more expensive. Complex pulse shapes and background contamination also remain challenges.

This explains the value of the small random subsets. They keep the candidate problem manageable, at the price of many unsuccessful trials. The larger collection of observations becomes useful once a promising timing model gives researchers a much more focused question to test.

Binary pulsars offer a possible next step. These stars orbit companions, and their orbital motion changes the distance their photons travel to Earth. Recovering their spin from photon arrivals means accounting for that additional timing pattern. A blind search, in which the orbital properties must also be discovered, has many more possibilities to explore.

The authors present such searches as future work. Their rough estimate involves about a million additional sets of binary parameters, potentially multiplying the isolated-search workload by a similar factor. They propose using Einstein@Home, a project that draws computing power from volunteers. Earlier gamma-ray searches through that project have already found more than 80 pulsars.

The proposed extension shows both the promise and the remaining difficulty. A successful isolated-pulsar search provides a foundation; orbital motion creates a much larger problem. The number of further discoveries depends on what future searches find and confirm.

For now, one fast-spinning star supplies a concrete answer about the value of revisiting old measurements. Roughly 15 years of Fermi observations contained enough information to identify its beat, and a new way of arranging the search finally made that information usable. How many other signals already recorded in public archives remain beyond the reach of the methods used to search them?
