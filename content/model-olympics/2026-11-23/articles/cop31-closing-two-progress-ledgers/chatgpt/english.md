# COP31’s Political Text Stalled. What Will Its Promised Progress Deliver?

At COP31, the annual United Nations climate conference in Antalya, Türkiye, governments faced a familiar task: agreeing how countries with different resources and energy needs should respond to a warming planet. Utilities, cities, businesses and lenders also came to present their own work. By the closing plenary on 22 November 2026, those activities had produced sharply different accounts of progress. UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell reported disagreement on issues concerning both presidency-led decisions, alongside negotiating advances and activity beyond the formal talks.

The distinction matters because climate cooperation connects decisions made at several levels. Governments establish common expectations and national policy. Electricity companies build the equipment that carries power. Banks finance projects, and cities make choices about transport and infrastructure. Each can influence what the others accomplish. A conference brings these participants together, while their decisions and construction schedules extend across years.

The standing international framework is the Paris Agreement, adopted on 12 December 2015 and in force since 4 November 2016. This legally binding treaty aims to hold the rise in the long-term global average temperature well below 2°C above preindustrial conditions, while pursuing efforts toward 1.5°C. These figures describe a change in the planet’s climate over time; daily temperatures in individual places vary much more widely.

Under Paris, countries submit national climate plans, called nationally determined contributions, or NDCs, on a five-year cycle. Reporting and review help track action, and a periodic global stocktake assesses collective progress. Finance, technology and support for countries’ practical capabilities form part of the framework. The treaty’s legal force and the legal treatment of individual national targets are distinct questions. That continuing structure provides the background against which each annual conference should be judged.

COP31 was scheduled for 9–20 November and reached its closing plenary on 22 November. The gathering serves several related legal bodies. The COP brings together parties to the UN climate convention; the CMA brings together parties to the Paris Agreement. A proposal’s formal home matters because decisions belong to the relevant body and its participating parties.

Stiell referred to 194 Parties. That term includes the European Union as well as countries. The UN Climate Change Secretariat facilitates the process, and its executive secretary offers a public assessment of proceedings. The authority to agree decisions rests with the parties.

At the close, Stiell expressed disappointment over the presidency-led disagreements and called for greater transparency and reform of the process. He also reported key negotiating decisions, including on just transition. His account therefore described a particular political setback within a conference that had several strands of work.

The *Antalya Pact* document dated 22 November illustrates why the status of a text deserves attention. Its header identifies an advance version, a draft decision and a proposal by the President. Publication made the proposed text available for scrutiny; agreement and final adoption are separate procedural steps. The official just-transition record, FCCC/PA/CMA/2026/L.24, dated 22 November at 10:45 GMT+3, likewise carries draft and presidential-proposal labels.

This account draws on Stiell’s assessment, those document records and the Secretariat’s electrification announcement. Project figures remain participants’ reports; the inspected material establishes draft status for the cited texts, with final adoption and independently verified project effects requiring further records.

That leaves a practical question beneath the closing disagreement: what kind of progress had each participant actually described?

Climate action has several purposes. Mitigation means reducing the emissions that warm the planet. Adaptation means preparing for climate effects, such as making infrastructure better able to cope with damaging weather. Just transition concerns people and communities affected as energy systems and economies change. Workers’ livelihoods, access to services and the distribution of costs belong within that discussion.

These purposes can overlap, yet each calls for its own evidence. A stronger electricity network may help deliver cleaner power. Support for an affected community addresses the consequences of economic change. A negotiated decision can establish shared direction for such work, while its implementation determines what people experience.

An everyday analogy helps explain the relationship. Imagine residents of a shared apartment building preparing to use less energy. Several households can improve appliances or renovate their own rooms. Those improvements may be useful while the residents continue discussing upgrades to shared wiring and the division of costs. Work inside individual apartments and an agreement covering the building serve connected purposes. The analogy illustrates the coordination problem; international negotiations involve a far larger range of interests, institutions and responsibilities.

At COP31, the formal negotiations ran alongside the voluntary Action Agenda, through which governments and other participants presented activity. Stiell credited progress across six areas, including food and agriculture and the resilience of cities and infrastructure. That broader activity helps explain how an unresolved political text and reports of practical advances could emerge from the same conference.

Three days before the closing plenary, the UN Climate Change Secretariat had announced an Electrification Delivery Package. Electrification means replacing some uses of fuels with electricity. Familiar examples include an electric vehicle taking over journeys previously made in a petrol vehicle, or electrically powered equipment replacing a fuel-burning machine. The climate effect depends in part on how the electricity is generated and how the equipment is used.

The 19 November package brought together announcements and updates involving more than 150 countries plus the EU, 37 cities and more than 260 businesses across six continents. Participants could act individually or through coalitions. The cities’ contributions combined reports of progress during 2026 with announcements of action planned for 2027.

That breadth is meaningful as a sign of participation. It brings together institutions capable of changing demand for electricity, financing equipment and building supply. The count itself measures involvement. Assessing delivery requires following the particular projects and policies behind each participant’s contribution.

The announcement described the country-plus-EU participants as representing more than two-thirds of global gross domestic product, or GDP—the value of economic output. Separately, Stiell characterized pledges and a Leaders’ Summit as showing roughly half the global economy accelerating electrification. The two figures cover different collections of activity. Each conveys economic reach; a calculation of emissions reductions would require evidence about changes in energy production and use.

The utility figures offered a more physical measure. Members of the Utilities for Net Zero Alliance reported building more than 21,000 kilometres of new electricity grid in 2025, about 50% more than in 2024. The reporting group and the dates are essential. This was alliance members’ account of construction during the preceding year, presented in a November 2026 announcement.

An electricity grid is the connected system of lines and equipment that moves power from where it is produced to where people use it. Its role becomes especially important when new generators, storage facilities and electricity-consuming equipment are added in different places. A generating project needs a connection through which its power can reach customers. Growing demand can also require changes to the network serving it.

Reported construction therefore tells readers something substantial: alliance members said physical expansion had occurred, and they supplied a comparison with their previous year’s work. The figure is more directly connected to completed activity than a future investment target. Its climate meaning still depends on what those lines carry and whom they serve.

Length captures only one property of a network. Lines with similar distances can carry different amounts of power and serve different purposes. Some may connect generation; others may strengthen service in places where customers already use electricity. Capacity, reliability, access and the source of the electricity help explain the value of the construction. Those measures would turn a distance into a clearer account of changed energy services.

The same alliance reported combined annual investment plans of at least US$156 billion across grids, renewable energy and storage. Those categories describe complementary parts of an electricity system. Renewable generation supplies power from sources such as sunlight and wind. Storage can hold energy for later use. Grids connect supply and demand across locations.

The scale of the plans signals substantial intended spending, but the tense carries much of the meaning. The 21,000-kilometre figure describes reported past construction. The US$156 billion figure describes annual plans spread across three uses. Expenditure accounts and completed assets would show how those plans developed into delivery.

There is also a timing distinction between work reported at a conference and work produced by it. The alliance’s 2025 construction preceded COP31. The conference provided an occasion to bring that record together and present future intentions. Establishing how much additional activity the gathering encouraged would require tracing decisions and commitments over time.

A banking example makes the difference between a target and a delivery record particularly clear. The package reported that AIB had doubled its 2030 green and transition lending target from €30 billion to €60 billion, building on €28 billion reported as deployed since 2019.

The larger target describes the bank’s ambition toward a future date. The €28 billion describes reported lending over a period that had already begun years earlier. Keeping those figures separate preserves the story: an institution with a reported lending history raised the scale of its intended activity.

Following lending through to its effects takes another step. Money can finance projects at different stages, and the resulting assets can serve different purposes. A financing total establishes the scale of reported financial activity. Project records would explain what was built, when it began operating and how it changed energy use or emissions. This is why the announcements resist compression into a single total for climate progress delivered in Antalya.

The political negotiations address another part of the same chain. Investors and project developers make decisions within rules about energy, infrastructure and public support. Governments also negotiate responsibilities across economies with very different resources. Shared decisions can help coordinate expectations about action and support, while unresolved terms leave those collective questions open.

Fairness enters through those practical arrangements. A project’s benefits may depend on which communities gain reliable electricity, which customers bear costs and which workers receive support as industries change. A large financial plan and a long stretch of new grid each provide useful information. Distribution requires its own account of who received what.

This gives just transition a distinct place in the story. Stiell’s closing assessment identified it among negotiating advances. The cited draft record establishes the existence and procedural label of a presidential proposal. Evidence about specific assistance, funding arrangements and outcomes for communities would come from final decisions and implementation records. The people affected by energy change are the eventual test of what that work achieves.

Taken together, the sources show movement at different stages: reported construction, expanded financial ambition, broad participation and an unresolved presidency-led political process. They support a detailed picture of activity. A global warming forecast would require a much wider assessment, including actual emissions, the implementation of national plans and the future development of energy systems.

The next evidence will consequently arrive through several kinds of records. Final adopted texts can clarify which collective decisions emerged from COP31. Spending and lending disclosures can show how announced plans developed. Operating projects can reveal changes in electricity supply, access, resilience and emissions, while community-level evidence can show how benefits and costs were shared.

The closing speeches and the construction reports belong in the same account because climate progress depends on both collective arrangements and physical change. Their different dates, purposes and stages tell readers where progress can already be described and where its consequences remain open. After Antalya, the question is whether those strands will come together: will the decisions and investments produce measurable improvements in energy and emissions, with support reaching the people whose lives are changing?
