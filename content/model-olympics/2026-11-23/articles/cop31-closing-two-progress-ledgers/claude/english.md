# COP31 Closed in Antalya With Its Presidency Texts in Dispute. What Do the New Power-Line Figures Really Prove?

On 22 November 2026, the United Nations climate conference known as COP31 closed in Antalya, Türkiye, with a reported setback over its Presidency-led decisions. Simon Stiell leads UN Climate Change, the secretariat that organises and facilitates these talks and supports the governments taking part. He told the closing session that participants had failed to agree on issues connected to both of the decisions led by the conference Presidency. The Presidency is the conference's chairing office: it presides over the meeting and steers its main texts. Stiell said he was disappointed, called for reform of the process and greater transparency, and restated his support for countries working together. He also reported other negotiating decisions, so the setback covered the two Presidency-led decisions within a wider agenda. Three days earlier, on 19 November, the same secretariat had published a large collection of electrification announcements. It came from more than 150 countries plus the European Union, 37 cities and more than 260 businesses. One alliance of power companies in that collection reported building more than 21,000 kilometres of new electricity lines in 2025. The same fortnight therefore produced two very different pictures: a partial political stall and a tally of power lines, plans and pledges. A reader following climate news is left to work out what each picture actually shows.

## The long clock behind a two-week meeting

Some background explains why a single closing session matters, and where its limits lie.

COP stands for Conference of the Parties. The parties are the governments, along with the European Union, that belong to the UN climate convention, the world's main shared framework for dealing with climate change. In his closing remarks, Stiell referred to 194 Parties. "Parties" is the accurate word because the European Union holds a seat alongside national governments. The parties meet every year. COP31 was scheduled for 9 to 20 November, and its closing session ran on to 22 November.

Inside the same conference, the parties also meet in a second capacity, as members of the Paris Agreement. That gathering has its own formal name, the CMA, and it adopts decisions about how the Paris Agreement works. The Paris Agreement was adopted on 12 December 2015 and entered into force on 4 November 2016. It is a legally binding treaty. Each party submits a national climate plan, usually called an NDC, for "nationally determined contribution." These plans come in five-year cycles. Countries report on their efforts, those reports are reviewed, and a periodic collective check called the global stocktake asks whether the combined effort adds up. The treaty also contains provisions on support: finance, technology and help with building skills and institutions, so that poorer countries can take part.

The agreement's temperature goal is to hold warming well below 2°C above preindustrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. Those figures describe the planet's long-term global average temperature. One hot afternoon in one city is weather; the Paris goal concerns the average of the whole planet across many years.

That background sets the scale. The Paris framework runs on a clock of years and decades, and it stays in force after a difficult conference. COP31's disagreement concerned the texts the Presidency put forward this year. It was a serious event inside a continuing system.

## What happened on 22 November

The official record shows what the Presidency proposed. One document, numbered FCCC/PA/CMA/2026/L.25 and called the Antalya Pact, is dated 22 November 2026. Its header marks it as an advance version, a draft decision and a proposal. A second official record, FCCC/PA/CMA/2026/L.24, concerns the just transition, meaning the effort to treat workers and communities fairly as economies move away from fossil fuels. Its record is timestamped 22 November at 10:45 local time and labels it a draft decision proposed by the President.

These labels matter. A published draft is evidence of what was placed on the table. Whether each paragraph became an adopted decision is a separate question, settled by the final decision record. Stiell's account supplies part of the answer and complicates any simple verdict. Alongside the failure on the Presidency-led texts, he reported that negotiators had reached key decisions, including on the just transition. The accurate summary of COP31's political ending is therefore mixed: a documented disagreement over the two Presidency-led decisions, together with reported agreement on other negotiating items.

Stiell's remarks represent his assessment as head of the secretariat, the body that facilitates the talks. The speech establishes the disagreement and ties it to the two Presidency-led decisions. Vote counts, the positions of individual governments and any single clause that decided the outcome lie beyond what this review of the speech and draft records establishes. Decisions at these meetings are reached through agreement among parties whose economies, energy systems and exposure to climate damage differ enormously. That method gives many voices weight. It also means a text can stall late in the final session.

## Two tracks under one roof

Stiell himself drew the distinction that helps make sense of the contrast. A COP contains formal negotiations, where parties bargain over shared decisions and rules. It also contains the Action Agenda, a voluntary track where governments, cities, companies, investors and other groups announce and report their own work. Stiell described progress in the Action Agenda across six areas, including food and agriculture, and the resilience of cities and infrastructure, meaning their ability to withstand floods, heat and storms.

He also said that country pledges and the Leaders Summit indicated that around half of the global economy was speeding up electrification. Electrification means switching machines and systems to run on electricity, such as electric cars, electric heat pumps for buildings and electric factory equipment, so that clean power sources can replace burning fuel.

An everyday comparison helps show how these tracks relate. It is offered purely as an illustration. Picture a large apartment building whose residents meet once a year to agree on rules for a building-wide renovation: how costs will be shared, which standards everyone will follow, and what help older residents will receive. This year the meeting breaks up with two main sections of the rulebook unsigned, while residents settle some other items. Meanwhile, some residents have already replaced old boilers in their own flats, and a team of electricians has rewired several floors. The rewiring is real. The quarrel at the meeting is also real, and it still affects who pays, how the floors connect, and whether residents with smaller budgets can afford their share. The comparison has clear limits. Countries are sovereign states, COP decisions emerge from international climate negotiations, and a building contract operates on a far smaller scale. The picture simply shows why physical progress and collective agreement belong in two separate ledgers.

## Reading the electricity numbers carefully

The 19 November announcement was the secretariat's Electrification Delivery Package. Its numbers are worth reading slowly, because each one comes with a verb, and the verb carries the meaning.

The headline count describes breadth: more than 150 countries plus the European Union, 37 cities and more than 260 businesses across six continents. Participants could act alone or through coalitions. The package gathered announcements and updates made at different stages and covering different periods, and presented them together on 19 November as a single compilation.

The city figure shows how much the wording matters. The 37 cities are those reporting progress in 2026 or announcing actions for 2027. One city in the count may have finished a bus-electrification programme this year. Another may have announced a plan for next year. Both sit inside the same number. Several verbs that are easy to blur together carry very different weight here: joined, announced, planned, built, spent, measured and verified. Each describes a different kind of evidence, from a name on a list to a result an outsider has checked.

The most concrete physical figure in the package comes from the Utilities for Net Zero Alliance, a group of electricity companies. The alliance reported that its members built more than 21,000 kilometres of new grid in 2025, about 50 percent more than in 2024. As a rough picture of length, 21,000 kilometres is a little more than half the distance around the Earth at the equator. A grid is the network of cables, towers and substations that carries electricity from power plants to homes and businesses, and it is often the bottleneck in the energy transition. New wind and solar farms need lines to reach the customers who will use their power.

A length measurement tells readers one specific thing. Kilometres describe how long the lines are. The capacity of those lines, how many homes they serve, where they run, and how reliably they deliver power are separate measurements, and the announced figure covers length. The figure is also the alliance's own report, relayed by the UN secretariat. As announced, it looks back at completed construction during 2025.

Next to it sits a forward-looking number. The same announcement describes the alliance members' combined annual investment plans of at least US$156 billion across grids, renewable power generation and storage, such as large batteries. That amount adds together what the member companies intend to spend each year, spread across three types of infrastructure, so it covers more than grids alone. It measures planned company spending; evidence of a single newly financed fund created at COP31 would require a different kind of record. The grid kilometres describe the past; the US$156 billion describes intentions for the future. Both have value, and they answer different questions.

A single finance example shows the same distinction clearly. The package reports that AIB, an Irish bank, has set a target to double its green and transition lending to €60 billion by 2030, up from a previous target of €30 billion. The bank separately reports €28 billion deployed since 2019. One figure is a goal for the end of the decade. The other is a reported total of lending over the past several years. Adding them together would produce a meaningless sum, and describing the €60 billion as money already lent would turn an ambition into an achievement. Read side by side, the two figures show where the bank says it has been and where it says it is heading.

## Two shares of the world economy

Two broad figures about the size of the economies involved can easily collide. Stiell said country pledges and the Leaders Summit showed around half of the global economy accelerating electrification. The 19 November package said its participants represent more than two thirds of global GDP, the total value of goods and services the world produces in a year.

These two shares describe different scopes. Stiell's figure concerns pledges and summit activity as he described them. The package figure concerns the economies represented among the package's wider list of participants, who could act individually or through coalitions. Each is a statement about who is taking part in an effort. Neither measures how much of the world already runs on electricity, how much clean power has been delivered, or how far emissions have fallen. The published announcements give the headline shares; the calculations behind them lie beyond the official speech, draft records and announcements reviewed for this article, and they are the natural next thing for researchers to examine.

## Why the stalled text still matters

If grids are being built anyway, a reader might ask why the political disagreement deserves much attention. The answer lies in three terms that often get bundled together.

Mitigation means cutting the greenhouse gas emissions that drive climate change, for example by replacing coal power with wind and solar. Adaptation means preparing for the effects that are already arriving, such as stronger sea walls and heat-resistant roads. A just transition concerns the people affected by the shift, including coal miners, oil workers, and towns whose budgets depend on fossil fuel industries. These three goals overlap, but each has its own measure of success.

A new grid line can help cut emissions when it connects clean generation, such as wind or solar farms, and carries that power to homes and businesses. The size of that benefit depends on what the line connects and what it carries, and the length figure leaves both questions to separate evidence. The line's existence also says little, by itself, about whether a poorer country can afford its own grid, whether a city exposed to flooding can protect its substations, or whether workers in a closing coal region find new employment. Those questions are where collective decisions do their work. The Paris Agreement's provisions on finance, technology and skills exist because countries start from very different positions. Stiell's emphasis on fairness and support at the close of COP31 points to the same concern. Shared rules can shape who receives help, on what terms, and how progress is counted, so that the transition reaches beyond the places already able to pay for it.

The evidence from Antalya draws a clear outline of that problem, and the specific consequences remain open. The official speech, draft records and announcements reviewed here trace the disagreement to the Presidency-led texts; they leave the effects on specific projects, budgets or households for further reporting to establish. What these records do show is an unresolved question of coordination: physical work reported by companies and governments with resources is moving, while the two Presidency-led decisions ended the conference in disagreement.

## The question for the year ahead

COP31's closing week leaves two ledgers open. The first records collective negotiations, proposals and decisions: a reported failure on two Presidency-led decisions, reported agreement on others including the just transition, and draft texts whose final status sits in the official decision record. The second records reported action in the real economy: a long list of participants, 37 cities at various stages, more than 21,000 kilometres of grid built in 2025 by one alliance's members, at least US$156 billion a year in planned investment, and a bank lending target for 2030.

Each entry in the second ledger now faces its own test. Will the reported kilometres turn out to carry enough power, in the right places, to connect new clean energy? Will annual investment plans show up in records of money actually spent, and of power plants and batteries in operation? Will city announcements for 2027 later appear as electric buses and electrically heated homes in service? And in the first ledger, will next year's negotiators find a way to settle the questions about fairness and support that Antalya left open?

Climate progress, read this way, becomes a set of separate measures: joining, announcing, planning, building, spending and a measured fall in emissions. Each calls for its own evidence, and different participants move through them in different orders; a company can commit money before a line is finished, for instance. The Antalya fortnight added real entries to several of these records and exposed an open question in collective coordination. Later records may show whether the grid construction reported for 2025 and the members' plans for future investment come to be matched by shared rules and support that help each country take part in the transition.
