# Twelve Corals Were Filed Under Other Names. What Have Reef Surveys Been Counting?

On November 12, 2026, a team led by Augustine Crosbie, a coral researcher working with James Cook University and Queensland Museum in Australia, published a study that gives twelve reef corals their own names back. The corals belong to *Acropora*, a large group of reef-building corals that includes the branching forms called staghorns and the broad, flat forms called table corals. Scientists had described each of the twelve long ago. Later classifications folded each one into another species as a duplicate. The new study appeared in the journal *Invertebrate Systematics*, and James Cook University announced it the same day. It concludes that these twelve are separate species. The corals were living on reefs the whole time. The scientific record of who they are is what changed.

That makes this an unusual kind of reef news. Most coral stories people hear involve something physical: a heatwave, a bleaching event, a recovery after a storm, a new patch of growth. This one involves a list. The study supports recognising twelve corals as separate species, and that changes the scientific inventory of reef life. How many colonies of each coral grow on a reef is a separate question, answered by its own counts in the water. A newspaper headline about "twelve species" can sound like a discovery made by divers on a lucky afternoon. The study joined museum collections, modern sampling of living corals and genomic analysis, and it concerns identity: which coral is which.

Identity sounds like a small matter until you consider how much work a name does. A coral is an animal. Each colony is built by many tiny soft-bodied animals called polyps, which lay down a hard limestone skeleton beneath themselves. Over years, those skeletons pile up into the structures that fish, crabs, turtles and people depend on. *Acropora* corals are among the most important builders on Indo-Pacific reefs, including Australia's Great Barrier Reef. Researchers who monitor reefs, managers who decide where to direct protection, and teams who grow corals for restoration all record their work by species name. A name lets a museum label a specimen, lets a survey diver write down what they saw, and lets a conservation assessment gather thousands of scattered sightings into one picture of where a species lives and how it is faring.

If two different corals share one name, all of their sightings land in one pile. That pile may look large and reassuring. The real picture may be two smaller piles, each describing a species with its own range and its own risks. The November 12 study opens exactly that possibility for twelve corals, and it raises a question that every reef survey has to face: when we counted this coral, which coral were we counting?

## A name tied to an object in a drawer

Scientific names for animals and plants are anchored to physical things. When a scientist first describes a species, they designate a reference specimen, called a type specimen, and that specimen is kept in a museum collection. The name points to that object. If someone later wants to know what a name truly means, the type specimen is the final reference. One especially important kind, the holotype, is the single specimen chosen to carry the name.

Over time, taxonomists, the scientists who sort living things into species, compare names and specimens. Sometimes they decide two names describe the same species. In that case one name stays in use and the other becomes a synonym, a second label for the same thing. The second name stays in the records, attached to its type specimen, and it waits there. If later evidence shows the two are distinct after all, a scientist can lift the old name back out of synonymy and restore it as a species. That is what happened to the twelve *Acropora* names.

Corals make this sorting famously hard. A single coral species can grow into quite different shapes depending on light, wave strength and depth, and different species can grow into shapes that look alike. Many *Acropora* names were first given in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, based on skeletons alone. The people who named them worked with the evidence they had: the shape of branches, the arrangement of tiny cups where the polyps lived, the texture of the limestone. Later researchers looking at that same kind of evidence sometimes judged that two names described ordinary variation within one species, and merged them.

Modern genetics offers a way to test those judgments, with a catch. Old museum skeletons often hold DNA that is too degraded or too scarce to read usefully. So the oldest reference specimens, the ones that define the names, frequently stay silent on genetics. The researchers needed a bridge between the old skeletons and living coral.

That bridge is called a topotype. A topotype is a modern comparison specimen linked to the place a name's original type specimen came from. Where the original locality was recorded clearly and could be sampled, the topotype comes from there. Where the old record is vague or the locality unknown, or where access is limited, the researchers chose the best comparison provenance they could attain. The logic runs like this: if the old skeleton came from a particular reef, and a modern colony from that reef or a well-matched stand-in matches the old skeleton's shape, then the modern colony probably belongs to the same species, and its fresh DNA can speak for the name.

According to the paper's abstract, the team examined 369 specimens. Among them were four holotypes whose DNA they were able to sequence directly, and 49 topotypes that linked names to modern material. These are counts of specimens, the physical samples in the study, and they measure the size of the evidence base. The researchers combined three kinds of evidence: the skeleton's form, the geography of where specimens came from, and phylogenomics, a method that reconstructs family relationships among corals from many regions of their genetic code at once. Each kind of evidence covers a weakness in the others. Shape can mislead, geography alone is a hint, and genetic relationships need a trustworthy link back to a name. Together they support a hypothesis about which name belongs to which group of living corals.

The paper is candid about where that bridge is firmer and where it is looser. Some historical localities were recorded vaguely, and a few are unknown. Field access and collecting permits also limit where researchers can gather fresh samples. The authors assign their topotype links differing levels of geographic confidence and treat all of them as testable hypotheses that future work can revise. Some links between a name and a modern colony are strong; others are the best available match for now. That openness is part of the result.

Here is an everyday analogy, offered purely as an illustration. Picture a large public library whose catalogue, decades ago, filed several different books under one title because their covers looked similar. A careful librarian pulls the original copies from the archive, compares them with copies on today's shelves, reads the pages inside, and checks which branch each copy came from. She concludes that the single catalogue entry actually covers several distinct books. Fixing the catalogue changes the library's records; the books on the shelves are a matter for a separate count. It does let the library see which book sits at which branch, and it may reveal that one of those books exists in just a handful of copies. The coral study works at that catalogue stage. It improves the inventory first; any change on the reef itself is a separate matter.

## Counting carefully: 53, 47, twelve and four

The numbers in this study are precise, and they describe a carefully chosen set of names.

The team evaluated 53 named species of *Acropora*. Forty of those names have type localities in eastern Australia, meaning their reference specimens came from that region. The other 13 are names from elsewhere in the Indo-Pacific, included because they were needed to test whether particular eastern Australian names duplicated them.

Of the 53, the study supports 47 as distinct species. Within those 47 sit the two headline groups. Twelve are the names lifted out of synonymy. Four are taxa whose status had been unresolved, which the study now recognises as valid species. The twelve and the four are part of the 47. Adding them on top would count the same findings twice.

It also helps to be clear about the scale of the tally. It covers this targeted set of names connected to eastern Australia. The full roster of *Acropora* species worldwide, the total coral diversity of the Great Barrier Reef, and the number of living colonies all lie outside these counts. Forty-seven is a verdict on a list of names. It measures how many distinct species those names describe.

This account draws on the university's announcement and on the paper's abstract and excerpts from its methods and discussion. Both come from the same research effort. The paper's full species-by-species tables and supplementary material hold the detailed account for each name.

## From a sharper list to sharper questions

Why would a correction to a list matter to the people trying to protect a reef? Two researchers featured in the university's announcement offer the clearest answers.

Peter Cowman raises the possibility that a coral regarded as common and widespread may actually consist of several distinct species, each with a smaller range of its own. That possibility matters because range is one of the things conservation assessments weigh. In principle, a species spread across a vast area could hold populations in many places, so trouble in one place might leave others in good shape, provided those other populations are themselves healthy. Actual risk depends on where a species lives and on the conditions across that range; a wide range offers a possible cushion whose real strength has to be checked. If that wide range belongs to several species, each one covers less ground than the combined picture suggested, and any such cushion may be thinner. Cowman connects this to how scientists assess risk, how they design monitoring and how they allocate conservation resources. His point is a reasoned implication of the taxonomic result. The study, as reported, leaves each of the twelve species' ranges and risk levels for future work to establish.

Andrew Baird emphasises a different lesson: even the Great Barrier Reef, one of the most intensively studied reef systems on Earth, still holds more coral diversity than scientists had recognised. Decades of surveys, expeditions and research papers had passed over these distinctions. That observation carries weight for less-studied reefs, where the gap between recognised and actual diversity may be larger.

From those two points, a set of practical questions follows. They are questions the new classification makes possible to ask; answers will come from future fieldwork and record checks.

The first concerns monitoring. Reef surveys often record corals by species or by groups of species. If a survey team has been recording one name that now covers several species, its records may mix them together. A team could revisit samples, photographs or field notes to see whether the distinctions can be recovered.

The second concerns old records. Some historical sightings came with a specimen, a photograph or a precise location, and those may be reassignable to the correct species today. Other records hold only a name and a place. Those older entries may be hard to reassign reliably for now, and later surveys can test them. Their ambiguity is itself useful information, because it shows where fresh surveys would add the most.

The third concerns restoration. Coral restoration programmes grow fragments in nurseries and plant them back onto damaged reefs, and some collect and store coral material for the future. Queensland Museum runs a coral project called CoralBank. A restoration mix labelled with one name might, under the new classification, contain more than one species. Knowing exactly what is in the mix helps a programme track which species it is supporting and match corals to the places they belong.

The fourth concerns maps. Once names are separated, each species needs its own map of where it actually lives. Some of those maps may turn out broad. Others may turn out narrow. Either way, the mapping draws on existing records re-identified under the new names, together with fresh observations tied to the revised identities.

This reporting establishes a finding about species identity. Recognising a species and seeing a reef recover are two different kinds of measurement. The first is read from specimens, places and genomes. The second is read from living corals observed in the water over time. Any claim that the new names improved coral survival or guided a better restoration project will need its own suitable evidence of those effects. A revised threat assessment can draw on existing specimens, photographs and historical records once they are re-identified under the new names, alongside new observations where they help. For now, the gain is in knowledge: twelve distinctions that earlier classifications had set aside are back on the record, supported by evidence that others can test.

There is something quietly significant in how that gain was made. Museum drawers holding skeletons collected generations ago turned out to be essential to a modern genetic study. The old specimens supplied the names; the living reef supplied the DNA; careful matching of place and shape joined the two. The collections earned their keep by holding onto evidence until tools arrived that could read it more fully. The authors' own framing of their links as revisable keeps the door open for the next round of evidence to strengthen or adjust what they found.

The library analogy helps one final time, again purely as illustration. A corrected catalogue is a beginning. The librarian now knows that several different books share a shelf. The next task is walking the branches, counting copies of each, and noticing which ones are rare.

For reef science, that walk is just starting. Twelve corals have their own names again, and each name carries a fresh question that divers, monitoring teams and museum researchers can now go and answer: where does this particular coral actually live, and what is happening to it there?
