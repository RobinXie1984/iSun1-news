# Climate Money Has a Target for 2035. Will It Reach Vulnerable Countries Before the Next Storm?

On November 13, 2026, Simon Stiell, the Executive Secretary of UN Climate Change, spoke in Antalya, a city on Türkiye's Mediterranean coast. The occasion was the seventh biennial high-level ministerial dialogue on climate finance. That meeting took place during COP31, the United Nations climate conference scheduled for November 9 to 20. Stiell leads the UN body that organises these summits and supports the global climate treaty process. Climate finance broadly involves two groups. One group supplies climate money, such as governments of wealthier nations, development banks and investors. The other group needs that money, including poorer and more exposed countries. Stiell's subject was the journey money takes between those two groups. He asked the providers to change how finance is delivered, so that countries can afford to protect themselves from climate hazards and grow their economies while releasing less greenhouse gas.

Some background helps. "Climate finance" is money meant to support action on climate change. In this story it serves two main purposes. The first is resilience, which means strengthening people, roads, hospitals, farms and power lines so they can withstand a flood or heatwave, or recover quickly afterward. The second is lower-emissions development, which means meeting real needs for electricity, transport and jobs while putting less heat-trapping gas into the atmosphere. Both purposes share an awkward feature. They demand spending up front, and the benefits arrive later, sometimes years later. Often the benefit takes the form of a disaster that does less damage than it otherwise would have. Governments weigh that spending against schools, salaries and debts that fall due this month.

For many years, international climate finance revolved around a goal of $100 billion a year for developing countries. At COP29, held in Baku, Azerbaijan, in November 2024, governments set larger targets. One target is $300 billion per year by 2035, with developed countries taking the lead. The other is broader. It calls for efforts by all actors to scale up finance for developing countries, from all public and private sources, to $1.3 trillion per year by 2035. A billion is a thousand million. A trillion is a thousand billion, or a million million. So $1.3 trillion is a little more than four times $300 billion.

These two numbers fit together in a specific way. The $300 billion goal is the narrower one, centred on the leadership of developed countries. It sits within the wider $1.3 trillion ambition, which covers money from every kind of source, including private investors. The largest headline figure is therefore $1.3 trillion. Adding the two together would treat overlapping scopes as two separate pots. The COP29 summary describes the goals and their scopes. The method for counting future reported flows, and for comparing them with money actually paid out, lies beyond the sources used here. The comparison with the older goal is also useful, with care. $300 billion is three times $100 billion in nominal dollars, which means dollars as written, before any adjustment for inflation. That tripling measures a rise in stated ambition. Whether the new figure matches what countries need is a separate question, and needs assessments and price data are required to answer it.

One more distinction matters before the speech itself. Each of these figures describes a destination for the year 2035. A target states an intended annual scale for a future year. A payment received in 2026 is a different thing, and a particular project receiving funds is different again.

## The loop Stiell described

Stiell's speech began from a problem he sees in the finances of vulnerable countries. In his account, the expectation of future climate shocks can make it more expensive for a country to borrow, because lenders charge more when they see a higher risk of trouble. When a shock arrives, recovery costs rise. Government income can also shrink, as damaged farms, ports and businesses produce less tax. The country may then borrow to rebuild, which adds to its debts. Repaying those debts, a cost economists call debt service, takes money that could have paid for protection. A less protected country is more exposed when the next shock comes, and the cycle can turn again.

This is Stiell's economic argument, presented at a policy meeting. He described a mechanism that he sees trapping countries. The speech offers it as a diagnosis. Measuring how strongly the loop operates in any particular nation would require country-level data. The strength of each link will vary with the country, its lenders, its existing debts and the size of the disaster. Even so, the logic explains why Stiell spent much of his speech on how money arrives, alongside how much of it there is.

An everyday analogy can make the mechanism concrete. It is an illustration only, about an imaginary family. Picture a household whose roof leaks badly ahead of storm season. The family's bank announces that it plans to make far more repair money available to customers over the coming decade. That is welcome news. Yet the family still faces a series of practical steps. It must qualify. It must learn whether the money is a gift or a loan, and if it is a loan, how large the monthly repayments will be. The money must arrive before the storms do. The family must then hire a competent roofer. After the first heavy rain, it can finally see whether the roof holds. If the only option is an expensive loan, the family might fix the roof and then struggle to pay for the next repair. If a storm hits first, the family pays for water damage and has even less left for the roof. Each step between the announcement and a dry living room is a place where the promise can lose some of its value.

Stiell's four proposals map roughly onto those steps. Each is a change he advocated. The speech presents them as priorities for providers and for the dialogue's continuing work.

## Four ways to make the money count

The first proposal concerns terms. Terms are the conditions attached to money, and they decide what a recipient owes afterward. Stiell called for affordable finance that takes account of debt sustainability, meaning a country's ability to keep paying its debts while still running the state. He saw a role for private investment, loans and guarantees. He also treated grants and highly concessional finance as especially important for three groups. The first is the Least Developed Countries, a UN category for the poorest economies. The second is Small Island Developing States, many of them low-lying and heavily exposed to storms and rising seas. The third is other vulnerable economies.

Those instrument names carry real differences, and they explain why a headline total reveals only part of the picture. A grant is money given for a purpose. In general, the recipient keeps the principal, though grants usually come with conditions about how the money is spent. A loan must be repaid, usually with interest, on an agreed schedule. Concessional finance comes on kinder terms than a comparable ordinary deal, such as a lower interest rate or a longer time to repay. The exact benefit depends on each contract. A guarantee works differently again. It is a commitment to carry a specified risk. The guarantor pays only if agreed conditions occur and the guarantee is called. That commitment can make investors more willing to take part, and a call on it can turn into a real bill later. Consider a hypothetical case: two countries each reported as receiving the same sum for flood defences. One receives a grant. The other receives a loan at commercial rates. On a spreadsheet the amounts match. Inside a finance ministry, the grant leaves the budget roomier, and the loan adds a repayment line for years to come.

The second proposal is simpler, better coordinated application and administrative procedures. Before an award becomes usable, a recipient spends staff time and money writing proposals, meeting reporting requirements and steering approvals through. Several funders may each ask for different forms, standards and timelines. When that happens, a small ministry can spend much of its limited capacity on paperwork. In the roof analogy, the family has to fill in a separate lengthy application for every lender in town, each with its own rules, while the rain clouds gather. Stiell's proposal aims to lighten that load. The speech treats the burden as a priority to address.

The third proposal concerns whose hands hold the money. Stiell urged stronger national treasuries, local governments, national development banks and other domestic systems, with each country's own priorities in the lead. The reasoning behind the proposal is practical. A seawall, a clinic or a power line needs an institution that can plan it, fit it into a budget, oversee construction and maintain it for decades. In Stiell's view, strong local institutions that set the agenda put outside money to work on what a country actually needs, and they keep the results running long after the opening ceremony. Stiell advocated this as a direction for investment and reform. The condition of any particular country's institutions is a matter for evidence from that country.

The fourth proposal joins money to knowledge. Stiell called for finance, technology transfer and capacity building to be coordinated around country priorities. Technology transfer means sharing equipment, designs and know-how, such as methods for building drought-tolerant water systems or running a power grid with a large share of solar energy. Capacity building means training people and strengthening organisations so they can run such systems themselves. Money that arrives alone can stall. To take a hypothetical case, a city might receive funds for flood forecasting and lack the engineers to install sensors or the software to interpret their readings. A project usually needs people, expertise and a workable design in addition to cash.

Beyond fixing individual steps, Stiell sketched a hopeful sequence. In his account, early grants can help countries build institutional strength and a pipeline of investment-ready projects. Concessional and risk-sharing support can then help those projects grow to a larger scale. As institutions grow stronger, investment conditions improve and capital becomes cheaper. Resilience then grows and economies strengthen. This sequence is the mirror image of the debt loop he described at the outset, a virtuous circle in place of a vicious one. It is a proposed pathway. Each link depends on execution and local conditions, and the speech presents it as a route worth pursuing. Stiell noted that this dialogue series dates back to 2013 and that its work will feed into COP32, the next summit. Feeding into a future conference is a step in a process. Approval of any new financial instrument would come through later decisions.

## From promise to proof

Where do matters stand in mid-November 2026? COP31 remained in session on November 16, with its close scheduled for November 20, and the conference's formal outcome lies ahead. This article reports Stiell's November 13 proposals and the earlier COP29 targets; the implementation and outcomes of climate finance call for evidence from subsequent records.

That boundary sharpens the real story. The sums attached to climate finance have grown on paper, from $100 billion a year toward $300 billion, and toward a wider ambition of $1.3 trillion, by 2035. Stiell's argument treats size as one dimension of value. The other dimensions are the terms that decide what a country owes, the effort required to obtain the money, the strength of the institutions that spend it and the knowledge needed to turn it into working infrastructure. Consider a promised dollar delivered as an expensive loan, after long paperwork, to an overstretched ministry with few engineers. It is likely to buy less protection than a dollar delivered promptly as a grant to an institution ready to use it.

Judging whether the new targets are working therefore means following money through separate stages. The first stage is the terms offered: how much arrives as grants, how much as loans and at what cost, and how much takes the form of guarantees, which stand as risk commitments and pay out when called. For a funded project, the second stage is disbursement, the moment funds reach a recipient's accounts, which can come well after a commitment is announced. The third is implementation, such as a built seawall, a trained team or a functioning early-warning system. The fourth is measured results: evidence that people and infrastructure are safer, or that emissions are lower. Each stage follows the previous one, sometimes by years, and each can be reported separately. In a hypothetical timeline, a government could announce a commitment in 2027, pay out in 2029 and finish construction in 2031. Design reviews, testing and maintenance records would then supply evidence of the defences' readiness before any extreme event. A later major flood would add observations of how they performed in that particular event.

Return, for a moment, to the imaginary family with the leaking roof. The bank's announcement is encouraging. The household's question is more down to earth. When the storm comes, will the roof hold, and will the repair leave the family able to afford the next one?

A similar question now faces the vulnerable countries at the centre of Stiell's proposals. As 2035 draws closer, what evidence will show that larger climate-finance goals are helping these countries build protection and cleaner growth, while easing the cycle of debt and disaster he described in Antalya?
