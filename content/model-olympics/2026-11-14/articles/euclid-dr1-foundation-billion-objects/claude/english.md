# Euclid Has Caught a Billion Lights. Now the Hunt Begins for How Far Away They Are.

On 12 November 2026, the European Space Agency released the first major batch of science data from Euclid, a space telescope built to chart galaxies across a vast stretch of sky. The European Space Agency, usually shortened to ESA, is an organisation of European countries that builds and flies science missions, and it is a separate body from the institutions of the European Union. Euclid exists to help scientists study two of the largest puzzles in physics. The first is dark matter, an unseen form of matter that astronomers detect through its gravitational pull on everything around it. The second is dark energy, the name scientists give to whatever is making the expansion of the universe speed up. The new release, called DR1-Foundation, contains images, catalogues and light measurements for more than one billion objects, according to ESA. It works like a huge library that has just opened to researchers. Turning that library into a three-dimensional map of the universe is the work that comes next.

To see why that next step matters, start with a basic limitation that every picture of the night sky shares. A photograph records where a light appears: a little higher, a little further left. That position is a direction. It tells you which way to look. A true map of the universe also needs a third number for each object, its distance, and a flat image holds only weak hints about it. A small, faint galaxy might be modest in size and fairly close, or enormous and extremely far away. Astronomers call this missing coordinate depth. Much of Euclid's design, and much of the work that follows this release, is aimed at recovering it.

## A telescope built for a census

Euclid launched in July 2023 and began routine science observations on 14 February 2024. The public has seen its work before. ESA shared striking early images and released preliminary survey data in earlier rounds, including releases in March 2025 and June 2026. DR1-Foundation is different in scale and purpose. It is the mission's first substantial data release, and ESA presents it as a large, calibrated body of Euclid survey material packaged for wide scientific use.

Two dates sit inside this story, and they describe different events. The release date is 12 November 2026. The observations themselves come from roughly 13 months of work, which ESA's survey caption places between spring 2024 and spring 2025. The gap between gathering light and publishing it is filled with processing: removing instrument quirks, lining up overlapping images, measuring brightness consistently and sorting millions of detections into catalogues.

Euclid is built as a census-taker. Some telescopes stare deeply at a few small patches of sky to study individual objects in fine detail. Euclid sweeps steadily across large areas, photographing galaxy after galaxy in the same way, so that scientists can compare enormous numbers of them fairly. ESA's material describes two survey modes. The Wide Survey covers broad territory, and the Deep Survey returns repeatedly to smaller fields to collect fainter light. The headline numbers in this release come mainly from the Wide Survey.

Those numbers are large, and each one measures something different. ESA reports light from more than one billion objects across about 1,900 square degrees of Wide Survey sky. A square degree is a unit of apparent area on the sky, the size of a patch as seen from Earth. It measures how much of the sky's dome a region covers; the region's physical width in kilometres and its depth into space are separate quantities that call for distance information. ESA gives a friendly comparison: 1,900 square degrees is roughly the area that about 9,500 full Moons would cover if you tiled them across the sky. Each full Moon appears as a small disc on the sky's dome, and the Wide Survey territory equals thousands of those discs in total apparent area.

Even this large area is a fraction of the plan. ESA describes the 1,900 square degrees as about 14% of the area Euclid intends to survey over its six-year programme. That percentage refers specifically to the planned survey footprint. It describes progress across the sky Euclid is scheduled to cover, and it leaves the share of eventual scientific discoveries an open matter. The release also has a physical bulk: ESA puts the data volume at about 1.7 million gigabytes, a figure the agency reports for the release as a whole.

## A billion lights, many labels

Inside the release, ESA reports several catalogues. More than 900 million objects are listed as galaxies. Over 60 million are quasar candidates. Around 15,000 are candidates for strong gravitational lenses. And more than 160 million sources have spectra, which are measurements of how their light is spread across different colours.

These figures describe overlapping labels applied to one shared pool of lights. A single galaxy can carry a spectrum and also appear in the lens list, so each count stands on its own, and ESA's overall figure for the release remains more than one billion objects.

The word "candidate" deserves attention because it describes the status of an entry. A quasar is the blazing centre of a galaxy where a giant black hole is swallowing gas, which heats the surrounding material until it outshines the whole galaxy. From far away, a quasar can resemble an ordinary star or a compact galaxy. A quasar candidate is an object whose light suggests it belongs in this category, and confirming it requires further checks. The same applies to the lens candidates. The catalogues are sorted shortlists, prepared so that scientists can decide where to look closely.

That is why a billion recorded lights are a starting point. Each entry has a position, a brightness and a shape. Many have colour information. Some have spectra. For most of them, the question of how far away they are is still being worked out.

## From direction to depth

Here an everyday comparison helps, and it is only a comparison. Picture a photograph taken straight down a long city street at night. You can see lit windows to the left and right, high and low, and you can point to each one precisely. The photograph alone leaves you guessing how far down the street each window sits. A bright window could be a nearby lamp or a large floodlight several blocks away. If you also had a clue that tied each light to a distance, such as a tag stating which block it belongs to, you could rebuild the street in three dimensions. Euclid's images supply the directions. Its spectra supply the kind of clue that can lead to distance. The comparison ends there, because the cosmic clue comes from physics on a scale far beyond any street.

A spectrum is made by spreading light out by wavelength, the way a prism spreads sunlight into a rainbow. ESA's own explainer uses that rainbow picture, and it serves as a simple illustration of the idea; Euclid's instrument is a precision device built for the task. Atoms and gas leave fingerprints in that spread-out light: bright or dark lines at particular wavelengths. One fingerprint Euclid searches for is hydrogen-alpha, a glow produced by hydrogen gas in regions where new stars are forming.

The universe is expanding, and light travelling across expanding space gets stretched on its journey. Stretched light shifts towards the red end of the rainbow, an effect called redshift. If researchers find the hydrogen-alpha line and measure how far it has moved from its usual position, they can estimate how much the light was stretched. Combined with a model of how the universe has expanded over time, that stretch translates into an estimate of distance.

Each step in that chain carries its own work. A recorded spectrum is raw material. Identifying a line within it is a judgement that can sometimes go wrong, because a different line at a different redshift can land in a similar place. A redshift estimate then needs checking, and turning it into a distance depends on cosmological modelling. ESA reports spectra from more than 160 million sources. The number of those that will yield secure distances, and how precise those distances will be, are results for later analysis to establish. The release makes the three-dimensional map possible; the map itself is still under construction.

## When gravity bends the view

The most vivid part of the release involves light that has been bent on its way to us. Anything with mass curves the space around it, and light passing nearby follows that curve. When a massive galaxy or cluster of galaxies sits almost exactly in front of a more distant one, the foreground mass acts as a lens. The background galaxy can appear smeared into a bright arc, stretched into a near-perfect ring, or copied into several images arranged around the foreground object. Astronomers call this strong gravitational lensing.

The bending comes from all the mass in the foreground system. That includes its stars, its dark matter and the giant black hole that usually sits at a galaxy's centre. Because the amount of bending depends on the total mass, careful modelling of a lens lets researchers weigh the foreground object and estimate how much of its mass is invisible. That is one route by which lensing feeds into the study of dark matter, while the mass budget remains a mix of ingredients.

Lenses also act as natural magnifiers. They enlarge and brighten background galaxies that would otherwise be too faint to study in detail, giving astronomers a closer look at the distant universe. ESA highlights rarer arrangements too. In some systems, two or more background galaxies at different distances line up behind the same foreground lens, producing nested patterns. Because each background source sits at its own distance, these compound systems carry extra information about geometry.

Lensed quasars offer another kind of clue. A quasar's brightness flickers over days, weeks and months. When a quasar is lensed into several images, those images show the same quasar seen along different routes through space. Light along each route takes a slightly different time to arrive, for two linked reasons: the routes have different shapes and lengths, and the gravity of the foreground mass changes the travel time by a different amount along each one. So a flicker reaches us through one image first and through another later. As a general principle of the method, explained in earlier published lensing research, the size of that delay depends on the lens's mass, the distances in the system and how fast the universe is expanding, which is why ESA points to lensed quasars as useful for expansion studies.

ESA reports around 15,000 strong-lens candidates in this release. According to the agency, they were found by combining machine learning, which trains computer programs to recognise lens-like shapes, with the work of thousands of volunteer citizen scientists who examined images through the Space Warps project on the Zooniverse platform. ESA describes the resulting catalogue as larger than all previous lens catalogues combined.

The value of that collection lies in its consistency as much as its size. Earlier lens samples came from many telescopes, each with its own sharpness, colours and search methods, which makes comparisons across samples awkward. A large set gathered by one telescope in one systematic search gives researchers a better footing for population studies: how lens masses compare, how lensing galaxies are distributed, how common the rare configurations really are.

Two conditions shape what that population can show. First, each candidate needs confirmation, often through follow-up spectra that establish the foreground and background distances. Some candidates may turn out to be look-alikes, such as galaxies with naturally curved arms. Second, any search finds some kinds of lenses more easily than others. Big, bright arcs are easier to spot than faint, compact ones. Scientists will need to measure the search's selection, meaning which lenses it tends to catch and which it tends to miss, before treating the catalogue as a fair sample of all lenses. The ESA release materials consulted for this article report the candidate count and describe the search method; confirmation rates and completeness estimates lie outside what those materials report.

## A glimpse of the scale

ESA also published a northern mosaic that makes the scale visible. It stitches together 740 observations into an image of nearly 500 gigapixels, meaning nearly 500 billion picture elements, covering 367 square degrees observed in autumn 2024 and spring 2025. The mosaic is a showcase drawn from part of the data. Its area is about a fifth of the 1,900 square degrees in the Wide Survey release, and its pixel count is a separate measure from the 1.7 million gigabytes of the full data volume.

## The web behind the lights

Zooming out, the reason to collect so many galaxies is that galaxies trace something larger. On the biggest scales, matter is arranged in a pattern astronomers call the cosmic web: dense knots where galaxy clusters gather, long filaments connecting them, and sparse regions between. Most of that structure's mass is thought to be dark matter, whose gravity built the scaffolding that ordinary matter later filled with galaxies.

Dark matter and dark energy are two distinct mysteries. Dark matter is a question about what holds structures together, inferred from the way galaxies move and light bends. Dark energy is a question about the expansion of the whole universe, and why that expansion appears to be speeding up. The word "dark" in both names means "unexplained" in the scientific sense; it describes the state of our understanding, and the two names label separate open problems.

Euclid is designed to test ideas about both. The way the cosmic web has grown over time depends on the balance between gravity pulling matter together and expansion pulling it apart. If researchers can place enough galaxies at reliable distances, they can compare the patterns they measure against the patterns that different models of the universe predict. That comparison needs positions, distances, an understanding of selection effects, careful calibration and statistical analysis, all layered on top of the raw catalogues.

ESA has said the fuller DR1 release is expected in mid-2027. The agency plans for it to add weak-lensing observations, which measure the faint, collective distortion of many background galaxies by intervening matter, along with further work on galaxy clusters and the cosmic web. That is a schedule and a stated plan. For now, DR1-Foundation provides raw data, calibrated images, catalogues and spectra, with access offered to researchers through ESA's Data Space platform.

All of the figures in this story come from ESA, the agency that runs the mission. They describe what the agency has released and how it frames the release's purpose. Independent scientists will test those catalogues, check the classifications, confirm or reject the candidates and publish their own analyses. Those studies will decide which of the patterns hold.

That brings the story to its open question. For decades, astronomers studying lenses, quasars and the cosmic web have often worked from scattered collections of remarkable individual objects. A consistent census of a billion lights invites a different kind of question: whether a pattern seen in one striking example holds across tens of thousands of comparable cases. Euclid has supplied directions for an enormous number of lights and spectral clues for a large share of them. As researchers work through the spectra and assign depth to more and more of those points, which patterns in the cosmic web will remain sharp enough to test our current picture of dark matter and dark energy?
