# Euclid Has Recorded a Billion Cosmic Lights. Which Will Reveal How the Universe Grew?

The European Space Agency’s Euclid space telescope studies how matter gathered into galaxies and how the Universe expanded around them. On November 12, 2026, ESA released DR1-Foundation, its first major collection of mission data, opening observations of more than a billion objects to research. The ambition behind this immense resource is to connect the visible arrangement of galaxies with the forces shaping cosmic history. Its immediate achievement is a shared collection of images, catalogues and spectra—measurements that separate light into its different wavelengths. Turning those records into a reliable account of the Universe’s growth is the scientific work now taking shape.

That history is written across an uneven landscape. Galaxies gather in dense regions and stretch along vast connections, forming what astronomers call the cosmic web. Gravity draws matter together, while the expansion of space changes the distances between large-scale structures. The pattern visible today carries traces of both processes. Looking farther into space also means receiving light that has travelled for longer, giving researchers access to earlier chapters of that history.

Two unresolved questions sit behind Euclid’s mission. Dark matter is matter whose presence scientists infer through its gravitational effects. Dark energy names the unresolved cause or description associated with the accelerating expansion of the Universe. Each enters the cosmic story differently: one helps explain how gravity shapes structures; the other concerns how expansion changes over time. Euclid’s observations can help researchers test models that bring these effects together.

A useful map for that purpose needs several kinds of information. Researchers must establish where galaxies appear, estimate their distances and measure how gravity affects the light reaching the telescope. They also need to understand which objects their methods capture most readily. The challenge is to connect a magnificent view with measurements whose strengths and uncertainties are clear.

Euclid launched in July 2023 and began routine science observations on February 14, 2024. ESA says the new release draws on roughly 13 months of observing, with its survey description placing the collection period from spring 2024 to spring 2025. Earlier public images and preliminary survey material had already introduced the mission’s capabilities. November’s release marks a larger step: an extensive research resource assembled from sustained observing.

According to ESA, the Wide Survey material covers 1,900 square degrees, an apparent area comparable to roughly 9,500 full Moons. A square degree measures a patch of the sky as seen by an observer. It describes how broadly the telescope has looked; depth requires additional information. The Moon comparison likewise concerns the area those familiar discs would occupy across our view.

ESA places this coverage at about 14% of the intended survey area for the mission’s six-year programme. That percentage tracks the planned footprint on the sky. Scientific progress has its own timetable, as researchers examine the recorded light and establish which measurements can support particular questions. ESA reports a release volume of about 1.7 million gigabytes, encompassing raw data, calibrated images, catalogues and spectra, with access through its Data Space platform.

The catalogue figures convey the opportunity, provided their meanings stay distinct. ESA reports more than 900 million galaxies, over 60 million quasar candidates, around 15,000 strong gravitational-lens candidates and spectra from more than 160 million sources. A quasar is an intensely bright, active galactic centre, powered by material falling towards a central black hole. Candidate status identifies an object for further assessment. These categories can overlap: one object may appear in an image catalogue, have a spectrum and participate in a lensing system.

Imagine, as an illustration, making a city map from a very high window after dusk. You could mark thousands of lights across the view. A nearby light and a distant one might appear close together, even though their streets lie far apart. Your drawing would record their directions accurately while leaving their arrangement in depth unresolved. Extra clues would help place them along the line of sight.

A sky image poses a related problem. It supplies two angular coordinates, locating a source across the view. Galaxies that appear to be neighbours may occupy very different distances. Establishing depth allows astronomers to distinguish a physical gathering of galaxies from an apparent grouping produced by perspective. Across a huge survey, that distinction changes the structure of the map.

Spectroscopy provides an important route towards that depth. It separates incoming light by wavelength, producing a pattern somewhat like a finely measured rainbow. At particular wavelengths, atoms and gas leave recognizable features. Researchers can identify a feature, compare its observed position with its known wavelength and measure how much the light has shifted.

One feature sought in Euclid’s spectral work is hydrogen-alpha emission, associated with gas in star-forming regions. As space expands during light’s journey, the light’s wavelength stretches. The resulting shift towards longer wavelengths is called redshift. Measuring that shift helps researchers place a source within cosmic history; translating it into a distance also depends on a model of the Universe’s expansion.

The city-window analogy reaches its limit here. Galaxies evolve, and their light travels through an expanding Universe. A distance estimate emerges from identifying spectral features and interpreting their shifts. A source’s apparent colour alone leaves several possible explanations, including its own properties and the journey its light has taken.

The distinction between recording a spectrum and establishing a reliable redshift therefore matters. A clear, correctly identified feature can be highly informative. A faint or ambiguous feature leaves a wider range of interpretations. ESA’s figure of more than 160 million spectral sources describes the scale of the recorded resource. The number of secure distances, and their precision, requires assessment of those measurements.

That assessment has direct consequences for the cosmic web. If distance estimates scatter galaxies into the wrong positions, a real concentration can appear more spread out. If the same measurement tendency affects many sources, it can alter an apparent pattern across the survey. These are general analytical risks that researchers must quantify when building a three-dimensional map. The size of a catalogue makes that work more consequential.

Euclid’s strong-lens candidates offer a particularly vivid example of the information hidden in an image. Under a favourable alignment, the gravity of a foreground galaxy or another massive structure bends light arriving from a more distant source. The background object can appear stretched into an arc, spread into a ring or repeated in several images. The arrangement depends on the mass along the light’s path and the positions of the objects involved.

The foreground mass includes contributions from stars, dark matter and central black holes. Researchers can model how that combined mass produces the observed distortion. A suitable lens can therefore reveal information about the foreground structure while magnifying details of the background source. One patch of sky can carry evidence about objects at several distances.

Repeated images demand careful interpretation. Several bright shapes can be different views of a single background object. Other configurations involve multiple background galaxies at different distances. Establishing which arrangement produced the image is part of extracting its scientific meaning. The appearance supplies clues; the model connects those clues to a physical explanation.

Some lensed quasars offer another possibility. Their brightness varies, and the light forming their repeated images can travel along paths with different travel times. Comparing when the variations arrive, together with a model of the lens, can help researchers study cosmic expansion. This is a potential use of suitable systems. Its value depends on measurements specific to each case.

ESA says machine learning and thousands of participants in the Space Warps project on Zooniverse helped assemble the approximately 15,000 lens candidates. The catalogue reflects a combination of computational searching and human inspection. ESA describes it as larger than previous catalogues combined; that ranking remains the agency’s assessment. The entries retain their candidate status as researchers investigate which systems support detailed study.

A large candidate collection opens questions that a handful of striking images leaves difficult to answer. How common are particular lens arrangements? How does the distribution of mass vary among foreground galaxies? Do patterns persist when researchers compare systems at different distances? With enough well-characterized examples, astronomers can examine a population and assess how representative an individual case may be.

The search process itself enters those questions. A bright, broad arc may be easier to recognize than a faint, compact distortion. The observed collection therefore reflects both the population in the sky and the method used to find it. To estimate how common lenses really are, researchers need to establish the kinds of systems their search detects readily and those it tends to overlook.

Using one telescope and a consistent search can make comparisons more manageable. Shared observing characteristics help researchers understand why one system appears different from another. The remaining task is to measure how those characteristics influence detection. A consistent method can carry a consistent preference, and accounting for that preference helps turn a candidate list into evidence about the wider population.

The same reasoning applies across Euclid’s survey. Larger samples give scientists more opportunities to measure recurring patterns and examine rare objects. They also make shared measurement errors especially important. For example, a small error in how brightness is calibrated can influence which sources enter a selected sample. Calibration—the work of tying an instrument’s recorded signals to reliable measurements—helps establish what comparisons across the survey mean.

ESA’s northern mosaic makes the scale visible in another way. The agency describes an image of nearly 500 gigapixels, assembled from 740 observations made in autumn 2024 and spring 2025. A gigapixel is a billion picture elements. This mosaic covers 367 square degrees, a subset of the wider release. Its pixel count describes the image’s scale, while the survey area describes its reach across the sky and the gigabyte figure describes stored data.

A mosaic rewards exploration one object at a time. A research catalogue lets scientists ask how thousands or millions of objects relate to one another. Shared access gives different teams the opportunity to select populations, compare methods and investigate competing explanations using a common observational resource. The scientific gain depends on those analyses establishing a clear connection between their choices and their conclusions.

ESA expects a fuller DR1 release in mid-2027, including weak-lensing observations and further work on galaxy clusters and the cosmic web. Weak lensing concerns subtle changes in background galaxy shapes caused by intervening mass. Researchers study those changes statistically across many galaxies. Combined with information about distances and galaxy distributions, such measurements can provide complementary ways to examine how matter gathered over time. The timing and contents of the fuller release remain plans.

For the November release, the published counts and descriptions come from ESA’s mission reporting. Their scientific interpretation will develop through catalogue validation, detailed methods and subsequent analyses. DR1-Foundation establishes the resource on which that work can proceed; the identity of dark matter and the nature of dark energy remain open research questions.

The suspense lies in the passage from visible abundance to dependable relationships. Each useful distance adds depth. Each confirmed lens connects a distorted image to the gravity that shaped it. Each well-understood selection method helps explain how the observed sample relates to the Universe beyond it. As those pieces come together, Euclid’s billion lights invite a precise question: will their arrangement across cosmic history agree with our models of how the Universe grew?
