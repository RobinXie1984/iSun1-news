# Europe’s 121 Million Carbon Allowances Carry a Question That Money Alone Cannot Settle

The European Union makes covered factories account for their carbon dioxide emissions through its emissions trading system, or ETS. Each allowance covers one tonne of CO₂ for compliance, giving emissions a financial cost. On November 12, 2026, negotiators representing EU governments and the European Parliament provisionally agreed on a roughly 121 million allowance cushion for certain industries over 2026–2030. The proposal addresses a difficult balance within European climate policy: helping businesses keep production in Europe while preserving a financial reason to produce with fewer emissions.

That balance begins with an ordinary business problem. A factory pays for energy, equipment, materials and workers. Under the ETS, its emissions also require allowances. The way those allowances reach the factory affects how much money it must spend acquiring them. Purchasing allowances creates a direct expense; receiving an eligible allocation free can ease that purchasing burden. Both routes supply the same kind of compliance instrument.

The European Commission describes auctioning as the default method of distributing allowances. Free allocation serves a particular policy purpose: addressing the risk of carbon leakage. This means production, together with its associated emissions, moving to a location with weaker climate rules. Europe could then lose economic activity while emissions continue elsewhere. Whether a business actually relocates depends on many considerations, including energy costs, access to customers and the expense of moving production. Carbon leakage is the risk this policy seeks to limit.

The question therefore reaches beyond a factory’s allowance bill. Governments want industrial activity, employment and investment to remain viable as climate requirements tighten. They also want businesses to find value in using cleaner energy and improving production. Free allocation sits inside that negotiation between immediate financial pressure and longer-term change. Its design determines who receives assistance, how much they receive and which obligations accompany it.

Understanding the November proposal requires a closer look at how that assistance is calculated. The Commission’s benchmark framework has 54 approaches: 52 for particular products and two fallback approaches, covering heat and fuel. A benchmark is a reference measure used to determine eligible free allocation. For product benchmarks, the Commission describes a reference based on the average performance of the best-performing 10% of installations making that product.

A shared reference gives the calculation an efficiency dimension. If comparable producers receive assistance according to a common yardstick, a producer with higher emissions can face a greater remaining allowance requirement. This is the underlying logic of using benchmarks: public support can reflect a standard of performance while each installation remains responsible for covering its emissions.

Production, however, encompasses many processes. Where a suitable product benchmark is unavailable, the heat and fuel fallback approaches use energy consumption in determining eligible allocation. Their role is to provide a measurement route for those activities. The best-performing-10% explanation belongs to the product framework; the heat and fuel calculations have their own rules.

According to the Council’s account of the November agreement, these two fallback approaches represent around 25% of total free allocation. That gives the proposal substantial reach within industrial assistance. The denominator matters: the quarter refers specifically to allowances distributed free. Using it to describe the share of Europe’s emissions, energy use or industrial output would answer a different question.

The relevant activities include chemicals, pulp and paper, food and beverage processing, ceramics and glass. Energy is a material input or operating cost across this varied landscape, although individual plants have different equipment, products and emissions profiles. A change in a common allocation method can therefore reach businesses whose production processes look quite different. Their shared exposure arises through the calculation rules.

The immediate policy sequence began with a regular benchmark update in June 2026 that raised concern about reduced free allocation. The European Commission proposed a response in July, and the Council of the EU adopted its negotiating position on September 16. The November 12 compromise followed between Council and Parliament negotiators. The Council represents member-state governments; Parliament represents elected lawmakers. The Commission proposed the measure and would develop the implementing calculations after adoption.

The reported cushion has three components: approximately 88 million allowances from an existing free-allocation pool, 28 million previously left unallocated because some installations had unmet ETS conditions, and five million reserved for targeted support. Together, the rounded figures total roughly 121 million across the 2026–2030 arrangement. The five million sits inside that total. The quantity describes allowances across the arrangement, with the eventual distribution depending on the applicable rules.

Earlier reporting can make those numbers appear more complicated. The September Council position described 88 million plus 33 million. The November account describes 88 million plus 28 million plus five million. Each presentation reaches approximately the same total. The change in the component descriptions is consequential because it distinguishes the targeted reserve, while the arithmetic keeps the package’s overall reported scale in view.

What does a free allowance actually change? An illustrative example helps separate two ideas that often become tangled.

Imagine a town with a limited number of transferable parking permits. It sells some and gives others to eligible businesses. A business receiving a permit free saves the expense of buying that permit. The town’s overall permit limit remains a separate decision. Distribution determines who receives the permits initially; the limit determines their total quantity.

Now imagine that the business can either use its permit or transfer it to someone else. Using it means giving up the transfer opportunity. The permit therefore has value even though the business originally received it free. Economists call the value of the forgone alternative an opportunity cost.

This illustration covers only distribution, scarcity and transfer value. ETS allowances govern emissions compliance across regulated activities and carry their own benchmarks, eligibility conditions and environmental consequences. Within that limited comparison, however, the distinction is useful. Free allocation can relieve an installation’s purchasing burden while the allowance retains economic value.

Applied to the ETS, this reasoning suggests why a business may still benefit from reducing emissions after receiving free allowances. Lower emissions can reduce the number of allowances it needs for compliance, potentially freeing valuable allowances for another permitted use. The strength of that incentive depends on the rules, market conditions and company decisions. This is an explanation of the economic mechanism; the proposal’s actual effect on industrial behaviour remains a future measurement.

The same distinction helps interpret the headline quantity. One allowance’s one-tonne compliance unit tells us how emissions are accounted for. The 121 million figure describes the proposed allocation arrangement. Actual emissions depend on subsequent activity, and the systemwide cap concerns the overall quantity of allowances. Drawing an emissions forecast from the package total would require additional evidence about those separate relationships.

For a company, immediate purchasing needs and longer-term incentives can matter at the same time. An investment that cuts energy use may require substantial spending before its savings arrive. Relief on allowance purchases could improve the financial room available for such spending. A company could also direct that room toward other business needs. Establishing which response occurs requires evidence about investment decisions and operating results.

This is where the origin of the 28 million allowances becomes especially important. The Council says they had remained unallocated because some installations had unmet conditions concerning energy-efficiency audits or climate-neutrality plans. That history places eligibility at the centre of the next stage. The adopted legislation and implementing rules will determine access to this pool and the safeguards attached to it.

An efficiency audit can help identify where energy is being used and where improvements may be possible. A climate-neutrality plan sets out an intended route toward a different emissions position. Such requirements connect financial assistance with a process of examining or planning change. Their practical force depends on what companies must submit, how compliance is assessed and what follows from that assessment.

Consequently, the political meaning of the 28 million extends beyond its share of the total. A pool that accumulated through unmet conditions raises a concrete question about the obligations future recipients will face. The provisional announcement establishes the pool’s origin. Detailed eligibility remains an implementation question. Keeping those two statements together preserves both the accountability issue and the present legal status.

The five-million component has a narrower purpose: support for highly exposed, energy-intensive sectors. Under the agreement, unused allowances within that targeted measure would support investment through the Modernisation Fund and innovation through the Innovation Fund. The destination applies specifically to the unused portion of this targeted reserve, with the split between the funds still unspecified in the Council’s account.

That arrangement presents two possible routes for this part of the package. Allowances used for targeted assistance would help qualifying industrial recipients. An unused portion would support the named investment and innovation channels. The eventual balance depends on how the targeted measure operates and how much of it is taken up. At present, both routes describe the proposed design; transfers and resulting projects belong to later implementation.

For governments, predictability is another part of the argument. Irish climate, energy and environment minister Darragh O’Brien presents the case for giving industry greater certainty and retaining production. These are policy aims. A business considering equipment that will operate for years has reason to care about how its future allowance needs will be calculated. Clear rules can help it compare the costs of continuing with an existing process and investing in a cleaner one.

Predictability still leaves a substantive choice about what those rules encourage. A stable allocation arrangement can make planning easier, while its benchmarks and eligibility requirements shape the choices within that plan. The central analytical issue is therefore how the assistance interacts with the company’s reasons to improve performance. The package’s size provides one part of that story; its conditions provide another.

The agreement remains provisional. Institutional endorsement, legal and linguistic revision, and formal adoption are still required, followed by Commission implementing calculation rules. The stated first-quarter 2027 completion target is a target. Actual delivery will depend on the legislative and administrative sequence. Businesses can use the compromise as a signal of policy direction, while precise entitlements depend on the completed rules.

Several distinct results will eventually matter. Allocation records can establish which installations received allowances and in what quantities. Investment evidence can show whether businesses changed their equipment or processes. Production and employment figures can illuminate industrial activity, while emissions data can show environmental performance. These measures answer different questions, and their timing may differ.

Even then, interpretation requires care. Emissions can fall because a process becomes cleaner or because output declines. Production can rise alongside changes in energy prices or demand. A credible assessment of the cushion’s effect would consider those influences and compare relevant businesses or periods. A useful result would connect the assistance to both industrial decisions and emissions performance, with the uncertainty made visible.

For now, Europe has a provisional agreement about the distribution of a valuable compliance instrument. Its reported scale is clear enough to frame the debate, and its components reveal where the difficult decisions sit: ordinary allocation, a pool arising from unmet conditions, and a smaller targeted reserve.

The next chapter will be written in eligibility rules, factory decisions and measured results. Can this allowance cushion give exposed industries room to remain competitive while making cleaner production more attractive? The November agreement has put that question into a concrete form. The answer will emerge as the proposed assistance reaches the businesses it is intended to help.
