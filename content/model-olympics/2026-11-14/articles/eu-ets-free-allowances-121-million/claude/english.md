# A Provisional Deal Would Give Europe's Factories 121 Million Free Carbon Permits. Who Will Carry the Carbon Bill?

On November 12, 2026, negotiators for two European Union lawmaking bodies reached a provisional agreement on how many free carbon allowances certain energy-hungry factories can receive between 2026 and 2030. One body is the Council of the EU, where the governments of the member states sit. The other is the European Parliament, whose members are elected by voters across the bloc. The deal covers roughly 121 million allowances. It concerns factories whose free share is calculated with two special measuring rules, one for heat and one for fuel. A third body, the European Commission, proposed the measure in the summer and would later write the detailed calculation rules. The agreement still needs approval from both institutions, a legal and language review, and formal adoption. It sits inside a larger European project: making polluters pay for carbon dioxide while keeping factories, and the jobs they support, inside Europe.

To understand why a few lines about factory permits drew months of negotiation, it helps to start with the machine these permits belong to.

## A market built on a fixed supply of permission

The EU emissions trading system is Europe's main tool for putting a price on greenhouse gases from large emitters such as power stations and heavy industry. Its basic rule is simple. Each covered emitter must hand over allowances to match its covered emissions. One allowance accounts for one tonne of carbon dioxide, or the equivalent amount of another greenhouse gas. At the end of each compliance period, a company tallies its emissions and surrenders that many allowances. An allowance works like a ticket that settles an account. It is a compliance document. It is separate from cash, and separate from the carbon credits people buy when they pay for tree planting.

The system limits the total number of allowances in circulation. That limit is called the cap. Because the supply is limited and companies can trade allowances with one another, each allowance carries a market price. A company that cuts its emissions needs fewer allowances and can sell its spares. A company that pollutes more must buy extra. The price is the signal meant to push investment toward cleaner equipment.

The Commission describes auctioning as the default way allowances enter the system. Companies bid for allowances, and selling them this way raises public revenue. Some industrial installations, though, receive part of their allowances free. The stated reason is a risk called carbon leakage. The phrase describes something economic. It refers to the danger that a company facing high carbon costs in Europe moves production to a country with weaker climate rules. The goods still get made, the emissions still happen, and Europe loses the jobs and the factory. Free allocation is designed to soften that pressure for exposed sectors while the carbon price still applies across the system.

An everyday comparison helps here, and it is purely illustrative. Picture a city that issues a fixed number of parking permits each year. It can auction them to the highest bidders, or it can hand some to qualifying drivers at no upfront charge. The second choice changes who pays first. The number of permits printed stays a separate decision, set by the city's overall limit. The comparison ends there. Real emissions allowances are traded on a market, tied to measured tonnes of gas, and handed out through formulas far more detailed than any parking office uses. The useful lesson is the split between two questions: how many permits exist, and who receives them first.

That split matters for reading this deal. The November agreement concerns the second question, the initial distribution of allowances to certain factories. What the provisional agreement establishes is the size and make-up of a proposed allocation package, counted in allowances. Its wider consequences belong to later evidence, described toward the end of this article.

## How Europe decides who gets a free share

Handing allowances to factories at no charge raises an obvious fairness problem. Pay each plant for whatever it emits, and the dirtiest plants collect the most. The EU addresses this with benchmarks, which are yardsticks of good emissions performance.

According to the Commission, the system uses 54 benchmark approaches. Fifty-two relate to specific products, such as a tonne of a particular material. For a product benchmark, the Commission explains, the yardstick reflects the average performance of the best 10 percent of installations making that product. A plant that matches those leaders can receive close to its full need in free allowances. A plant that emits more per tonne of product receives the same benchmark-based amount and must cover the gap itself, by buying allowances or by cleaning up. The benchmark rewards efficiency built into the formula.

The remaining two approaches are the heart of this story. They are the heat benchmark and the fuel benchmark, together called fallback approaches. The word fallback can make a reader imagine an emergency fuel tank. Here it means a second-line measuring rule. Many factory activities fall outside the 52 product benchmarks, because their outputs are too varied or specialised for a single product yardstick. For those activities, allocation is calculated from energy use: the heat the plant consumes, or the fuel it burns. The fallback is a way of counting.

These two counting rules carry real weight. The Council says the heat and fuel benchmarks account for around 25 percent of total free allocation. That share describes the slice of free allowances handed out through these two methods. It is a separate figure from the share of Europe's emissions, energy use or industrial output.

The industries that lean on these methods reach well beyond the steelworks and cement kilns that usually stand for heavy industry. The Council names chemicals, pulp and paper, food and beverage processing, ceramics and glass, along with others. These are energy-intensive businesses, meaning energy forms a large part of what they use and what they spend. A brewery's boilers, a paper mill's drying rollers and a glassworks' furnaces all draw heavily on heat and fuel.

## A routine update that set off alarms

The trigger for the negotiation was ordinary housekeeping. Benchmarks are updated periodically so they keep pace with improving technology. In June 2026, a regular update set the benchmark values for 2026 to 2030. According to the Council, that update raised concern that free allocation under the heat and fuel methods would fall. As the best performers improve, the yardstick tightens, and plants measured against it receive fewer free allowances.

For energy-intensive producers, a smaller free share means more allowances to buy, which adds to costs. Their argument, which the Council echoes, is that higher costs in Europe make production harder to keep when competitors elsewhere face looser climate rules. That is the carbon leakage concern applied to this specific update.

The Commission proposed a response in July. On September 16, the Council agreed its negotiating position. Its account at that stage paired about 88 million allowances with a further 33 million. The same September release carried a Commission estimate of about €6 billion, which applied only to the 88 million component under an earlier version of the proposal. That figure belongs to that earlier stage and describes one part of an older plan, so it offers a poor guide to the value of the final package. Allowance prices move, and the Council's November account counts the package in allowances. Putting a value on the 121 million would require a stated allowance price, a time frame and a clear method.

Then came the bargaining between Council and Parliament negotiators, which produced the November 12 compromise.

## What 121 million means, and how it divides

The Council describes the agreed cushion as roughly 121 million free allowances. That number covers the whole package for the 2026 to 2030 period. It is a single total for the arrangement, and it counts allowances in the proposed package, each worth one tonne of covered emissions for compliance. It counts tonnes of permission.

The total has three parts.

About 88 million comes from the existing pool of free allocation.

About 28 million consists of allowances that had previously gone unallocated. The Council explains why. Free allocation can come with requirements concerning energy-efficiency audits or climate-neutrality plans, which are roadmaps for reaching net-zero emissions. According to the Council, unmet conditions of this kind left these 28 million allowances unallocated. The detailed rules on which requirements apply to which installations remain open.

About 5 million forms a targeted set-aside for highly exposed, energy-intensive sectors during 2026 to 2030.

Add them up and the result is roughly 121 million. The five million sits inside that total. Comparing September with November reveals the shape of the compromise. The earlier 33 million became 28 million plus a separate targeted set-aside of five million. The headline total stayed at about 121 million. The later version gives five million of it a distinct purpose.

That distinct purpose comes with its own exit route. The Council says that if allowances under the targeted five-million measure go unused, they would support investment through the Modernisation Fund and innovation through the Innovation Fund. Both are existing EU funds financed through the emissions trading system. The Modernisation Fund helps lower-income member states upgrade energy systems. The Innovation Fund backs new low-carbon technologies. This route applies to the targeted measure specifically. The size of any unused amount, its division between the two funds and the projects it might finance will become clear only as the measure operates.

## The 28 million question

The most politically sensitive slice is the 28 million. These allowances had gone unallocated because conditions concerning audits or climate-neutrality plans went unmet. Moving them into a cushion for industry invites a fair question: on what terms can they now reach factories, and which factories qualify?

The adopted legal text and the Commission's calculation rules will determine eligibility and the treatment of the audit and planning conditions. The information available today describes where the allowances came from. Whether those conditions continue to apply in full, how recipients are chosen and how the 28 million is divided remain open questions. The account discusses installations as a group. The responsible reading is to keep the origin of these allowances in view and to read the final terms closely when they appear.

## What a free permit really changes

This is where the economics becomes interesting, and the following is analysis based on how the system works.

A factory that receives a free allowance avoids buying one upfront. For a business facing a sudden cost increase, that eases its cash position and its bookkeeping. This is the competitiveness case in its simplest form.

Yet a free allowance keeps its value. Because allowances are scarce and tradable, a factory holding one has a choice. It can surrender the allowance to cover a tonne of its own emissions, or it can cut that tonne and sell the allowance to someone else. Economists call the value of the option given up an opportunity cost. A plant that keeps polluting gives up the sale. A plant that cleans up gains something to sell. In principle, then, the reward for cutting emissions survives free allocation, because the allowance itself remains worth money.

Both observations hold at once. The free allowance eases the immediate cost, and it still carries a price signal. How strongly each force acts depends on the market price of allowances over the period, on how much free allocation each plant finally receives compared with its emissions, and on choices made inside each company. Benchmarks add another layer. Because allocation follows a yardstick, an inefficient plant still faces a shortfall, while an efficient one may have spares. The design aims to protect production and reward improvement in the same stroke. Whether it achieves both is an empirical question.

The government side of the argument rests on predictability. In the Council's announcement, Irish climate, energy and environment minister Darragh O'Brien presented the deal as giving industry certainty and helping keep production in Europe. Those are the stated aims of the governments that negotiated it.

## The road from handshake to allocation

The agreement is provisional. The Council's representatives and the Parliament must each endorse it. Lawyers and linguists then revise the text so that it reads consistently in every official EU language. The institutions then adopt it formally. After that, the Commission sets the implementing rules that turn the law into numbers for each installation. A planning target places completion of the file in the first quarter of 2027. That is a target date for the legislative work. It is separate from any date when allowances reach factories.

Today's agreement establishes a proposed package: its size, its three parts, its period and the industries it targets. Later evidence can show the rest. The adopted text and the Commission's rules will settle eligibility for the 28 million and how the heat and fuel formulas apply to each plant. Installation-level allocation tables will show which factories receive how many allowances. Over the following years, auction records, production figures, investment decisions and verified emissions data can show how the measure touches public revenue, industry and the climate, and whether the governments' stated aims of steady production and retained jobs are met. Even then, separating the effect of this one measure from energy prices, global demand and other climate rules will require careful comparison with what would plausibly have happened otherwise.

For now, the November 12 deal reveals a choice Europe keeps returning to as its carbon price bites. The cap sets how much carbon the system permits. The allocation rules decide who pays first for each tonne. By proposing to steer roughly 121 million allowances toward heat- and fuel-dependent industries, negotiators have agreed to give brewers, papermakers, glassmakers and chemical plants a larger free share of the initial distribution. The question waiting in the final rules and the factory data is whether a permit handed over at no charge keeps those furnaces in Europe while still giving their owners a reason to burn less.
