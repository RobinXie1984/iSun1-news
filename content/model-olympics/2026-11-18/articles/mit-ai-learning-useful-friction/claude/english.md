# When the Machine Does the Hard Part, Who Is Still Learning?

On November 17, 2026, MIT News published a report by Danna Lorch on a set of research projects at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The projects study what artificial intelligence does to people: how students learn, how minds work, how musicians create together and how people relate to machines that talk back. The researchers come from distant corners of the university, including media studies, brain science, music technology, anthropology and computer science. An MIT program called the Human Insight Collaborative supports much of this work. One question runs through all of it, and it applies to every student who now has an AI assistant a click away. When a tool makes a task easier, which part of the effort has it usefully removed, and which part was the learning itself?

That question sounds simple, and it hides a puzzle. AI chat tools are programs that answer typed questions in fluent sentences, solve math problems, draft essays and write computer code. They arrived in schools and universities quickly. For a student facing a stack of homework, they offer speed. Picture, as a hypothetical example, a problem that might take a student an evening to work through: an AI tool can produce an answer to it in moments. Producing that answer and completing the learning are two separate matters.

The puzzle comes from the two purposes of schoolwork. One purpose is the finished product, the answer turned in on time. The other is the change the work produces inside the student: a new skill, sharper judgment, a better eye for mistakes. A fast tool clearly helps with the first purpose. Its effect on the second depends on which steps the tool takes over.

## A worry from the classroom

MIT's account approaches this worry through an earlier MIT report on AI and education. As the November account summarizes it, that report described three changes among students. They attended office hours less often. They spent less time on problem sets. They spent less time working with classmates.

Office hours are scheduled times when students sit down with a teacher or teaching assistant and work through questions they are stuck on. Problem sets are assigned collections of practice problems, the everyday diet of courses in science, math and engineering.

The account presents these changes as observations and concerns. It reports their direction, toward less of each activity. It leaves their size and their cause for further study. That boundary matters, because students change their habits for many reasons, including schedules, course design and the spread of online resources. What the account offers is a pattern worth investigating, with the explanation still open.

Even so, the three activities share something important. In each one, learning often happens through effort. At office hours, a student has to put a confusion into words, and describing a confusion clearly often reveals half of the answer. A problem set asks a student to choose a method, try it, notice when it fails and try again. Working with classmates pushes a student to defend a step, hear a different approach and revise. All three involve friction: the small resistance a learner feels when the next move is hard to see.

What follows is this article's own analysis, which links MIT's projects together; it is separate from any MIT finding. Friction comes in at least two kinds.

Some friction is simply wasted time. Examples include retyping a table, hunting for a formatting rule, or repeating the same arithmetic forty times after the method is already clear. Removing that kind of effort can free hours for harder thinking.

Other friction is the training itself. This includes deciding which approach to try, discovering why an answer is wrong, explaining a choice to someone else and rewriting after criticism. Removing that kind of effort can leave a student holding a correct answer and a weaker grasp of how to reach one.

An everyday comparison, offered purely as an illustration, makes the mechanism easier to see. A calculator spares a learner endless long division, and in that role it is a gift. Now imagine a child who uses a calculator from the first day of learning numbers. Later, the child types 12 times 11, a finger slips, and the screen shows 1,320. The child accepts it, because the child has little feel for how big the answer should be. The calculator did its arithmetic correctly every time. What the child missed was the chance to build the number sense that catches errors. So the useful question about a calculator concerns timing and checking: when to bring it in, and what understanding to confirm afterward. AI differs from a calculator in many ways, since it writes, explains and argues. The timing question still carries over.

Seen this way, finishing an assignment, understanding a method, explaining a choice and revising after feedback are four different achievements. An AI tool might handle the tedious parts of the first while leaving room for the other three. It might also perform the exact step a student needed to attempt. Which of these happens in a given classroom is still an open question, and that openness is why researchers are paying attention.

## Where the thinking goes

MIT launched the MIT Human Insight Collaborative, known as MITHIC, in 2024. According to the November account, the initiative has since supported 67 projects across 31 MIT units, with US$5.5 million in total funding. Those totals cover the whole initiative, which spans many human questions. Roughly one third of its projects focus on AI, society and humanity.

The figures describe breadth: how many teams received support, and how many departments, labs and programs took part. They measure effort and investment. The results of that effort belong to a later chapter.

The breadth tells part of the story. A question about homework turns out to reach into the brain, into creativity and into trust. Bringing professors from distant fields into the same conversation lets each field contribute its own way of looking.

Eric Klopfer, a professor in Comparative Media Studies/Writing, and Pawan Sinha, a professor of brain and cognitive sciences, co-lead ongoing research on AI and cognition. Cognition means the mental work of thinking: remembering, reasoning, paying attention and solving problems.

Their research asks two questions that point in opposite directions. The first is whether taking in ever more information, much of it delivered ready-made by machines, can shift mental work away from people. Researchers call that shift offloading. The second is whether creative use of AI can support stronger mental function. The account also notes that experts gathered on campus in October 2026 for an event on AI and the brain.

Offloading is an old human habit. People write grocery lists so their memories can relax, and they use maps so they can stop memorizing streets. Much offloading is healthy, because it frees the mind for other work. The research asks two things about AI. Does some kind of use move the very thinking a person needed to practice? And does another kind of use spark thinking that would otherwise stay dormant? Both possibilities remain live. MIT's account describes these as questions under study, and the answers await the research.

What kind of evidence could tell the two possibilities apart? This article can suggest four comparisons a study might make. They are research design ideas, separate from any tests MIT has reported.

- **Independent performance:** after weeks of AI-assisted practice, how well does a student solve a fresh problem alone?
- **Explanation:** can the student explain a solution in their own words? This tests understanding more directly than a correct final answer does.
- **Revision:** when a teacher or classmate points out a flaw, can the student find it and repair it?
- **Collaboration:** do students still talk ideas through with peers, and does the quality of that talk change?

Taken together, results like these could help researchers distinguish whether a tool built skill or mainly produced answers. Linking any change in skill to the tool itself would call for careful study design as well.

## Music, manners and the human part

The music project makes the abstract question easier to see and hear. Anna Huang, a professor of music technology, and Joe Paradiso, a professor at the MIT Media Lab, study improvisation between humans and AI. Improvisation is music made up on the spot: players listen to one another and decide in real time where the piece goes next.

According to the account, a collective of musicians, ethnomusicologists and engineers meets regularly to test frameworks for playing with AI. Ethnomusicologists are scholars who study music within its cultural setting. During their sessions, the group pauses to discuss three things: authorship, the musical direction of a piece, and the dynamics of the ensemble, meaning how its members work together. A future course or practicum, a hands-on class, is an intended outcome of the work.

Why does that matter to a student with a homework assistant? In an ensemble, contributions are audible. When a melody turns in a new direction, the players can stop and ask who started the turn, who followed and whether the group chose it together. Those pauses keep human judgment on display.

In this article's reading, the practice offers a model of attention. It is a way to keep asking, while working with a machine, which ideas came from whom and who decided what happened next. A student could ask parallel questions about homework. Which step did I choose? Which step did the tool choose? Could I defend the result if a teacher asked? The account reports the group's habit of pausing to discuss. The classroom lessons drawn from that habit are interpretation.

A final project looks at the manner in which AI speaks. Graham Jones, a professor of anthropology, and Arvind Satyanarayan, a professor of computer science, helped launch a course called Humane User Experience Design. User experience design is the craft of shaping how people interact with software: what a screen shows, how a system responds and what it feels like to use. The course explores AI systems and human relationships.

Jones distinguishes between design that serves people and design that imitates people. He warns that humanlike behavior can encourage dependence, even when a system seems attentive and responsive.

That warning connects back to the classroom. An AI tutor that answers warmly, praises effort and stays patient at any hour can feel like the ideal study partner. Its friendliness, though, reveals little about what the student is learning. A patient tone could come with help that builds skill. It could also come with help that quietly takes over the work. Jones's concern, as the account reports it, centers on dependence that humanlike design can encourage. Connecting that concern to a tutor taking over a student's work is this article's interpretation. The account presents the concern as a design question to examine, and the scale of any dependence remains to be measured.

Placed side by side, the projects bring a single question into focus. Office hours, problem sets, study groups, improvised music and conversations with friendly software all involve people doing effortful things, alone or together. AI can lift some of that effort. In each setting, what matters is which effort it lifts.

MIT's account describes questions being asked, projects underway and courses being built. The measured answers lie in research still ahead.

Meanwhile, the analysis in this article suggests a way to frame any single use of AI help. Start with the ability the activity exists to build: choosing a method, catching an error, explaining a choice, working through a disagreement with a classmate. Judged by that purpose, help that clears away the tedium around those abilities looks valuable. Help that performs them on the student's behalf looks costly.

Which kind of help is happening in a particular classroom, on a particular night, is the question MIT's researchers have set out to study. Every student who opens an AI tool before starting a problem is answering a small version of it. How many of those students know which answer they are giving?
