# How Much Materials Data Can an AI Model Lose Before It Loses the Plot?

On October 19, 2026, the journal *npj Computational Materials* published a study by seven researchers from Michigan Technological University and the University of California, Los Angeles. Its central result sounds backward. The team built machine-learning programs to predict how electrons behave inside materials. Before training those programs, the researchers removed most of the examples from their training sets, and models trained on the much smaller sets largely held up. Susanta Ghosh, an associate professor of mechanical and aerospace engineering at Michigan Tech, worked with Amartya Banerjee's group at UCLA and with co-authors Hossain, Thiagarajan, Pathrudkar, Taylor and Gangan. Michigan Tech described the work in a news report on November 16. The study belongs to a busy corner of science where computers help researchers hunt for better batteries, tougher alloys and cleaner chemical processes. It raises a question that matters across that whole corner: how much of the costly data feeding these programs teaches them something new?

To see why that question carries weight, start with the electrons. Every solid, liquid and gas is built from atoms, and each atom carries a cloud of electrons around its core. When atoms sit close together, their electrons spread out, share space and settle into patterns. Scientists call those patterns the material's electronic structure: where the electrons tend to gather and how much energy they hold. Electronic structure sits behind many properties people care about. It helps explain why copper carries electricity well, why some metals bend while others snap, and how water molecules tug on one another. A researcher who can predict electronic structure for a new arrangement of atoms gains an early look at how that material might behave.

The standard tool for that prediction is a family of calculations called Kohn–Sham density functional theory, usually shortened to DFT. In everyday terms, DFT is a recipe that lets a computer estimate the electron cloud and the energy of a group of atoms by working through the physics step by step. It runs on ordinary computers, often very large ones, and its results are computed estimates grounded in quantum physics. The study's authors describe these calculations as costly, and the cost climbs as researchers ask about bigger groups of atoms or many different arrangements.

Machine learning enters as a shortcut. A researcher runs many DFT calculations, gathers the results, and hands them to a machine-learning model as worked examples. The model studies how each arrangement of atoms pairs with its calculated electronic structure, and it gradually builds its own internal rule of thumb. Once trained, the model can offer a quick prediction for a fresh arrangement. That quickness lets researchers screen far more candidate materials than DFT alone could handle.

This setup has bred a familiar instinct. If examples teach the model, more examples should teach it more. Labs assemble large collections of calculations, and each new calculation feels like an investment in a better model. The Michigan Tech and UCLA team set out to measure how much of that investment carries fresh information and how much repeats lessons the model has already absorbed.

## Shrinking the lesson plan

The experiments worked on the training data, the set of examples a model studies before anyone asks it a question. Before training began, the researchers removed large portions of each training set. They trained models on what remained and compared the results with models trained on fuller collections. The change happened at the very start, in the choice of lessons. Each model then learned from its smaller set in the ordinary way. Making an already trained model forget something, or trimming parts of the model itself, lies outside this study.

The examples spanned a wide spread of substances. Michigan Tech's report lists metals, water and complex alloys, which are blends of several metals mixed to tune qualities such as strength or resistance to heat. That range matters. A trick that works on a single material could be a quirk of that material. A similar pattern across such different systems makes the finding more intriguing.

The results arrive in three layers, from strongest to most carefully hedged.

According to Michigan Tech, removing 90 percent of the training data produced virtually no loss of prediction accuracy in the settings the team studied. Put plainly, in the team's reported comparisons, models trained on the remaining tenth of a training set performed about as well as models trained on the fuller set. That result belongs to the specific datasets and selections the researchers tested.

The second figure is bolder and carries an important qualifier. When the team removed 99 percent of the training data, keeping one example in a hundred, most predictions remained highly accurate, the university reports. The word "most" does real work in that sentence: it marks a qualified majority, and the university's summary leaves the performance of the remaining predictions uncharacterized. The 99 percent figure measures training examples removed; the reported outcome is that most predictions remained highly accurate.

The paper's own summary, its abstract, frames the result a third way. It describes a selection method that worked with up to two orders of magnitude less data. An order of magnitude is a factor of ten, so two orders means a factor of a hundred. "Up to" marks the best end of a range. The university's account and the abstract point in the same direction.

Then comes speed. Michigan Tech reports that model training ran at least three times faster, and the abstract gives a factor of three or more. That fits common sense: a model with fewer examples to study finishes studying sooner. The stopwatch in that comparison measured model training, and only model training. The DFT calculations that produced the original data still had to run. Choosing which examples to keep takes its own effort. Laboratory testing and manufacturing lie further down the road. The report and the abstract leave savings in money, energy, carbon and overall discovery time unquantified.

## Random cuts and careful choices

How the researchers chose which examples to drop turns out to matter, and the abstract describes two approaches.

The first is random pruning: deleting examples by chance, like drawing names from a hat. The abstract reports that random pruning alone could shrink datasets substantially with little loss of predictive power. That finding is revealing in itself. If cutting at random works well, the original collections must have held a great deal of repetition.

The second approach is more deliberate. The authors considered the learning difficulty of examples, a measure of how easy or hard each one is for a model to master. Their coverage-based strategy samples across differing levels of that difficulty. According to the abstract, this method almost always preserved two benchmarks. The first was chemical accuracy, an energy-error benchmark the authors use to judge prediction quality. The second was generalizability, meaning strong performance on examples held back from training. The method preserved both with dramatically less data.

Michigan Tech's report emphasizes carefully chosen, representative samples. The abstract also credits random cuts. Both points belong in the story. Random pruning is reported to shrink datasets substantially with little loss of predictive power; coverage-based selection is reported to almost always preserve the two stated benchmarks. Repetition in this data appears widespread enough for random cuts to help, and the coverage approach aims to retain variety by drawing examples across levels of learning difficulty.

## Why a short list might teach the whole lesson

**An everyday analogy, offered as illustration:** picture a trainee librarian learning to sort books by subject in an enormous library. Popular subjects fill whole aisles with near-identical beginner guides. Reading the fortieth introduction to bird-watching teaches little that the first few did. A single odd volume from a rarely visited shelf, perhaps a history of medieval shipbuilding, could reveal a gap in the trainee's understanding. A short reading list that samples every shelf, including the strange ones, can teach the sorting rule almost as well as reading the entire collection. In this picture, random pruning resembles grabbing books blindly off the returns cart. That works well when the popular aisles dominate, and it leaves the rare shelves to chance. A coverage-based list resembles a reading plan that deliberately mixes easy reads with demanding ones.

The analogy also marks the limit of the approach. The trainee proves the skill only by sorting books left off the reading list. When the library later acquires an entirely new subject, that shelf poses a fresh test. In the study, the matching step is checking predictions on examples set aside from training. That check covers the kinds of materials tested. Brand-new chemistries remain their own test.

The authors offer a deeper explanation for why so much repetition exists, built on an idea called low intrinsic dimensionality. Each arrangement of atoms can be described with a long list of numbers. In library terms, every book has a catalogue card with dozens of entries: page count, binding, publisher, year, cover color. The authors propose that the meaningful variation in electronic-structure data follows a much simpler pattern hidden inside all those numbers. They suggest it may even trace a curved path through them. A book's subject, likewise, depends on a few underlying traits buried among the dozens of catalogue details. Mathematicians call the count of truly independent directions in which data varies its intrinsic dimensionality. When that count is low, many examples crowd close together along the simple pattern, and a modest sample can trace its shape.

The authors link this geometry to a long-standing idea in physics sometimes called nearsightedness: certain electronic effects act over short distances, shaped mainly by the nearby atomic environment. In the authors' proposed account, that short reach helps explain the redundancy. On this view, a large chunk of material can be treated as many small local neighborhoods, and the same kinds of neighborhoods recur again and again. A dataset of many arrangements would then record many copies of familiar neighborhoods, an argument consistent with the redundancy the team measured.

This explanation has a clear status. It is the authors' proposed account of their observations, consistent with what they measured. It describes the shape of the data. The electrons keep their full physics; the simplicity lives in how the examples cluster. Its reach across other materials is a question for future studies.

## What faster training could change

**What follows is analysis of possible consequences, separate from measured results.** Building a useful machine-learning model usually takes many attempts. Researchers train a model, test it, adjust its design, and train again. If each round of training runs three or more times faster, a team could compare more designs in the same week, or retrain quickly as fresh calculations arrive. Smaller research groups with modest computing budgets might find this kind of work easier to join. Those are plausible gains in the rhythm of computational research. They describe a possibility and await direct measurement.

A second possibility concerns planning. If researchers could judge in advance which new calculation would teach a model something fresh, they might steer expensive DFT time toward informative cases and spend less on repeats. That idea goes beyond what the study showed. In the reported work, the team trimmed training sets drawn from existing collections, and those collections had already been calculated in full. Turning the finding into savings on new calculations would require a reliable way to spot an informative example before paying to compute it.

The tested range sets the remaining boundaries. Metals, water and complex alloys make a broad sample, and they still leave the vast catalogue of possible materials open. Unfamiliar chemistries, extreme temperatures or pressures, and other material properties each call for their own checks. Several distinct kinds of evidence also apply at different stages. A prediction that matches held-out calculations shows the model learned the calculations well. A material behaving as predicted in a laboratory is a separate confirmation. A material produced reliably at scale is a further step again.

The paper itself has a clear standing. The publisher lists it as peer-reviewed accepted research with a permanent identifier, subject to further editing before its final version of record. This account follows Michigan Tech's report together with the paper's abstract and publication details. Several details await checking against the paper's full methods, tables, data and code: the original dataset sizes, the error values behind the university's summaries, the threshold used for chemical accuracy, whether the 99 percent statement and the abstract's hundredfold figure describe the same tests, how coverage-based selection compared with random pruning case by case, and the timing behind the speed result.

What the study offers, then, is a well-supported surprise inside a defined setting. Electronic-structure datasets for the materials tested appear to repeat themselves heavily. Models trained on a small fraction of them performed strongly, and they trained faster. The authors have proposed a geometric reason for that repetition, linked to the short reach of certain electronic effects. The puzzle it leaves behind is the one every careful learner eventually faces. For each new material or condition a researcher wants to explore, how does anyone recognize the moment when a smaller set of examples has quietly dropped the one rare case that mattered?
