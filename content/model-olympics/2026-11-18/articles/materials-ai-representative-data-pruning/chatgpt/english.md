# When 99% of the Training Data Goes, What Must Stay?

Materials researchers use artificial intelligence to predict how electrons behave inside matter, a task that helps connect the arrangement of atoms to a material’s properties. A study published on October 19, 2026, and reported by Michigan Technological University on November 16 suggests that these models can sometimes learn from a surprisingly small share of their available examples. The collaboration includes Susanta Ghosh, a Michigan Tech associate professor of mechanical and aerospace engineering, and Amartya Banerjee’s team at UCLA. Their work brings a basic question into focus: how much useful variety does a large scientific dataset actually contain?

The bigger picture begins with the work involved in understanding a material. A change in atomic arrangement can change how electrons behave, influencing the material’s characteristics. Researchers use physics-based calculations to investigate that relationship. Machine learning offers another step: train a model on calculated examples, then use the patterns it learns to make predictions. The value of that model depends on how faithfully it carries those lessons into the cases researchers want to explore.

In the settings described by Michigan Tech, removing 90% of the training data left prediction accuracy virtually unchanged. With 99% removed, most predictions remained highly accurate. The university also reports model training at least three times faster. Those percentages describe the share of examples removed; accuracy is a separate measure. The word “most” matters especially at the larger reduction, where the report’s claim leaves room for predictions with weaker performance.

Consider the arithmetic through an illustrative collection of 100 examples. A 90% reduction leaves ten; a 99% reduction leaves one. These are proportions, and the study’s actual dataset sizes require the detailed results. Still, the proportions convey the scale of the finding. They raise a more interesting possibility than simply making a dataset smaller: perhaps many examples teach a model closely related lessons, while a much smaller collection carries much of the useful variation.

Electronic structure is the subject being learned. Broadly, it describes how electrons are distributed and behave within a material. Electrons help determine how atoms bond and how materials respond to their surroundings, so understanding them is an important foundation for materials research. Yet predicting their collective behaviour is demanding. A material contains interacting parts, and a useful calculation must represent those interactions through mathematical approximations that a computer can handle.

The paper’s data come from Kohn–Sham density-functional-theory calculations, a physics-based method for estimating electronic behaviour and energies. The authors propose that redundancy arises partly from low intrinsic dimensionality: many recorded features may reflect fewer underlying patterns, linked through potentially curved or complicated relationships. Michigan Tech describes examples spanning metals, water and complex alloys. Together, these details place the research within computational materials science, with a specific prediction task and several studied systems.

A training example in this setting pairs information describing a material with a calculated answer. During training, the model adjusts its internal numerical settings so that its predictions move closer to those answers. Repeating this process across examples helps it learn relationships. Researchers can then assess its predictions using examples reserved for testing. This separation gives them a way to examine whether the learned relationships carry over to fresh cases.

Two kinds of work therefore sit behind an apparently simple prediction. First comes producing the answers used for teaching, through physics calculations. Then comes fitting the predictive model to those answers. An existing calculation retains its historical cost even if its result is later left outside the training selection. The reported speed gain belongs to the second stage. That distinction helps explain both the immediate appeal of the research and the further evidence needed to assess its effect on a whole research programme.

An everyday analogy makes the selection problem easier to see. Imagine a student learning to identify the subjects of books from their contents. A library might hold many editions of similar cookbooks, alongside smaller collections on astronomy, gardening and history. Reading another closely related cookbook could reinforce a familiar lesson. Reading the first astronomy book could introduce an entirely different set of ideas. Both add a book to the student’s experience, while their contributions to learning differ.

In this illustration, a useful teaching collection would span the shelves and include books that are difficult to classify. A volume about growing food might connect gardening and cooking; a history of space exploration might connect history and astronomy. An exam using fresh books would show how well the student applies the lessons. The analogy illustrates diversity, difficult cases and testing. In materials data, the categories are subtler, and researchers must discover which differences matter for the prediction they are trying to make.

The paper’s abstract reports that even random pruning can substantially reduce the datasets with limited degradation in prediction. Its coverage-based strategy samples across levels of learning difficulty and almost always preserves chemical accuracy and generalizability with up to two orders of magnitude less data, alongside training speed factors of three or more. Two orders of magnitude means a hundredfold change. The upper-end qualifier and the acknowledged exceptions are part of the result.

Random selection has an understandable role here. If a collection contains many examples of similar situations, a randomly chosen portion may still include enough of those situations to teach the model. The more often a pattern appears, the more opportunities it has to survive a reduction. That offers an intuitive explanation for why random pruning could work well in some settings. A rare pattern has fewer such opportunities, making the composition of the retained set increasingly consequential as that set shrinks.

Coverage-based selection addresses the composition more deliberately. Learning difficulty provides a way to distinguish examples beyond their sheer number. In principle, a collection can include both familiar cases that establish common relationships and challenging cases that reveal where those relationships become harder to learn. The abstract describes the strategy at that level. Its practical strength depends on how the chosen examples represent the range of behaviour that a model will encounter.

“Generalizability” is the term for carrying learned relationships into further cases. Its practical meaning depends on the distance between those cases and the teaching examples. A test involving closely related atomic arrangements asks one question. A test involving substantially different chemistry or conditions asks a more demanding one. Both can be useful, provided readers know what territory the evaluation covers. The breadth of a dataset and the breadth of a successful test are related pieces of evidence, each with its own role.

The phrase “chemical accuracy” also needs care. It refers here to a technical standard for prediction error. Such a standard becomes informative when tied to a numerical threshold, a particular predicted quantity and a scientific purpose. An error small enough for one comparison may have greater consequences for another. This account follows the university report and the accepted paper’s abstract and metadata; the full methods, numerical tables, supplementary analyses, underlying data and code remain independently unverified, including the precise accuracy threshold and detailed performance of difficult cases.

The authors’ explanation of redundancy offers a useful way to think about these results. A dataset can record many features while containing fewer independent sources of variation. Some features may change together because they arise from shared physical relationships. Counting every recorded feature then gives a different impression from asking how many underlying patterns a model must learn. “Low intrinsic dimensionality” describes that second idea: the effective complexity of the relationships inside the data.

This is a statement about the organisation of information. A model may receive a long description of an atomic environment, yet parts of that description can carry overlapping clues. Nearby atoms help shape the environment experienced by an atom and its electrons, so related local arrangements may produce related information across examples. The authors’ geometric account is consistent with arguments about the importance of these local environments. Its explanatory reach remains tied to the systems and calculations examined.

There is a subtle point in the word “redundant.” An example can overlap strongly with others for a particular learning task and still have value for a different question. Researchers might later ask about another property, another range of conditions or another kind of model. The usefulness of a sample therefore belongs to a relationship among the data, the learner and the intended prediction. A smaller training selection changes what enters a particular learning process; preserving the wider scientific collection can support future investigations.

The intervention also happens at a specific moment. Researchers reduce the examples used before training. The resulting model then learns from the retained set. This differs in purpose and mechanism from changing a trained model’s internal size or editing knowledge after it has learned. The study’s practical proposition concerns how to prepare the teaching material. That gives researchers a potentially accessible place to seek efficiency, because the choice of examples already forms part of building a predictive model.

What would faster training change in everyday research? One plausible benefit is a shorter cycle between trying an idea and examining its outcome. A team could compare alternative model designs or revisit a disappointing prediction sooner. Such possibilities matter because research often involves successive adjustments. A training run provides information about what works; the next run tests a revised approach. Reducing the time spent on each run could make that process more flexible, subject to the cost of preparing and checking each selection.

Whole-workflow gains depend on where the time goes. Consider a purely illustrative project requiring nine hours to prepare data and one hour to train a model. Reducing training time to twenty minutes brings the total to nine hours and twenty minutes. The training stage becomes three times as fast, while the overall saving is forty minutes. A project dominated by repeated training would have a different balance. This arithmetic explains why timing individual stages matters when translating a benchmark into a practical research benefit.

Selection itself also has a cost. Identifying representative examples may involve calculation, processing or comparison, and a reduced dataset still needs a credible evaluation. A useful assessment would place those costs alongside the training time saved. Repeated use could change the balance: a selection prepared once might support several training runs, while a rapidly changing research question might require fresh selection work. These are consequences of the workflow, with their scale awaiting measurement in the particular setting.

The same care applies to the route from a prediction to a physical material. A computational model can help researchers decide which possibilities deserve attention. A candidate then faces further questions about preparation, stability, measurement and practical use. Each stage produces evidence of a different kind. The reported result concerns the efficiency of learning electronic-structure predictions; faster discovery and successful manufacturing remain outcomes to establish through subsequent work.

The most revealing feature of the study may be the coexistence of useful random pruning and a strategy designed around coverage. Together they suggest that familiar examples can be plentiful, while the challenge of retaining distinctive information becomes sharper as the teaching set contracts. A model’s average success offers one view of that trade-off. Its behaviour on rare, difficult or scientifically decisive cases offers another.

The next example added to a scientific dataset could repeat a lesson the model already handles well. It could also describe the case that changes a research decision. As researchers learn to train with smaller selections, the central question becomes increasingly precise: how can they recognise which of those two examples they are holding?
