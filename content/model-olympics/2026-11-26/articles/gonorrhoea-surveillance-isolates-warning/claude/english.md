# Ceftriaxone-Resistant Gonorrhoea Turned Up in Four Countries' Clinic Samples. How Far Does the Warning Reach?

On 25 November 2026, the World Health Organization (WHO) published 2025 results from its Enhanced Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme (EGASP), covering ten countries in five WHO regions. Gonorrhoea is one of the world's common sexually transmitted infections, and a network like this one watches it for signs that antibiotics are losing their grip. Clinics collect swabs from patients with symptoms, laboratories grow the bacteria, and scientists test them against the antibiotics doctors use. The most pressing finding involves ceftriaxone, an injected antibiotic that the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) described in 2024 as the recommended gonorrhoea treatment in the United States.

Selected outpatient sexual-health clinics enrolled mainly men with urethral discharge. Thailand also enrolled men with urethritis, an inflammation of the tube that carries urine, in the absence of discharge. In all, 4,015 men contributed 4,123 episodes. Laboratories grew gonorrhoea from 2,911 episodes. They ran susceptibility tests, which measure how bacteria respond to drugs, on 2,886 isolates, or laboratory-grown bacterial samples. That figure is 99.1% of the culture-positive total. The drug-specific tested groups were 2,883 isolates for ceftriaxone, 2,643 for cefixime, a related antibiotic, and 2,884 for azithromycin. Samples from Cambodia, India, Thailand and Viet Nam showed ceftriaxone and cefixime resistance, and 85 ceftriaxone-tested isolates, or 2.9%, exceeded the breakpoint. Samples from eight countries showed azithromycin resistance. Ceftriaxone and cefixime results use clinical resistance breakpoints, and azithromycin results use an epidemiological surveillance cutoff; both kinds of laboratory threshold are explained below. A separate Thai substudy examined rectal and throat samples.

That finding is a serious signal. It also raises a puzzle that sits under every warning of this kind. The bacteria came from a set of chosen clinics, so how much of the wider world do those clinics reveal?

Answering that question takes three steps. The first is the infection itself. The second is how bacteria outlast medicines. The third is the long journey from a swab to a drug result.

## An infection that often stays quiet

Gonorrhoea is caused by a bacterium, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, which passes between people through sex. CDC background material from January 2025 says it can infect the genitals, the rectum and the throat. Many infected people have mild symptoms or ones they would easily miss, and this silent pattern is especially common in women. A person can therefore carry and pass on the infection while feeling perfectly well.

The stakes rise when an infection goes untreated. CDC lists serious complications, including pelvic inflammatory disease, an infection of the womb and nearby organs, and infertility. Successful treatment clears an infection. A cured person can still catch gonorrhoea again later, because clearing one infection leaves the body open to the next.

Together, these facts explain why health services care so much about a dependable cure. Silent infections spread quietly, and people can be infected again. An infection left in place can cause harm that lasts a lifetime. A medicine that works reliably is one of the main tools for breaking that cycle.

## How a germ outlasts a medicine

Antibiotic resistance is a property of the germs. It describes bacteria that have become able to withstand a drug designed to kill them or halt their growth.

CDC's October 2026 explanation describes the process in a few steps. Bacteria vary from one another, as any large population of living things does. When an antibiotic arrives, it kills or halts the bacteria it can reach. Bacteria carrying a trait that helps them withstand the drug can survive this pressure. Those survivors can multiply, and their descendants can carry the same trait. Resistant bacteria can then spread from person to person, and an infection caught from them may be harder to treat.

This picture matters for fairness as much as for biology. A resistant infection in one person reveals something about the bacteria circulating in a community. It points investigators toward patterns of spread and antibiotic use across a whole population. Blame for the individual who happens to carry the infection has zero grounding in this biology.

Gonorrhoea has a long record of this kind of adaptation. CDC's 2024 page on drug-resistant gonorrhoea traces how the bacterium developed resistance to several groups of antibiotics that once treated it. That history is the reason health agencies track its response to drugs so closely. Each time one medicine becomes less reliable, the remaining options carry more weight.

## Finding an infection and testing a drug are two different jobs

At this point the story moves into the laboratory, where a quiet but important split takes place.

Much modern gonorrhoea diagnosis uses a nucleic acid amplification test, or NAAT. The test looks for the bacterium's genetic material. It copies small stretches of that material again and again until there is enough to detect. It answers a crucial question clearly: is gonorrhoea present?

A second question calls for a different method. To learn which antibiotics a particular strain responds to, a laboratory usually needs the living bacteria. That means culture: placing the sample on a nutrient plate and growing the organism. Gonorrhoea is a delicate microbe. It needs suitable conditions during collection, transport and growth in the laboratory, so culture asks more of clinics and laboratories than a genetic test does.

Once the bacteria grow, the laboratory exposes them to a range of drug strengths and records which strengths halt them. This step is called antimicrobial susceptibility testing, or AST. It measures how the bacteria actually behave when they meet a drug.

CDC describes a historical shift in the United States. As NAAT became the standard way to diagnose gonorrhoea, the country's capacity to culture the bacterium declined. That account is American and dates from 2024; laboratories in other countries follow their own histories.

The broader lesson still applies widely. A health system can know a great deal about where infections are while seeing a narrower slice of how the bacteria respond to drugs. Genetic tests can also predict resistance for some known genetic markers, and that adds useful information. The full measured response to a drug still comes from growing the organism and testing it. Surveillance networks built around culture exist to widen that slice.

## Reading the numbers link by link

Each figure in a surveillance report of this kind belongs to a different link in a chain, and following the chain is the clearest way to read it.

1. **A man** comes to a participating clinic with symptoms.
2. **An episode** is one distinct bout of infection or symptoms. Follow-up visits for the same bout belong to the same episode.
3. **An isolate** comes from a swab that reaches the laboratory and grows living gonorrhoea.
4. **A drug test** checks that isolate against one particular antibiotic.

One man can return with a fresh episode, so the second link can hold more records than the first. Successful cultures form a smaller group than episodes, and isolates that receive any testing form a group equal to or smaller than that. At the fourth link, the counting changes shape. Each drug's tested group is a subset of the tested isolates, and one isolate can be tested against several drugs, so the total number of drug tests can exceed the number of isolates. Each link answers a narrower question.

A completion percentage sits at the junction of the third and fourth links. A high completion proportion among successful cultures means that nearly every isolate a laboratory manages to grow goes on to some susceptibility testing. That reflects thorough testing of the isolates in hand. Overall laboratory quality is a separate question, measured through checks such as retesting and quality control. The men, the episodes and the wider communities around the clinics sit further up the chain, and a completion figure speaks to them only indirectly.

"Some testing" also covers a range, as separate drug-specific totals show. A careful reader keeps the unit fixed: men, episodes, isolates, or drug-specific tests. Sliding from one unit to the next is the easiest way to turn a sound finding into a misleading claim.

A resistance percentage shows why this matters. It is a share of tested isolates from the participating clinics, a proportion of a laboratory sample that crossed a line. A national infection rate would need a sample designed to represent a whole country. The chance that a given patient's treatment fails is a separate question again, and it depends on events in that patient's body and life.

## What a laboratory line means

To call an isolate resistant, a laboratory measures its minimum inhibitory concentration, or MIC. The MIC is the lowest drug strength tested that stops visible growth under standard laboratory conditions. The MIC is a number, and a threshold gives that number meaning.

Thresholds come in two kinds, and they serve different purposes. A clinical breakpoint is tied to expectations about treatment. An epidemiological surveillance cutoff marks bacteria that stand apart from the usual population and flags them for attention. A result above a surveillance cutoff is therefore best read as a flag, and a result above a clinical breakpoint as a measurement against a clinical standard.

Even a clinical breakpoint describes bacteria in a dish. A treatment outcome describes what happened in a person. CDC's laboratory background treats these as complementary pieces of evidence. A person who returns with gonorrhoea after treatment may have resistant bacteria. That person may also have caught a fresh infection, or other factors may have played a part. Laboratory susceptibility results measure how bacteria behave. Knowing how often that behaviour leads to treatment failure in patients requires follow-up after treatment, a separate layer of evidence.

## Lookout posts on a long coast

An everyday picture helps here, offered as an illustration. Imagine a long coastline guarded by a handful of lookout posts. When a lookout spots a hazard, that sighting is valuable. It is a real hazard, seen clearly, and it justifies warning the stretch of water in view and sending someone to look closer. Each post still sees its own stretch of sea, and the coast between posts stays dim.

Adding posts may reveal hazards that were already out there, so the number of sightings can rise as the view widens. Removing posts, or a foggy week, can make the sea look calmer than it is.

A selected symptomatic-clinic network works much like those posts. It sees most clearly the people who arrive at its clinics with symptoms. CDC's background explains that infections in women are often silent. Silent infections, many throat and rectal infections, and people who use other kinds of health services tend to sit farther from such a view.

Where a set of samples shows zero resistance to a drug, that result describes those samples. It describes that stretch of the coast, at that time, for those patients.

The same logic shapes comparisons. In any network of this kind, countries can differ in which clinics take part, how many people enrol, how often cultures succeed and which drugs are tested. A difference between two countries' results may reflect real differences in the bacteria. It may reflect differences in how each country looked, or a mix of both. Changes over time carry the same complication: when a country joins a programme or adjusts its sites, its recorded counts can shift whatever the bacteria are doing.

The available evidence leaves those possibilities open. A sighting of this kind is solid evidence about the bacteria in hand. Its reach stays attached to the clinics, specimens and tests that produced it.

## What would make the warning sharper

This is a measurement problem with real clinical stakes. Better detection can make a threat look larger at first, because more of it becomes visible. Weak detection can make a threat look reassuringly small. A health system that wants to act wisely on a warning needs to know which of those effects is at work.

Several kinds of evidence would help. These are proposals drawn from the surveillance methods and the testing background, and they describe information needs. They are separate from any programme already in place.

- **Steady, comparable sampling.** The same kinds of clinics, using the same methods over several years, with coverage recorded clearly, would let readers tell a changing bacterial pattern apart from a changing view.
- **Confirmation of unusual results.** Steady quality checks and retesting of surprising findings raise confidence that a striking number reflects the bacteria.
- **Follow-up after treatment.** Linking laboratory results with what happened to patients would show how closely a result above a breakpoint tracks a real-world outcome.
- **Wider views.** Extending the view to women, throat and rectal infections, and people who receive care in other settings would fill in more of the coast.

Clinical guidance belongs with the authorities responsible for it. They can review it using the fuller picture that such evidence would provide.

Newer treatments add one more reason to keep watching. Any new drug would face the same pressures of bacterial variation and selection, and would need the same careful tracking.

This account follows WHO's 2025 clinic-sample susceptibility findings alongside separately dated CDC background from 2024 to 2026; clinical choices rest on current local guidance.

The warning is clear about the bacteria the laboratories grew. The harder task now belongs to the health systems that read it. They need to widen the view, hold it steady and connect a result in a dish to the experience of a patient. If a later report arrives with new sightings, how much of the coastline will it be able to see?
