# The Gonorrhoea Warning Hidden in the Samples We Can Test

Gonorrhoea is a bacterial infection passed through sexual contact, and antibiotics are central to treating it. On 25 November 2026, the World Health Organization published a surveillance report examining the bacterium’s resistance to those medicines. The work brings together clinics that collect patient samples and laboratories that measure how the bacteria respond to drugs. Behind it lies a growing challenge for health systems: finding an infection and measuring its vulnerability to treatment require different kinds of evidence. A laboratory can identify a warning clearly while the warning’s reach across the wider population remains uncertain.

The infection can affect the genitals, rectum and throat. Some people develop symptoms that bring them to a clinic; others carry the infection with few outward signs. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention explains that symptom-free infection is especially common among women. Untreated gonorrhoea can cause pelvic inflammatory disease, an infection involving reproductive organs, and can lead to infertility. These possible consequences give timely diagnosis and effective treatment their importance. They also make visible symptoms an incomplete guide to where infection exists.

Resistance develops in bacteria. Within a bacterial population, inherited differences can help some organisms survive exposure to an antibiotic. When susceptible bacteria die or stop growing, surviving resistant bacteria can multiply. Resistant strains can also spread between people. Someone can therefore acquire an infection whose resistance developed earlier in a chain of transmission. A resistance result describes a property of the organism; explaining how an individual acquired it requires a separate investigation.

This distinction matters both medically and socially. An infection that responds poorly to a medicine can become harder to treat, while blame directed at the person carrying it adds little to understanding its biology. Resistance surveillance asks a collective question: how reliably do medicines act against the bacteria circulating among people? Answering that question requires samples, laboratory measurements and a clear account of whom those samples represent.

The report’s factual core is compact. WHO’s Enhanced Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme, or EGASP, recorded 2025 observations from 10 countries across five WHO regions. Its selected sentinel facilities mainly sampled men with urethral discharge; Thailand also included urethritis, inflammation of the urine-carrying tube, with discharge optional. There were 4,015 men, 4,123 episodes and 2,911 culture-positive isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing covered 2,886 isolates, or 99.1% of culture-positive samples. Drug-specific totals were 2,883 for ceftriaxone, 2,643 for cefixime and 2,884 for azithromycin. Among ceftriaxone-tested isolates, 85, or 2.9%, exceeded the resistance breakpoint. Ceftriaxone and cefixime resistance appeared in Cambodia, India, Thailand and Viet Nam; azithromycin resistance appeared in eight countries. The first two drugs used clinical resistance breakpoints; azithromycin used an epidemiological cutoff. The main series offers limited coverage of women, symptom-free infections and other anatomical sites; a separate Thai substudy examined rectal and throat samples.

To understand the numbers, it helps to follow the units being counted. A person is one unit. An episode is a recorded occurrence, so the same person can contribute another record during a later episode. An isolate is a sample of bacteria grown in a laboratory. A drug test is a measurement made on that sample. Each unit answers a different question, and each step changes the group to which a percentage applies.

That makes the denominator—the group beneath the line in a fraction—as important as the resistant samples above it. A proportion calculated from bacteria tested against a particular medicine describes those tests. Turning it into an estimate for a whole population requires evidence about how the tested samples relate to everyone else. Similarly, high testing coverage after successful bacterial growth describes one stage of the process. Earlier stages include reaching a clinic, providing a specimen and obtaining a usable culture.

A useful illustration is a network of lookout posts surveying a large landscape. Each lookout can report what appears within its field of view. Together, the posts can detect danger across several locations and help direct closer examination. Estimating conditions throughout the territory requires another piece of knowledge: which areas the posts can see, how consistently they watch and what lies beyond their view.

In surveillance, the field of view comes from clinic locations, attendance patterns, eligibility rules and laboratory capacity. People with symptoms may seek care through different routes from people who feel well. Facilities equipped to handle specimens may serve different communities from facilities with fewer laboratory resources. These features help determine which bacteria enter the measurement system. They are part of the meaning of the result.

Selected sites still have substantial value. Repeated observation in a defined setting can reveal a concerning organism, support confirmation and provide a basis for investigation. A carefully maintained lookout can produce a dependable warning from its own position. The broader estimate depends on how that position fits into the landscape.

The same logic complicates comparisons over time. Adding clinics or improving specimen collection could increase the number of resistant samples found because observation became more complete. Reduced sampling could produce a smaller recorded count even while resistant bacteria continued circulating. A genuine biological change could occur alongside either development. These are possible measurement effects, rather than established explanations for any particular annual result.

A persuasive trend therefore needs continuity in both the thing being measured and the way it is measured. Comparing similar patient groups at similar sites, using comparable laboratory methods, would help separate a change in resistance from a change in visibility. Where coverage expands, recording that expansion allows analysts to interpret the new findings in context. More information can initially make a problem look larger because it reveals more of what was already there.

The laboratory distinction at the centre of this story begins with the infection test. A nucleic acid amplification test, usually shortened to NAAT, looks for bacterial genetic material. It amplifies a target so that the laboratory can detect it. This provides evidence of infection and has become an important diagnostic approach.

Culture serves another purpose. It grows living bacteria from a specimen, giving the laboratory an organism whose behaviour can be examined. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing then exposes the organism to measured concentrations of a medicine. The result describes how growth responds under controlled conditions. Infection detection and susceptibility measurement thus contribute different information to care and surveillance.

The standard laboratory term is minimum inhibitory concentration, or MIC. It means the lowest tested concentration of a drug that stops visible bacterial growth under specified conditions. The laboratory compares that value with an established cutoff to classify the result. A higher concentration needed to stop growth can indicate reduced susceptibility, although the meaning of a classification depends on the drug and the standard being used.

Two kinds of cutoff serve related purposes. A clinical breakpoint helps interpret a laboratory measurement in relation to treatment. An epidemiological cutoff helps identify organisms whose susceptibility differs from the usual bacterial population, making it useful for tracking emerging resistance. Reading either result requires knowing which boundary was applied. Their different purposes remain important even when both appear in a table about resistance.

This is where a laboratory warning and a patient’s outcome meet. A controlled growth test provides evidence about the bacterium. Clinical follow-up provides evidence about what happened after treatment. Connecting the two can show how laboratory findings relate to actual recovery or persistent infection. The connection requires records that establish what treatment was given, when follow-up occurred and how the outcome was assessed.

Persistent infection also needs clinical investigation. Treatment received, completion of a regimen and possible reinfection can affect the interpretation of a later positive result. Assigning a cause depends on the circumstances of the episode. Conversely, a resistance signal can deserve investigation as soon as it appears in the laboratory, while its relationship to treatment outcomes is still being established.

Genetic information can sometimes help predict resistance when relevant markers are understood. The strength of such a prediction depends on the organism, the medicine and the test’s validation. For this account, the central distinction remains between identifying bacterial genetic material and directly measuring the growth response of living bacteria. Evaluating the capabilities of a particular molecular resistance test would require evidence specific to that test.

Maintaining the ability to perform culture introduces practical demands. Bacteria must remain viable through collection and transport, and laboratories need the equipment and expertise to grow and test them. A diagnosis based on genetic material and a result based on living organisms therefore depend on partly different laboratory capabilities. A health service can become highly capable at detecting infection while its access to direct susceptibility measurements follows a different path.

CDC’s February 2024 background describes declining culture capacity in the United States as NAAT became common. That historical observation illustrates how diagnostic practice can change the evidence available for resistance surveillance. Its geographical and temporal scope is specific. Assessing laboratory capacity elsewhere, or its condition today, calls for local evidence.

The practical issue is how health systems sustain both useful diagnosis and an adequate view of bacterial susceptibility. Each contributes something necessary to understanding infection. Rapid identification can bring an episode into care. A living sample can reveal how the organism behaves against a drug. Connecting those functions requires decisions about specimen collection, referral and laboratory support, grounded in the services people actually use.

The evidence boundary is straightforward: the findings establish resistance among tested samples. Estimating its wider frequency, explaining changes over time and measuring its effect on treatment require comparable sampling, documented laboratory quality and linked clinical follow-up. Those additional records would extend the warning’s meaning from an observed bacterial response towards an account of its consequences for patients.

This also explains why country rankings can mislead. A place that samples more intensively or reaches a different patient group may detect a different pattern. A low recorded count leaves its interpretation dependent on the amount and type of observation behind it. Fair comparison requires attention to coverage before assigning meaning to the apparent order of countries.

A signal can support confirmation and closer investigation while clinical authorities assess its implications for guidance. The judgment about treatment draws on a broader body of evidence, including outcomes and current local recommendations. Statements about available medicines in a dated background document retain their original time context. Establishing treatment options in a particular jurisdiction today requires current clinical and regulatory sources.

Surveillance itself creates information. Its health value depends on what happens when that information reaches laboratories, clinicians and public-health teams. An unusual result may lead to further testing, a closer look at related cases or an assessment of existing guidance. The useful question is how well the evidence travels through those decisions, and whether subsequent records show that the response helped.

For gonorrhoea, that task starts with an organism capable of spreading quietly and sometimes causing lasting harm. It continues through a sequence of human and laboratory encounters: a person reaches care, a specimen is collected, bacteria are detected or grown, and a measurement is interpreted. Every stage helps determine what the health system can see.

The warning is meaningful because living bacteria have shown a concerning response under test conditions. Its wider significance will become clearer as observation and clinical evidence connect. As surveillance improves, the question will sharpen: how much of the next apparent change reflects bacteria becoming harder to treat, how much reflects a clearer view of their spread, and where are both happening together?
