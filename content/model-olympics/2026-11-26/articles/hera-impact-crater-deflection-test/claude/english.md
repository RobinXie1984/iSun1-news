# Hera Finds the Crater DART Left Behind. Can the Moonlet's Hidden Interior Help Predict the Next Impact?

Four years after NASA flew a spacecraft into a small asteroid on purpose, a European probe has arrived to inspect the result. On 25 November 2026, the European Space Agency (ESA) and the German Aerospace Center (DLR) published reports on the first close-up findings from Hera, a spacecraft built to study the aftermath of that collision. Hera's subject is Dimorphos, a moonlet that ESA puts at about 151 metres across, roughly one and a half football pitches end to end. Dimorphos circles a larger asteroid, Didymos, which ESA gives as about 730 metres across. The two travel around the Sun together as a pair. In September 2022, NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test, known as DART, struck Dimorphos to see whether a crash could change how an asteroid moves. Telescopes on Earth showed that it could. Hera's task is to help explain how and why.

That task belongs to a larger effort that space agencies call planetary defence. Earth shares its corner of the solar system with a great many asteroids, and over the planet's long history some have struck it. NASA describes planetary defence as a chain of work. Astronomers find asteroids, track their paths and judge which ones could someday come close to Earth. Engineers and scientists test ways to respond if a dangerous one turns up. Most of that chain is patient watching through telescopes. DART was NASA's first mission to test a response by actually reaching out and touching an asteroid.

The response DART tested is called a kinetic impact. Kinetic is a word about motion. A kinetic impactor is a heavy, fast-moving spacecraft that changes an asteroid's motion through the force of the collision alone. It carries a simple payload: its own mass and speed. Momentum, the quantity that measures how hard a moving object is to stop, passes from the spacecraft into the asteroid when they meet.

The idea depends on time. A tiny nudge, given years ahead, grows into a large difference in where an asteroid will be on a given future day, because the change keeps adding up along every kilometre of its journey. In this sense, deflecting an asteroid means shifting its schedule so that it reaches the crossing point of Earth's orbit at a different moment from Earth. Breaking an asteroid apart is a separate idea, and DART was designed for the gentler goal of a nudge.

Mission planners chose the Didymos pair as a harmless target suited to a controlled experiment, and it offered two practical advantages. First, the pair comes with a built-in clock. As Dimorphos circles Didymos, it regularly passes in front of and behind the larger body as seen from Earth, and the combined light of the pair dips each time. By timing those dips, astronomers can tell how long one lap takes. Second, the moonlet's lap is small and short, so any change shows up within days or weeks. A change to the pair's long journey around the Sun would take far longer to detect. The experiment was built around a quantity that telescopes could measure with confidence.

## A clock that started running faster

On 26 September 2022, DART struck Dimorphos. In a summary published on 1 March 2023, NASA and the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL), which built and operated DART, reported the measured outcome. Dimorphos's orbital period, meaning the time it takes to complete one lap around Didymos, had shortened by 33 minutes, give or take about one minute. Before the impact a lap took roughly 11 hours and 55 minutes. Afterwards it took roughly 11 hours and 22 minutes. The analysis also reported that the collision initially slowed Dimorphos along its orbit by about 2.7 millimetres per second.

Those two numbers describe different things, and keeping them apart is the key to the whole story. The 33 minutes is a change in time per lap. The 2.7 millimetres per second is a change in speed, a very slow pace, closer to a crawl than a walk. That slowing sounds strange beside a faster lap, but it fits how orbits work. A moonlet that loses a little speed drops into a slightly tighter path around its partner, and a tighter path takes less time to complete. A small brake produced a quicker clock, and the quicker clock was the thing telescopes on Earth could time.

The 2023 summary also reported a surprise in the size of the push. DART delivered more momentum to Dimorphos than the spacecraft's own momentum could account for. The extra came from material blasted off the moonlet's surface by the impact. As that debris sprayed outward, back toward the direction DART had come from, the moonlet recoiled the other way, in the direction DART had been travelling. NASA and APL expressed this as a momentum enhancement factor. They estimated that the total push was somewhere between 2.2 and 4.9 times the push from the spacecraft alone. The summary ties that range to assumptions about the moonlet's mass, a quantity that had to be estimated at the time.

An everyday picture can make the recoil easier to imagine. This is an illustration of the physics, separate from any reconstruction of what happened on Dimorphos. Picture a child sitting in a small wagon on smooth pavement, holding an armful of sand. A friend standing in front of the wagon tosses a ball at it, and the wagon rolls back a little. Now suppose the child also throws a handful of sand forward, toward the friend. The sand carries momentum one way, so the wagon gains matching momentum in the opposite direction and rolls back farther. On Dimorphos, the impact itself supplied the energy that threw the debris, and the debris played the part of the sand. The picture shows the direction of the extra push. It leaves out how much material flew, how fast it went and how the moonlet's surface broke up.

That last part is where the forecasting problem begins. The amount of debris an impact throws depends on what the target is made of. A loosely packed body, held together mainly by its own weak gravity, may spray far more material than a firm, tightly bound rock. The same spacecraft hitting two different asteroids could therefore produce two very different pushes. DART gave scientists one excellent measurement on one particular moonlet. Turning that measurement into a rule for other asteroids requires knowing what this moonlet is like inside, how heavy it is and how its surface responded.

## What Hera has seen so far

Hera arrived to find a visibly changed moonlet. According to ESA's 25 November report, Hera's early close-up views show one face of Dimorphos flattened by a crater that the agency describes as stadium-sized and roughly as wide as the side DART struck. Other parts of the surface still carry boulder fields that resemble those seen in DART's own images from 2022, so the collision reshaped one face while leaving the rest of the moonlet recognisable. ESA's report also states that the DART impact has left Dimorphos tumbling irregularly, a point it attributes to Michael Kueppers of the Hera team. The mission teams further reported hundreds of previously unknown metre-scale boulders orbiting the pair. ESA reported that Hera has been observing from about 25 to 30 kilometres away. At the time of the reports, the pair was roughly 193.5 million kilometres from Earth.

DLR's report adds detail about the images themselves. One picture, dated 7 November 2026 in DLR's caption, was taken from 29 kilometres away at a resolution of 2.7 metres per pixel. Each tiny square in the image therefore covers a patch of ground about 2.7 metres wide, so a boulder the size of a family car fills only a pixel or two. Another image sequence dates from 3 November. The pictures were taken in early November, and the reports describing them appeared later in the month.

These are early findings, reported by the mission's own institutional teams. This account relies on the ESA and DLR reports and the 2023 NASA/APL summary as published, and reanalysis of the underlying data lies outside its scope.

Each observation carries its own meaning, and some meanings remain open. The stadium comparison gives a sense of scale; a precise crater diameter awaits detailed measurement. The orbiting boulders are newly observed, and their origin is still an open question. Some may have been knocked loose by DART. Others may have been circling the pair before 2022, too small for earlier telescopes to pick out. Separating those two groups is part of the work ahead.

The tumbling also needs a careful reading. Tumbling describes how Dimorphos turns around its own centre, so the face it points toward Didymos keeps shifting in an irregular way. The orbit describes the path of the whole moonlet around Didymos. The 33-minute result concerns the orbit. ESA links the tumbling to the impact; that link is the mission team's reported interpretation, and the orbital figure from 2023 remains the measured change in the moonlet's lap. The tumbling and the orbiting boulders are separate observations, and only the boulders' origin stays an open question in the reports.

## Weighing a moonlet from a distance

A photograph can show the shape of a body in fine detail, yet it leaves the body's weight and inner structure hidden. That gap matters because of the 2.2-to-4.9 range. Scientists know how much Dimorphos's speed changed. To turn a speed change into a total push, they need the mass of the thing that changed speed. A heavier moonlet would mean a larger total push for the same change in speed, and so a larger share of credit for the flying debris. A lighter moonlet would shrink that share. Before Hera, the mass rested on estimates built from the moonlet's size and a likely density, and the reported recoil range reflects those mass assumptions.

ESA and DLR describe Hera's work as measuring the moonlet's shape, crater, internal structure and mass, so that researchers can better interpret why DART produced the change it did. A common way for a spacecraft to weigh a small body is to follow, with radio signals, how the body's gravity gently bends the spacecraft's own path. Hera's reports list mass as a measurement goal; the November findings describe the target and the plan, and a final mass and final recoil value remain ahead.

The inside of Dimorphos is the other half of the puzzle. If the moonlet is a loose pile of rubble with empty spaces between its pieces, that would help explain a large spray of debris and a generous push. If it is more tightly bound, the explanation would shift. The two reports describe the next steps toward an answer. Hera is scheduled to move closer, to about 13 kilometres, in December 2026. In January 2027, it is planned to release two small companion spacecraft called CubeSats, each about the size of a shoebox. One, Juventas, led by Luxembourg, is intended to probe the interior with low-frequency radar, which uses long radio waves able to pass into rock and return echoes from inside it, and to attempt a landing later in its mission. The other, Milani, led by Italy, is intended to study the moonlet's materials and the dust around the pair. These operations are plans, and their results belong to the months ahead.

Moving closer brings its own trade-off. A nearer view sharpens every picture and strengthens every gravity measurement. The hundreds of orbiting boulders, however, turn the space around the pair into a more crowded place to fly, and Hera's navigators have to account for them as the spacecraft descends. The same rocks that make the approach more demanding are also part of the scientific record. Tracking their paths over time could help researchers judge where individual boulders may have come from, including whether some trace back to the impact.

## From one experiment to a forecast

Taken together, the measurements form a chain. In 2022 and 2023, telescopes on Earth timed the moonlet's lap and showed that a spacecraft impact had changed it. In November 2026, Hera's cameras showed the crater on the struck face and a moonlet that ESA reports the impact left tumbling, along with a crowd of orbiting boulders whose origin remains under investigation. The next links, mass and interior structure, are what could connect the visible damage to the measured change in motion.

One way to think about this is the way engineers treat a crash test. A car crash test is valuable partly because investigators can study the wrecked car afterwards and match the damage to the forces involved, then use that match to predict how other cars will fare. DART was the crash. Hera is the investigation. A single well-studied crash still describes one vehicle, and a single well-studied asteroid still describes one asteroid. Its value grows as it sharpens the computer models scientists use to predict impacts on asteroids that differ in size, makeup and structure.

That is why the work matters beyond Dimorphos. If a hazardous asteroid were someday discovered on a path toward Earth, the people planning a response would need to estimate how hard to push it, how early and with how large a spacecraft. Those estimates would depend on the asteroid's properties, many of which might have to be inferred from telescope observations at a great distance. Calibrated models, tested against a real impact that has been examined up close, could make such estimates more trustworthy. They would also depend on discovery coming early enough to leave time for a small nudge to grow into a safe miss. The November reports concern the science of one experiment. Detection, tracking, decisions and the readiness of any future spacecraft are separate parts of the larger planetary defence effort.

What humanity has earned so far is substantial. People chose a harmless moonlet, hit it on schedule, measured the change in its orbit from Earth and then sent a second spacecraft to examine the result up close. Hera has already shown a crater on the face DART struck and a moonlet that ESA reports the impact left tumbling, ringed by hundreds of boulders whose origins remain open questions. Hera's plans for the coming months include measuring the moonlet's mass and probing its interior with radar. The answer to one quiet question may shape how far this single experiment can reach: if those planned measurements of Dimorphos's weight and inner structure succeed, will the numbers help scientists predict the recoil from a crash on asteroids that no spacecraft has yet visited?
