# Hera Has Found DART’s Crater. Can It Help Predict the Next Asteroid’s Response?

An asteroid can travel through space for millions of years while the question of danger to Earth comes down to where it will be at one particular time. Planetary defence seeks ways to change that meeting. On 25 November 2026, the European Space Agency and Germany’s aerospace centre, DLR, reported a new view of an experiment designed to explore that possibility: Hera, a European spacecraft, had examined the aftermath of NASA’s deliberate 2022 collision with the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos. The target belonged to a pair of asteroids that posed no threat to Earth. Researchers already knew the collision had changed its motion. Now they could examine the damaged surface.

Dimorphos is about 151 metres across and circles Didymos, a larger asteroid roughly 730 metres across. Together, they travel around the Sun. According to ESA’s mission report, Hera’s close views show one face of the moonlet flattened by a crater roughly as wide as the struck side. Boulder fields remain elsewhere. The mission teams also reported irregular tumbling and hundreds of previously unknown, metre-scale boulders orbiting the pair. These findings turn a distant, measured change in motion into a physical aftermath that scientists can study.

The larger purpose reaches beyond this particular crater. A future attempt to divert a hazardous asteroid would require a prediction: how much would a spacecraft’s collision change that object’s motion? DART established that a deliberate impact could alter the orbit of this target. Hera’s task is to help explain how the target produced that response. The connection between those two achievements runs through the material inside the asteroid, the rock thrown into space and the gravity holding the system together.

NASA called its spacecraft the Double Asteroid Redirection Test, shortened to DART. On 26 September 2022, it deliberately struck Dimorphos. The method is called a kinetic impactor: a spacecraft uses the motion it already carries to deliver a push through a collision. Its useful effect comes from transferring momentum, the quantity of motion associated with an object’s mass and velocity. A heavier spacecraft, or one arriving faster, brings more momentum into the encounter.

Choosing a moonlet made the experiment easier to measure. Dimorphos followed a recurring circuit around Didymos, giving observers a clock they could compare before and after the collision. Earth-based observations established how the duration of that circuit changed. This local orbit offered a clear experimental measure within a much larger system: the smaller rock circling the larger one, while both continued their journey around the Sun.

A NASA/APL research summary published on 1 March 2023 reported that the orbital period had shortened by **33 ± 1 minutes**, from approximately 11 hours 55 minutes to 11 hours 22 minutes. The same analysis estimated an initial slowing along the mutual orbit of about 2.7 millimetres per second. It also estimated that the total momentum transferred was between 2.2 and 4.9 times the spacecraft’s incoming momentum, with the range depending on assumptions about Dimorphos’s mass. Material thrown out by the collision supplied the additional recoil.

The minutes and the millimetres describe different parts of the story. The first measure tells us how much sooner the moonlet completed each circuit. The second describes the estimated initial change in its speed along that orbit. A small slowing can lead to a shorter orbital period because gravity connects speed, orbital shape and distance. Slowing an orbiting body can move it onto a tighter path, with a shorter time for completing a circuit. Reading the result requires keeping that gravitational relationship in view.

These measurements concern Dimorphos’s motion around Didymos. Assessing a future threat to Earth would involve a separate calculation of an asteroid’s path around the Sun and its position when it approached our planet. The experiment gives researchers evidence about delivering a push. Turning a push into a successful intervention also requires understanding where the target is going and how much time remains.

An everyday illustration helps separate the evidence. Imagine investigators reconstructing a vehicle crash using a timing record and photographs of the damaged vehicle. The timing record establishes a change in motion. The photographs show where the structure bent and where material broke away. Each answers a different question, and a useful reconstruction brings them together. This is an analogy for the investigation, rather than a claim that asteroid rock behaves like a manufactured vehicle. With DART and Hera, the orbital observations provide the motion record, and the close views provide evidence of the physical damage.

The asteroid adds a complication that matters enormously: the material leaving the impact site can strengthen the push on the material that remains. Recoil is familiar from releasing an inflated balloon. Air moves out through the opening, and the balloon moves in the opposite direction. In an asteroid collision, the incoming spacecraft supplies energy that crushes, heats and accelerates material. Rock thrown away carries momentum, and the surviving body recoils in response. The amount and direction of that escaping material influence the final change in motion.

That process makes the target an active part of the result. A collision with loosely held material can distribute its energy differently from a collision with stronger, more tightly connected rock. Empty spaces inside an asteroid can absorb some of the impact through compression. Material may escape, move across the surface or fall back under gravity. Each route changes how the energy and momentum are distributed. Researchers therefore need to understand both the spacecraft’s arrival and the asteroid’s response.

Mass matters for an equally practical reason. The same push produces a larger change in motion for a lighter object. If observers know the change in velocity and want to reconstruct the momentum delivered, they also need the mass. An image supplies dimensions and surface features; determining mass requires evidence about the body’s gravitational influence. Two objects with similar outer shapes can contain different amounts of material, especially when their interiors have different proportions of open space.

This is why a broad estimate of impact efficiency has scientific value even while its exact value remains under investigation. It identifies a relationship that needs tighter measurement. Once researchers have a better estimate of mass, they can sharpen their account of how strongly the impact and the expelled material pushed Dimorphos. Interior measurements can then help explain why that amount of recoil occurred. A useful physical model must connect those quantities in a way that also fits the observed damage.

Hera’s photographs have their own chronology. DLR dates one close view to **7 November 2026**, before the reports released on 25 November. Its caption places the spacecraft about 29 kilometres away and gives an image scale of 2.7 metres per pixel, meaning each picture element represents roughly that width at the asteroid. The operating distances describe Hera’s position near the asteroid pair. At this scale, researchers can begin relating large surface features to the overall shape of the body.

The boulders and the tumbling introduce further questions about the aftermath. Previously unknown rocks have become visible through the new observations; their discovery date leaves their formation dates open. Tumbling describes irregular changes in the moonlet’s orientation or spin. Its orbital period describes how long its centre takes to travel around Didymos. A body can change the way it turns while also changing the path it follows, and those motions require separate measurements.

Nearby rocks also make the scientific environment an operational one. A spacecraft seeking finer detail benefits from approaching its subject. Closer work increases the importance of understanding the surrounding material and planning a safe route. Navigation and science therefore develop together: observations improve knowledge of the neighbourhood, and that knowledge helps determine where further observations can be made. Hera’s survey must turn an unfamiliar environment into a place where increasingly precise measurements are possible.

As described in the 25 November reports, Hera was expected to approach to roughly 13 kilometres in December 2026. Deployment of two shoebox-sized miniature spacecraft, called CubeSats, was planned for January 2027. Luxembourg-led Juventas was intended to probe the interior using low-frequency radar and eventually attempt a landing. Italian-led Milani was intended to investigate materials and surrounding dust. Shape, mass and internal structure remained measurement goals in those reports; the approaches, deployments and landing attempt belonged to the planned work ahead.

Radar offers a way to investigate beneath the visible surface by transmitting radio waves and examining their response. Changes in the returned signal can help researchers infer features of the material the waves encounter. Surface observations and measurements of surrounding dust address other parts of the same physical history. Bringing these kinds of evidence together can reduce the number of plausible explanations for the impact’s outcome.

A model is a simplified mathematical account of that history. Researchers can give it an asteroid with a particular shape, mass and internal arrangement, then calculate how a collision might affect it. Comparing the calculation with observations reveals where the model succeeds and where its assumptions need adjustment. Agreement across motion, shape and material behaviour provides a stronger basis for prediction than agreement with a single measurement. Several different interiors could potentially produce a similar-looking surface feature; additional evidence helps distinguish among them.

The evidence also has a defined boundary. ESA and DLR describe work from the same mission, so their reports share an observational lineage. The numerical DART findings here come from NASA/APL’s 2023 research summary; independent checking of the underlying journal calculations remains outside this account. Source details differ on some dimensions, one impact date and an image-resolution unit. The figures used here follow ESA’s current asteroid sizes, the 26 September date supported by NASA’s mission page and research summary, and DLR’s captioned resolution.

For planetary defence, the eventual value of this reconstruction lies in how well the resulting knowledge travels. Another asteroid could have a different mass, shape, internal structure or surface strength. A model calibrated against Dimorphos would offer a tested starting point, while predictions for a different target would still depend on evidence about that target. The difficult step is moving from explaining one completed collision to estimating the outcome of a collision that has to work on the first attempt.

Time can make that task more manageable. A small change in velocity accumulates into a difference in position as an asteroid continues travelling. With sufficient warning, a modest intervention could alter the circumstances of a future Earth encounter. The direction of the push matters alongside its size, because the aim is to change the future relationship between two moving bodies. Earth keeps moving too.

Detection and tracking therefore belong to the same larger effort as impact physics. Repeated observations help establish an asteroid’s trajectory and narrow uncertainty about its future position. Physical measurements help estimate its response to an intervention. Warning time allows room for planning, travel and assessment. Each contributes something distinct to the question of whether a proposed action would produce a safe outcome.

Hera has brought that question closer to the surface of a real asteroid. The orbital record preserves the measured success of DART; the damaged face gives researchers another way to investigate its cause. The coming measurements are intended to connect the visible scar with the mass and structure that shaped the response. From that connection comes the next, harder question: how confidently could researchers predict the response of a different hazardous asteroid, with enough time left to act?
