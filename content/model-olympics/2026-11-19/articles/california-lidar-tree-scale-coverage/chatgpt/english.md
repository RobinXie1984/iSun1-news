# California’s Wildfire Maps Reveal More Detail. Which Old Patch Could Change a Decision?

California’s forests, hillsides and neighbourhood edges have acquired a more detailed mapping framework, introduced by NASA on November 18, 2026. The US civil space and research agency described maps assembled from aircraft laser surveys that measure the shape of the ground and the surfaces above it. For people studying wildfire, those layers offer a clearer view of the physical landscape: steep slopes, vegetation heights and the places where buildings meet growing things. Their usefulness rests on a practical distinction. A landscape has a shape, a history and a present condition. These maps capture parts of that story at particular moments.

Wildfire planning brings those dimensions together. A hillside’s steepness helps describe its terrain; vegetation adds material that can burn; weather changes the conditions under which a fire might spread. Homes and people give that physical process human consequences. Researchers therefore need several kinds of evidence to assess risk. More detailed structural maps can strengthen one part of that assessment, especially when many places can be examined through a common set of measurements.

The new framework also contains a question that grows more important as users zoom in. Its documented valid survey footprint covers 331,804 square kilometres, about 78.3% of California’s land area. The measurements span 2013–2023. A planner opening a finely divided map in 2026 could therefore be looking at ground surveyed several years earlier, or approaching an area beyond the documented coverage. Which of those differences could change a real decision?

The publication dates help establish what has arrived. ORNL DAAC, a NASA-supported environmental data archive, published version 1 of the dataset on October 13, 2026. NASA’s November 18 account brought wider attention to that existing release. The observations came from earlier aircraft flights. NASA contributed computing resources to the work; the underlying public survey data came from the US Geological Survey, the federal geological and Earth science agency, through its 3D Elevation Program, or 3DEP.

The processing effort drew on approximately 40 terabytes of data from 118 survey campaigns. A terabyte is a unit of digital storage; here, the volume reflects an enormous collection of individual measurements. Researchers used the open-source programming languages R and Python on NASA supercomputing resources to turn those observations into usable map layers. The achievement involves making many surveys work together, with consistent products that researchers can compare across the available footprint.

That consistency matters because measurements gathered for separate projects can be cumbersome to use side by side. Each survey records a particular area at a particular time. Bringing many such surveys into a shared framework gives a researcher a common starting point for questions spanning different regions. It also makes the remaining differences in age and quality especially important: a uniform appearance can make a patchwork of observations feel like a single portrait.

NASA’s short account describes the project broadly in statewide terms. The dataset’s detailed guide provides the narrower coverage figure of 78.3% of California land area. Both statements become clearer when “statewide” describes the framework’s reach and ambition, while the numerical footprint describes where valid acquisitions exist. The share of individual high-risk communities covered by those acquisitions remains unresolved in the documentation available for this account. An area percentage alone says little about which local decisions the gaps might affect.

To understand what the maps offer, begin with the light used to make them. Lidar, short for light detection and ranging, sends out pulses of light and measures their returning reflections. Timing those returns helps locate surfaces in three dimensions. Some reflections come from leaves or branches, some from rooftops, and some from the ground. The resulting collection of measured positions is called a point cloud.

A point cloud becomes more useful for many planning tasks when its measurements are organised into surfaces. As the USGS explains in its background guidance, lidar observations and an elevation model made from those observations are different things. The observations supply evidence; the model arranges that evidence into a representation of the landscape. Choices about which returns belong to the ground, and how to estimate the spaces between measurements, help determine the finished result.

Consider an illustrative house survey. One drawing records the floor’s elevation, another the roof’s elevation, and a third records the height between them. Each answers a different question. The floor drawing helps describe the base; the roof drawing describes the upper surface; subtracting one elevation from the other gives a height. A dated roof measurement still describes the roof at the time of that visit. An unmeasured room remains a gap in the survey, however neatly the drawings line up.

The California products separate the landscape in a similar way. A digital terrain model, abbreviated DTM, estimates bare ground beneath vegetation and buildings. A digital surface model, or DSM, represents the upper visible surface. Subtracting the terrain elevation from the surface elevation produces a height above the ground, described in the dataset as a normalized surface or canopy-height representation, nDSM/CHM. Over vegetation, that difference can help describe the height of the plant canopy.

The distinction changes how a reader should interpret a map. A bare-ground layer can reveal a hillside’s form while vegetation is excluded from the represented surface. A canopy-height layer addresses the vegetation above that ground. These layers complement each other, and their meanings remain separate. A tall surface feature could also involve a building, making the classification of the original returns consequential for an ecological interpretation.

The finest products use cells roughly one metre on each side: each cell covers one square metre. That is the size of the map’s horizontal units. Vertical accuracy is a separate property, as is the reliability of identifying a particular object. A finely divided sheet of graph paper illustrates the distinction: small squares allow a detailed drawing, while the measurements used to place the lines determine its accuracy.

Resolution also varies with the question being asked. The one-metre suite includes terrain, surface elevation, slope, aspect and the canopy-height representation. Slope expresses steepness; aspect gives the direction a slope faces. More elaborate vegetation summaries appear at 10-metre and 30-metre scales. These include cover, measures describing the distribution of heights, and statistics about the first reflections received from laser pulses. The coarser products also include acquisition-year and building-fraction layers, recording survey timing and the share of a cell associated with buildings.

A larger cell gathers information over a larger patch of land. That can be useful when the question concerns the character of a patch rather than the height at one small location. It also changes what comparisons mean. A vegetation summary for a 30-metre cell describes an area containing many one-metre cells. Reading all of these products as interchangeable “one-metre maps” would blur the very distinctions that make the collection useful.

Time adds another layer. Where surveys overlap, the processing selects the most recent available acquisition. This gives preference to newer evidence within the collection, whose acquisition period ends in 2023. Neighbouring areas can still represent different years. Trees grow, fires alter vegetation, and construction changes the relationship between buildings and surrounding land. Those are possible changes that a user must consider when interpreting an older measurement; their occurrence at a particular mapped site requires local evidence.

NASA’s accompanying illustration provides a concrete example of this dating issue. It shows a point cloud from a Santa Clara County wildland–urban interface, a place where development and vegetation meet, surveyed in 2020. That date belongs to the illustrated locality. Across the larger collection, the relevant date follows the acquisition used for each area. The acquisition-year layers therefore carry information essential to interpreting the more visually striking height and terrain products.

The guide also describes measurement and processing limitations. Small water bodies and areas with sparse measurements or few ground returns can contain gaps or interpolation artifacts. Interpolation means estimating values between measured points; an artifact is a pattern introduced by the measurement or processing rather than a faithful feature of the landscape. Other possible problems include unusually high or low values and traces associated with aircraft flight paths.

Buildings create a further complication. The processing uses building information to reduce rooftop influence on vegetation measurements, yet some rooftop contamination remains possible in the 10-metre and 30-metre products. The building reference data date from 2014–2018, while the lidar spans 2013–2023. Those different time windows give the building filter its own historical limits. A vegetation statistic near development deserves attention to both the laser survey and the building information used to interpret it.

These limitations matter most when they intersect with a decision. Imagine, as a hypothetical example, a planning team comparing two areas for closer study. One appears steeper; the other appears to have taller vegetation. The map helps the team identify a difference worth investigating. At this stage, the structural evidence has already done useful work by making the comparison specific.

Now suppose the apparently taller vegetation lies beside buildings, while the other area was surveyed several years earlier. The team’s next question concerns the origin of the apparent difference. Part may reflect the landscape; part may reflect survey timing or rooftop influence. Checking the acquisition dates and examining present conditions could preserve the initial interpretation, narrow it or reverse it. Each outcome would teach the team something about how much weight the mapped contrast deserves.

The same logic applies to a gap. An area beyond valid acquisition coverage has an unresolved measurement status in this dataset. Assigning that area a low-risk meaning would turn an absence of observations into a substantive judgment. The coverage boundary instead tells the user where this particular source of structural evidence ends. Other evidence must carry the assessment there.

NASA describes collaboration with the California Natural Resources Agency and the California Air Resources Board, with intended applications in wildfire-risk assessment, wildlife and ecosystem protection, and resource management. Those aims fit the kinds of information the maps organise. Ground form and vegetation structure can support several questions about the same landscape, while each application brings its own additional evidence and standards.

For wildfire, the distance between a structural description and an operational result remains substantial. Weather changes rapidly. Vegetation condition affects how material burns. Ignition determines where a fire begins, and human exposure concerns who or what lies in its path. A terrain-and-height map supplies physical context for these processes. Its practical contribution depends on how that context is combined with the changing conditions and the decision at hand.

The documented achievement is the mapping framework and its intended applications. Verified reductions in ignitions, burned area, losses or response time remain outside the results established by these sources. This account draws on the institutional report, dataset catalog, user guide and USGS background explanation. Independent reproduction of the underlying tiles and downstream wildfire performance remains outside its evidence base. The products are publicly available under NASA Earthdata guidance, with sign-in required to download files.

A meaningful evaluation could follow the hypothetical planning comparison through to the ground: record the judgment made using existing information, examine how the additional layers change it, and check the locations responsible for that change. Such a test would connect finer detail to a consequential result. It would also reveal where a newer survey matters more than a smaller map cell.

California now has a common way to examine much of its landscape in greater structural detail. The value of that view will emerge through particular places and particular decisions. Somewhere within the dated surveys, processing uncertainties and coverage boundaries may lie a detail that changes which area receives closer attention. Which patch would change the decision—and what would a fresh look at the ground reveal?
