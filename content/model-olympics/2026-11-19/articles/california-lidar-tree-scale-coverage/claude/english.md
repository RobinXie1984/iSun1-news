# California Has a Sharper 3D Map of Its Wildfire Country. Which Patches Are Already Out of Date?

On November 18, 2026, NASA published a news article about a new set of three-dimensional maps. They show the shape of California's land and the height of what grows on it. Milan Loiacono of NASA's Ames Research Center in California wrote the article, which presented the maps as a resource for wildfire risk assessment and other land management work. The maps themselves had appeared five weeks earlier. On October 13, 2026, a NASA-supported data library called the Oak Ridge National Laboratory Distributed Active Archive Center, known as ORNL DAAC, published version 1 of a dataset titled *Lidar-Derived Topography and Ecosystem Structure Products, California, 2013–2023*. It is credited to researchers Kabasares, Benik, Brosnan, Guzman and Park. The measurements underneath are older still: aircraft gathered them across the decade from 2013 to 2023. So this story carries three dates. One tells when the land was observed, one tells when the maps were published, and one tells when NASA described them to the public.

Each institution did a different job. NASA is the United States' civil space and research agency. The U.S. Geological Survey, or USGS, is a federal earth-science agency. Its 3D Elevation Program, called 3DEP, organizes airborne surveys of the nation's terrain and makes the results public. ORNL DAAC stores environmental datasets for NASA and publishes guides explaining what each file holds. USGS survey programs supplied the raw measurements, the research team turned them into maps using NASA computers, the archive released the files with a detailed user guide, and NASA's newsroom told the story.

People who look after California's landscapes regularly decide where to clear brush, which hillsides deserve a closer look, and how forests and wildlife habitat are changing. Those decisions lean on three plain questions about any patch of ground. Where does the ground surface lie? What rises above it, and how tall is it? When did someone last look? Each question needs its own answer, and a richly detailed picture of the first two can be several years old on the third. That tension sits at the heart of the new maps. They make California's terrain and vegetation far easier to see across a very large area. Anyone planning around wildfire then faces a practical question: which parts of that picture are current enough and complete enough to support a particular decision?

## How light becomes a landscape

Lidar stands for light detection and ranging. An instrument aboard an aircraft fires rapid pulses of laser light toward the ground and times each reflection's return. Light travels at a known speed, so the timing gives a distance. Combined with the aircraft's precise position, that distance places each reflection at a spot in three-dimensional space. Some light bounces off leaves at the top of a tree. Some slips through gaps and returns from lower branches or from the soil beneath. A survey produces a *point cloud*: an enormous collection of located dots marking ground, vegetation and buildings.

A point cloud is raw evidence. To make it comparable from place to place, researchers sort the dots and convert them into grids. Picture turning a scatter of pebbles into a tiled floor where each tile holds one number. Three grids matter most here. A *digital terrain model*, or DTM, estimates the bare ground, as if every tree and building had been lifted away. A *digital surface model*, or DSM, records the uppermost surface the laser met: treetops, roofs, and open ground wherever the land lies bare. Subtract the terrain from the surface and a height layer emerges. The dataset calls it nDSM/CHM, short for normalized digital surface model or canopy height model. In a forest, that difference approximates tree height.

Each step involves interpretation. Sorting ground dots from leaf dots is a classification task. Where a thin scattering of pulses reached the soil, software estimates the ground by filling in between nearby points, a process called *interpolation*. The USGS treats lidar points as the measurements and elevation models as products derived from them. A bare-ground model removes vegetation by design, so on its own it describes the soil surface and leaves the trees to other layers.

As an illustration, imagine mapping a neighbourhood on stacked sheets of tracing paper. On the bottom sheet, you trace streets and yards, sketching the pavement even where a parked van covers it. On the next sheet, you trace the tops of everything: van roofs, house roofs, the crowns of street trees. Lay one sheet over the other, measure the gap, and you have a rough height for each object. Now suppose different blocks were traced in different years, and a few squares stayed smudged where the pencil had faint marks to follow. The stack lets you compare blocks far more easily than a pile of separate sketches would. It also prompts you to check the date in each block's corner before trusting what you see. California's new maps work on the same principle; the real dataset estimates heights cell by cell across a grid, subject to the classification limits described below.

The project drew on existing material. The team gathered about 40 terabytes of publicly available USGS 3DEP airborne point clouds from 118 separate survey campaigns over California. A terabyte is a million megabytes, so the collection dwarfs the entire storage of a typical laptop. The researchers wrote open-source workflows in the R and Python programming languages and ran them on NASA supercomputing resources, processing the surveys into one consistent family of products. The maps announced in November are newly processed products built from flights made during 2013–2023.

That scale is a genuine accomplishment. Each campaign was planned and flown on its own schedule with its own settings. Previously, someone studying a stretch of California that crossed several survey boundaries had to piece the surveys together. The archive now offers a common framework: the same product types on the same grids, processed the same way, across a large share of the state.

How large a share deserves a precise answer. NASA's article describes maps for California in broad terms, the natural shorthand of a news story. The user guide gives the exact figure: 331,804 square kilometres of *valid acquisition coverage*, about 78.3 percent of California's land area. Valid acquisition coverage means ground where usable lidar surveys exist in this collection. Roughly a fifth of the state's land lies outside this version. Both descriptions refer to the same work, and the guide supplies the denominator. For planning, the guide's figure is the useful one: the maps form a statewide framework across the available footprint, a large and detailed share of the state with clear edges.

## Reading the fine print of the grid

The guide's product table lists maps at three grid sizes: 1 metre, 10 metres and 30 metres. The finest products use square cells about one metre on each side, so each cell covers one square metre of ground. That number describes how finely the grid divides the land. The accuracy of the height stored in a cell is a separate property. So is the correctness of the software's call about ground or tree within that cell. The guide addresses both through its quality assessment and its stated limitations. Each deserves its own check against that assessment and, for weighty decisions, against measurements taken on the ground.

The product list also varies by grid size. At one metre, the table offers terrain and elevation products: the DTM, the DSM, slope and aspect. *Slope* describes how steep the ground is. *Aspect* describes which direction a slope faces, such as a hillside facing north or one facing south. Both describe the land's geometry. A fire analyst might examine them, and forecasting how a fire behaves calls for further inputs. For vegetation structure, the one-metre product is the height layer, nDSM/CHM.

The richer description of vegetation appears at 10 and 30 metres. A 10-metre cell covers 100 square metres, about the floor area of a modest house. A 30-metre cell covers 900 square metres, roughly three and a half tennis courts. Cells that size gather many laser returns, enough to summarize what grows there. The coarser grids include canopy-cover measures built from those returns. Each one gives the percentage of a cell's laser returns that came back from a chosen height band: 2 to 4 metres, 4 to 8 metres, or 2 metres and above. Some cover layers count among all valid vegetation-related returns, others among first returns, and the processing applies a weighting between buildings and vegetation. Cover therefore records how often the laser met vegetation at those heights, a return-based view of how densely plants fill each layer. The grids also include height percentiles, which summarize first returns, the first echo from each pulse and usually the topmost surface it met. Line up a cell's first-return heights above the ground from lowest to highest; the 95th percentile, for example, is the height that 95 percent of them fall beneath. The guide lists percentiles from the 10th to the 99th. Percentiles help separate a stand of tall trees from a patch of low shrubs. These coarser grids also carry two bookkeeping layers: the year each area was surveyed, and the fraction of each cell occupied by buildings.

Read precisely, the suite offers ground shape and vegetation height at one metre, and a fuller portrait of vegetation structure at 10 and 30 metres. A summary describing California's vegetation in one-metre detail stretches the finest grid across measures that live on coarser ones.

Time adds a second layer of fine print. Where two surveys overlap, the processing keeps the most recent available acquisition. Across the whole footprint, dates still range from 2013 to 2023. NASA's article includes a point-cloud illustration of a wildland–urban interface in Santa Clara County, where homes meet open wildland. Its caption dates that data to 2020. That year belongs to that picture. Neighbouring places on the statewide mosaic may date from an earlier or later flight, and the acquisition-year layer at 10 and 30 metres lets a user see which.

A cell's value is an estimate from the year of its survey. Since then, vegetation has grown, burned, or been cleared in places, and houses have been built or removed. A cell surveyed in 2014 describes 2014. By late 2026, that observation is about 12 years old, and the earliest 2013 observations are about 13 years old. Even the newest 2023 surveys describe the land as it stood roughly three years ago.

The guide also lists processing limitations. Gaps and interpolation artifacts occur in small water bodies and in areas with sparse returns or limited ground returns. The maps may contain outliers, individual values far from those of their neighbours. They may also show flightline artifacts, faint seams or stripes that follow the path an aircraft flew. In the 10- and 30-metre vegetation products, some rooftop signals remain after the processing masked known buildings. The building reference used for that masking dates from 2014–2018, while the lidar spans 2013–2023. One plausible consequence is easy to picture: a structure surveyed in a different year from the building reference could register in a vegetation layer as a tall, flat object. The guide documents these limitations; the frequency of each problem at a given location calls for a look at that location's data.

## From a map to a decision

NASA's article says the agency is working with the California Natural Resources Agency and the California Air Resources Board. The agency hopes to develop products for wildfire risk assessment, wildlife and ecosystem protection, and resource management. The user guide describes similar applications. These are stated aims and ongoing collaboration. The published materials document the maps and their intended uses; measured effects on fires, evacuations and losses remain open questions.

Static maps of terrain and vegetation structure also describe one part of wildfire risk. Weather on a given day, how dry the plants are, where a spark might start, and where people and homes sit all need their own information. The new layers can feed models that combine those factors. How much they improve such models remains a question for testing.

A hypothetical comparison shows how the maps could sharpen a question. Imagine two hillsides that look alike on a coarse regional map. The one-metre slope and height layers might reveal that one has steeper ground and taller, more continuous vegetation near its base. That difference invites attention. Before leaning on it, a planner would open the acquisition-year layer and might find one hillside surveyed in 2014 and the other in 2022. The planner would check for gaps or interpolation scars, look at the building-fraction layer for possible rooftop effects, and send someone to compare the map with what stands there today. In this imagined case, the map points the inquiry, and the field visit settles it. The scenario illustrates a possible workflow; it describes a method, separate from any actual agency decision or treatment.

Validation would test the same chain at larger scale. Researchers could compare mapped heights with heights measured by field crews across different terrain and forest types. They could run a risk model twice, once including the new layers and once leaving them out, and see which version better matches what fires later did. Each test would turn a promising input into measured evidence, place by place.

This account draws on NASA's report, the archive's catalog and user guide, and a USGS explainer on lidar. The data files and any results from applying them await independent checking.

California now holds a common, detailed three-dimensional framework for about 78 percent of its land, built from 118 survey campaigns that once sat apart. Its acquisition-year layer helps identify the cells surveyed long ago, and its building-fraction layer shows where structures share the grid with vegetation. Interpolated patches and places where a roof might pass for a tree appear in the guide as general processing limitations; assessing them at a given site calls for local inspection of the data. For any planner holding a decision about a particular hillside, the suspense comes down to one square of the grid. When was it last seen, and how much has the land changed since?
