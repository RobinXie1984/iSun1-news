# Maryland’s Wagner Unit 4 Can Be Called On Until New Year’s Eve. What Happens Then?

An oil-fired power unit in Maryland has received another temporary federal authorization to help keep electricity flowing while its regional grid operator faces a delivery problem. On November 17, 2026, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright issued an emergency order covering Unit 4 at the H. A. Wagner Generating Station in Anne Arundel County. The Department of Energy sets the order’s conditions; PJM Interconnection coordinates power generation and transmission across the regional grid; a Talen subsidiary owns and operates the unit. Their immediate concern is whether electricity can reach customers reliably as the grid adjusts to planned plant retirements.

That concern involves two parts of electricity service that usually disappear behind a working light switch. Power stations produce electricity. Transmission lines carry it across the network toward the places that use it. Reliable service depends on both, including how the network behaves when equipment becomes unavailable or demand places it under strain. A region’s total generating capacity tells only part of that story. The location of a generator, and the network surrounding it, can determine how useful its output becomes during particular conditions.

Wagner Unit 4 brings a further complication: physical generating capability and legal operating permission have separate limits. PJM describes the unit as commissioned in 1972, with a summer-rated capacity of about 397 megawatts. Its applicable environmental framework allows 438 hours of operation annually while burning fuel oil. In its October 23 renewal application, PJM estimated that zero permitted hours remained. The federal renewal creates conditional authority for operation from November 18 through December 31.

Those figures describe three different things: the rate at which a unit is rated to deliver power, its ordinary annual oil-burning allowance, and the dates during which special authority applies. Understanding their relationship explains why a power station can remain relevant to grid planning after its ordinary operating allowance has been exhausted.

A megawatt measures a rate of power delivery. Energy is the amount delivered over time. As a purely illustrative calculation, a generator delivering one megawatt steadily for one hour produces one megawatt-hour of energy. Wagner’s roughly 397-megawatt summer rating describes capability under specified conditions. Actual production depends on operating conditions, the output requested and the time spent running. Its summer rating also requires care in a story about winter availability: the figure carries the seasonal qualification PJM gave it.

The 438-hour limit belongs to a different accounting system. It comes through an environmental consent-order and state implementation-plan framework. PJM’s zero-hours estimate describes the remaining regulatory allowance under that framework. Fuel inventory, equipment condition and physical generating capability each require their own evidence. An operating-hours account measures permission; an engineering assessment measures what the machinery can do.

Likewise, the renewal’s 44 calendar days, counting November 18 and December 31, define a period of conditional authority. A dispatch instruction—a grid operator’s direction to run a generating unit—determines when that authority becomes actual operation. Output records then establish how much electricity was produced. Each step answers a distinct question.

The story behind this renewal began years earlier. According to PJM’s application, Talen announced in October 2023 that it intended to retire Wagner Units 1, 3 and 4 and CT1, targeting June 1, 2025. PJM says its January 2024 response warned that deactivating Units 3 and 4 could affect transmission reliability unless upgrades were made. It subsequently sought Unit 4’s continued availability into the 2026–27 winter.

This is a chronology of a retirement proposal, a grid operator’s warning and requests for continued availability. The retirement date was a target. The reliability concern was a forecast about how the system could behave. Those distinctions matter because a forecast can justify preparation while its assumptions and predicted consequences remain open to examination.

A familiar delivery problem helps explain the underlying concern. Imagine, hypothetically, a district supplied by several warehouses. Most stock sits beyond a main road whose carrying capacity becomes constrained at certain times. A smaller warehouse inside the district can still be valuable because it places supplies closer to the people who need them. Keeping that warehouse available could allow deliveries to continue while the road is improved or another dependable supply route is arranged.

In this illustration, warehouses represent available generation and roads represent transmission capability. Electricity adds an important complication: its flows follow the physical properties of an interconnected network. A grid operator influences those flows by changing generation and other operating conditions; individual parcels of electricity follow the network’s physics. The analogy explains why location matters. Establishing Wagner’s role on a particular day requires the actual grid conditions and an engineering assessment.

That is why the retirement question extends beyond replacing a number on a capacity chart. Another generator could offer comparable power while affecting the network differently because of its location. A transmission improvement could change how much power reaches an area from elsewhere. Reducing local demand could ease the amount that must be delivered. The relevant test is how each option addresses the particular electrical constraint.

DOE’s chronology shows that temporary federal authority was already in place before this latest decision. A May 21 order covered May 22 through August 19. An August 19 renewal covered August 20 through November 17. The November 17 decision continued the sequence from November 18 through year-end. The succession of dates makes the transition itself part of the story: the unit’s temporary role has persisted across several authorization periods.

The latest order offers two routes for dispatch. Paragraph A covers declared or anticipated Maximum Generation Alert or Transmission Security Emergency conditions. These are formal grid-operating conditions concerned with obtaining generation or protecting transmission reliability. The inclusion of anticipated conditions gives the provision a forward-looking role: operators may act in preparation for a developing problem.

For circumstances beyond that route’s coverage, the order also permits dispatch consistent with a settlement accepted by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, the federal wholesale-power regulator known as FERC. PJM describes the settlement as covering identified transmission-reliability needs that economic resources cannot meet, system restoration, capacity emergencies and required testing.

Testing deserves attention because it changes how an eventual operating record should be read. A test can establish whether equipment performs as required before a more demanding situation arrives. Restoration concerns bringing a power system back into service. A capacity emergency concerns the availability of enough generating capability. Each purpose creates a different context for interpreting the same outward event: the unit running.

The authorization therefore requires more careful reading than a simple emergency label provides. An operating hour could fall under an anticipated alert or under the settlement’s provisions, including required testing. To understand why the unit ran, a reader would need the corresponding dispatch reason and conditions. The legal route, operating purpose and electricity produced belong together in the eventual account.

DOE also attaches controls to that use. The order directs operation for necessary hours and calls for minimizing costs to ratepayers—the customers who ultimately pay electricity bills. It retains operating parameters and requirements governing cessation when relevant events or alerts end. If PJM relies on the order to operate the unit, it must notify DOE the following business day, including the level by which an applicable limit was exceeded.

That reporting requirement creates a potential link between broad emergency authority and specific actions. A notice can identify a use of the authority and the extent of an exceedance. Read alongside dispatch instructions and operating records, it could help establish how the permission was exercised. Assessing the reliability benefit would require an additional step: examining what the grid was facing and how the unit’s operation affected it.

Environmental obligations also remain part of the arrangement. DOE requires compliance with applicable law to the maximum extent practicable. Obligations involving emission fees, offsets or allowance purchases remain relevant, as do available regulatory flexibilities. DOE may request environmental information, and specified compliance obligations extend beyond the authorization window. An offset or allowance is an instrument used within an emissions-control system; its precise role depends on the applicable rules.

These provisions establish duties. Their practical consequences depend on operation and subsequent accounting. Necessary-hours language constrains use, while dispatch records show its duration. Cost-minimization language establishes an objective, while charges and the alternatives available at the time help reveal the financial result. Environmental provisions preserve obligations, while emissions measurements and compliance records show what occurred.

The public-health reasoning follows the same distinction between an official risk judgment and a measured outcome. DOE finds a statutory emergency and a public-health risk from a possible electricity shortfall. PJM warns of limited conditions in which load shedding could become necessary. Load shedding means deliberately reducing electricity service to some customers to protect the wider system. It is a serious potential consequence, and the application presents it as a conditional risk.

Electricity supports heating, communications and many services that households and institutions depend on. That dependence explains why grid operators prepare for stressful conditions before an interruption occurs. Judging the value of a particular precaution, however, requires a comparison between how the system performed and how it would likely have performed under the same conditions with other resources available.

This comparison can be difficult. A quiet period could reflect favorable conditions, effective preparation or several factors together. A unit’s operation could provide essential support, serve a testing requirement or address a smaller operational need. The reported purpose and contemporaneous grid data would help distinguish these possibilities. Credit for preventing an outage requires engineering evidence connecting the unit’s contribution to the threatened interruption.

The available account establishes the order’s authority, PJM’s stated rationale and the renewal chronology. Actual operation after November 18, ratepayer costs, emissions and the detailed grid-model results remain unverified here. The full settlement and individual dispatch decisions also require separate examination. These boundaries leave a clear subject for follow-up: what the temporary permission accomplished in practice.

There are two timescales to that question. One concerns each operating event during the 44-day window: why the unit was called, how long it ran and what conditions it addressed. The other concerns the transition across months and years: what changed so that the grid could eventually meet the same reliability need through a durable arrangement.

Transmission upgrades are one possible avenue because they can alter the network’s ability to deliver power. Demand response, in which customers adjust electricity use when requested or under agreed arrangements, could reduce pressure during particular periods. Storage can shift previously generated electricity to a later time, subject to its power rating, stored energy and location. Other generation could contribute according to where it connects and how dependably it performs.

These are possibilities for investigation. A useful substitute would need to address the identified constraint, perform during the relevant conditions and become available on a credible schedule. A project’s headline capacity alone leaves its local electrical effect unresolved. The same is true of an announced completion target: evidence of construction, testing and readiness gives the date practical meaning.

For Wagner, dated project milestones would make the phrase “buying time” measurable. A completed upgrade, a tested replacement arrangement or a demonstrated reduction in the relevant constraint could show that the temporary period supported a transition. Further reliance on Unit 4 would call for an account of the remaining need, the work completed and the reasons the dependency continued.

December 31 marks the end of this order’s dispatch-authority window. The condition of the grid on that date will be an engineering fact established through equipment, operating arrangements and system studies. As the calendar approaches that boundary, the most revealing question will reach beyond how often Wagner Unit 4 ran: what changed in the electricity network while it remained available?
