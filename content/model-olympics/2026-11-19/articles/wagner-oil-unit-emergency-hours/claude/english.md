# PJM Says Wagner 4's Oil Hours Are Spent. Why Did Washington Extend Its Emergency Permission Until New Year's Eve?

On November 17, 2026, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright signed an emergency order about a single machine: Unit 4 at the H.A. Wagner Generating Station in Anne Arundel County, Maryland. Three players share this story. The Department of Energy, usually called DOE, is the federal government's energy department. Under the Federal Power Act, its secretary can order electricity generators to run during emergencies. PJM Interconnection is the regional grid operator for a large territory that includes Maryland. It coordinates which power plants run and keeps the high-voltage transmission lines, the long-distance wires of the grid, within safe limits. PJM operates the system, while a subsidiary of Talen owns and operates the plant. The new order took effect November 18 and runs through December 31. Under conditions the order spells out, it lets Unit 4 operate beyond a yearly cap on the hours it may burn fuel oil.

The order is a renewal. DOE's dated record shows an earlier order covering May 22 through August 19, followed by a renewal covering August 20 through November 17. The November decision picks up the next day. This is the third chapter of a continuing arrangement for one aging unit. Each chapter has been a federal decision to keep a familiar piece of equipment available a little longer, and this one carries it to the start of winter.

## A rated machine and a spent allowance

Unit 4 is an oil-fired generator. PJM's October 23 application to DOE says it entered service in 1972, which makes it about 54 years old. The application gives its summer rating as roughly 397 megawatts. A megawatt measures speed of production: how much electricity the unit can deliver at a given moment when it runs at its rated level. The total electricity produced over time is a separate quantity, measured in megawatt-hours, and it depends on how long the unit runs and how hard. The 397-megawatt figure describes rated capability. Actual output, and the number of homes the unit actually supplied, would come from operating records.

That brings us to the puzzle at the heart of the order. Unit 4 operates under an environmental framework built from two pieces. One is a consent order, a binding agreement with regulators. The other is Maryland's state implementation plan, the state's official plan for meeting federal air-quality standards. Under that framework, the unit may ordinarily burn fuel oil for up to 438 hours a year. In its October 23 filing, PJM estimated that zero of those permitted hours remained. DOE's order also refers to an earlier PJM estimate from August 4.

"Zero hours remaining" sounds like an empty tank, but it means something different. The 438 hours are a permission budget set by environmental rules. What has run out, by PJM's estimate, is the ordinary legal allowance to burn oil for the rest of the year. Fuel supplies and the condition of the equipment are separate matters, documented in the plant's own records. The estimate is PJM's own figure from its filings. An independent, up-to-date count of Unit 4's running hours likewise belongs to the plant's operating records.

By PJM's estimate, the ordinary oil hours were exhausted. The two earlier DOE orders had already provided limited, conditional authority for the unit to run beyond them, and the most recent of those expired on November 17. With the ordinary allowance spent and the emergency authority carrying an end date, the question went back to Washington. PJM's application describes the unit and asks for its continued availability; how ready the unit is on any given day is a matter for operating records.

## Why one old unit matters

The deeper story begins in October 2023. According to PJM's application, Talen announced that month that it intended to retire several pieces of equipment at Wagner: Units 1, 3 and 4, and a combustion turbine called CT1. The target date was June 1, 2025. Retirement is a normal stage in a power plant's life. The owner gives notice, and the grid operator studies how the system will behave once the equipment is gone.

According to the application, PJM warned in January 2024 that shutting down Units 3 and 4 could harm the reliability of the transmission system unless upgrades came first. The application presents that warning as a forecast about risk; the detailed studies behind it lie beyond this account. Talen's 2023 announcement was likewise a statement of intent. The 2026 orders show that Unit 4 remained part of the picture well after its target retirement date. PJM has now asked for it to stay available into the winter of 2026–27.

Why would one 1972-vintage unit matter on a grid that stretches across a wide region? The answer lies in the difference between producing electricity and delivering it. A large grid can hold plenty of generation in total. A particular area can still face trouble if specific lines or paths serving it approach their safe carrying limits. Depending on how the network is laid out and on conditions at the moment, a plant at one location can relieve strain on a particular constrained line, while a plant elsewhere may add little relief to that same line. PJM's application describes the case for Unit 4 in these terms, as a transmission reliability concern tied to location. Judging how long Unit 4 may be required would call for the underlying power-flow studies and any timetable for upgrades.

A hypothetical comparison can help, and it is offered only to illustrate the idea. Picture a town whose main bridge is under repair. The town also keeps a narrow side road open under a permit that allows a set number of hours of use each year. On most days, traffic flows over other routes. On a few difficult evenings, when everything is jammed, the open side road could keep emergency vehicles moving. The permit's hour limit and the town's use log both matter, because the side road brings its own costs in noise, wear and fumes. Once the permit hours are used up, the town council has to decide whether to extend them until the bridge reopens.

The comparison has firm limits. Grid operators influence electricity by choosing which plants run, and the current then spreads across every available path according to the physical properties of the wires. Cars follow the roads their drivers pick. The town story captures the logic of keeping a backup route open. The real flows on Maryland's wires belong to PJM's engineering studies.

## When Unit 4 may run

Paragraph A of the order defines when Unit 4 may run past its hour limit, and it opens two routes.

The first route covers two named kinds of PJM emergency conditions. A Maximum Generation Alert is a PJM warning that the grid may need nearly every available generator to meet demand. A Transmission Security Emergency is a warning that the wires in some part of the system are nearing their safe limits. The order allows Unit 4 to run when either condition is declared, and also when PJM anticipates one. That word "anticipated" matters. It gives operators room to prepare ahead of a forecast crunch, before any formal announcement.

The second route applies to situations the alerts leave uncovered. In those cases, Unit 4 may run in a manner consistent with a settlement accepted by FERC, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, which regulates wholesale electricity markets. PJM's application quotes the relevant section of that settlement. As PJM presents it, the settlement covers four purposes:

- identified transmission reliability needs that the generation available through economic dispatch, the grid's routine market-based selection of which plants run, is unable to meet
- restoring the system after a blackout
- capacity emergencies, when supply runs short
- required testing of the unit

Testing deserves a moment. A generator held in reserve has to run from time to time, both to confirm that it works and to satisfy the rules for units kept for reliability. The settlement route, as PJM describes it, accounts for that need.

Together, the two routes give the authority real substance. Unit 4 can respond to forecast trouble as well as declared trouble, and it can run for specific reliability and testing purposes. The authority also has clear edges. It covers named conditions and named purposes, and ordinary day-to-day selling of power into the market falls outside its scope. Further examination of three records would help assess how the authority works in practice: the complete settlement text, the grid simulations behind PJM's request, and the record of each decision to call on the unit.

## The rules that come with the permission

Paragraphs B through H of the order attach conditions, and they fall into three layers.

The first layer is operating discipline. Unit 4 is to run only for the hours necessary to address the emergency. PJM is directed to keep costs to ratepayers, the ordinary customers who ultimately pay for electricity, as low as possible. When the alerts or emergency conditions that justified a run come to an end, the order's parameters call for the unit to stop operating under that authority.

The second layer is reporting. Each time PJM relies on the order to run Unit 4, it must notify DOE on the following business day, including how far the unit exceeded its usual limits. That creates a paper trail showing when the special permission was used and by how much.

The third layer is environmental. The order directs the unit to comply with applicable laws and regulations to the maximum extent practicable throughout the window. Emission fees still apply. So do purchases of emission offsets or allowances, which are credits that let a plant account for its pollution under various programs, along with other available compliance flexibilities. DOE may request environmental information about the unit's operation. Some compliance obligations under paragraph E continue past December 31, and paragraph H carves those obligations out of the order's date window. After the order ends, applicable law continues to govern the unit.

Put plainly, the order loosens one specific cap, the 438-hour oil limit, for a defined purpose and period. It keeps the surrounding rules in place as far as practical. These conditions are requirements. Whether they actually held down costs or emissions is a question for the operating, billing and environmental records.

## An emergency on paper, a risk in the forecast

To use this power, DOE has to find that an emergency exists. In the order, DOE finds a statutory emergency, meaning one that meets the definition in federal law. It also finds a risk to public health from a shortfall in electricity supply. PJM's application warns that under certain limited conditions, the grid could need to shed load. Load shedding means deliberately switching off power to some customers for a time. It is a controlled measure that protects the rest of the system from a wider collapse.

These are official judgments and forecasts. DOE's finding is the government's legal determination. PJM's warning is the grid operator's assessment of risk. Both explain why the order exists. A numerical estimate of this winter's load-shedding risk would require further evidence. Evidence of any outage avoided would come from later operations.

The order also carries a legal note. Footnote 21 acknowledges a September 11 court decision interpreting section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act, the emergency provision behind this order. DOE states that it disagrees with the court's interpretation and that it petitioned for rehearing on October 26. It maintains that this order is consistent with the opinion. The footnote records a live legal dispute over how far the emergency power reaches. The later course of that case, and any court's view of this particular order, lie beyond the order's own pages.

## What would count as time well bought

An emergency order of this kind is meant to buy time for a more durable answer. For Wagner Unit 4, PJM's January 2024 warning, as the application describes it, tied the safe deactivation of Units 3 and 4 to transmission upgrades. Where those upgrades stand, and when they might finish, are matters for project records beyond the order and application. Other options often come up in discussions like this one. Demand response is an arrangement in which large users agree to cut consumption at peak moments. Battery storage and new generation near the constrained area are others. These are avenues worth examining. The order and application describe the Wagner arrangement itself, and the status of any particular project sits in other records.

The 44-day window is easy to misread. PJM asked for a renewal running from November 18 through December 31, and those dates describe when the special permission applies. They mark a calendar. Unit 4's actual running time within that window could be a few hours, many hours or zero, depending on weather, demand and the condition of the wires. The 438-hour figure stays what it has always been, the ordinary annual cap. The order grants permission tied to emergencies and listed purposes, measured by the hours each situation requires.

That makes the central question an empirical one, and several kinds of evidence could answer it:

- The next-business-day notices would show how often the unit ran under the order and how far it exceeded its limits.
- The circumstances behind each run would show whether alerts were declared or anticipated, and whether the settlement route or testing accounted for some of the hours.
- Cost records would show what ratepayers paid.
- Emissions and compliance records would show the environmental price.
- Progress reports on transmission upgrades would show whether the gap is closing.
- PJM's grid-model comparisons would show how much difference the unit makes, on paper, during a stressed hour.

Some of these records grow directly from the reporting duties the order creates. Others would come from PJM, FERC and Maryland's environmental regulators. Taken together, they would let the public judge the bargain on its merits. That bargain is a 54-year-old oil unit, its ordinary permitted hours spent by PJM's estimate, kept available under emergency authority through the longest nights of the year.

In the hypothetical town, a council extending the side-road permit a third time would naturally ask how close the bridge is to reopening. Maryland's grid faces a version of that question as December 31 approaches: what has the borrowed time built, and how near is the durable answer it was meant to reach?
