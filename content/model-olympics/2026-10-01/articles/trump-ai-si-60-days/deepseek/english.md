The Name Game: What Trump's "Super Intelligence" Order Actually Does — and Doesn't Do

An analysis of the September 29, 2026 executive order.

On September 29, 2026, President Donald Trump signed an executive order with a deceptively simple premise: the words we use to describe technology shape how we think about it. The order directs U.S. executive departments and agencies, to the extent allowed by law, to replace "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" with "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in official communications, websites, reports, and other non-statutory documents-
1
.

It sounds like a rebrand. In many ways, it is. But beneath the surface lies a more interesting story — one about legal definitions, bureaucratic language, and the gap between what a government says and what a technology actually does.

Let me be clear from the start: this order is evidence of a naming policy, not scientific proof that machines have achieved superintelligence-
1
. No benchmarks were released. No models were upgraded overnight. The order itself acknowledges that "Super Intelligence" and "SI," for the purposes of this directive, mean the same technologies already defined in 15 U.S.C. § 9401(3) as artificial intelligence — "a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments"-
.

So what's really happening here?

The Policy vs. the Law

The order is an executive-branch instruction, not a ban on everyone using AI. It applies to how federal agencies talk, write, and label things. It does not require the alteration of earlier regulations, presidential actions, contracts, grants, or historical documents-
1
. If a contract signed in 2024 says "AI," that contract still says "AI." The order doesn't reach back in time.

More importantly, it doesn't change the law. The statutory definition of artificial intelligence remains intact. The order explicitly states that the SI definition "shall govern the implementation of this order unless and until superseded by subsequent Presidential action consistent with applicable law or by an Act of Congress"-
1
. In plain terms: this is a naming convention, not a legal redefinition.

The president's science and technology assistant must submit proposed legislative language for a federal SI definition within 60 days of the order-
1
. That proposal, when it comes, is just that — a proposal. It is not an enacted law. This is not a 60-day deadline for machines to become smarter. It's a 60-day deadline for a staffer to draft some words.

Why Words Matter — and Why They Don't

The accompanying White House fact sheet frames the change as recognizing technological progress-
2
. The administration's stated rationale is that the term "Artificial Intelligence" implies something fake or imitative, while today's frontier systems "increasingly amplify human ingenuity and unlock new forms of creativity"-
1
. The fact sheet argues that "Super Intelligence" better conveys "the true capabilities of the technologies being developed today"-
2
.

This is a philosophical claim dressed up as a linguistic one. And it's worth taking seriously — up to a point.

Language does shape perception. When the government calls something "artificial," it primes citizens to think of it as less than real, less than human, less than trustworthy. When it calls something "super," it primes them to think of it as more than human, more than capable, more than safe. The same technology, two different stories.

But here's the counter-intuitive twist: renaming something doesn't change what it can do. A large language model that hallucinates a citation on Tuesday will still hallucinate that citation on Wednesday, regardless of whether the federal register calls it AI or SI. The order itself is careful to note that the definition of "Super Intelligence" remains bound to the existing statutory AI definition unless and until Congress acts-
1
.

The Bureaucratic Reality

There's also a practical dimension that's easy to overlook. The order directs agencies to "no longer acknowledge the usage of 'Artificial Intelligence' or 'AI'" in applicable settings-
2
. That's an unusually strong phrasing for an executive order — not just "use the new term" but "don't acknowledge the old one."

In practice, this creates a peculiar situation for federal employees. A scientist at the Department of Energy writing a report on machine learning must now call it "Super Intelligence." A procurement officer drafting a contract for a predictive analytics system must use "SI." The technology hasn't changed. The paperwork has.

Does this matter? For the federal government's internal coherence, yes. For the technology itself, no. For the public's understanding, it's an open question. If the government insists on calling AI "Super Intelligence," and the systems themselves haven't changed, does the public become more impressed — or more confused?

What the Order Doesn't Say

The order does not claim a new benchmark. It does not impose a nationwide speech ban. It does not create criminal penalties for using the term "AI." It does not mandate an overnight model upgrade. It does not enact a statutory redefinition of artificial intelligence. No documented cost is established by these sources. No completed agency compliance has been reported. No private motive has been documented.

These are boundaries, not criticisms. The order is what it is: a directive about nomenclature, issued by a president who has long understood that branding is its own form of power.

The 60-Day Question

The most consequential part of the order may be the 60-day requirement. Within two months, the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology must deliver proposed legislative language that would establish a federal definition of "Super Intelligence" and "SI" that reflects "the capabilities described in section 1" of the order-
1
.

That proposal must include an assessment of whether the new definition should modify, expand upon, or supersede the existing statutory definition of AI, along with proposed conforming amendments to existing statutory references-
1
.

This is where the naming game meets the law. If Congress takes up the proposal and passes it, "Super Intelligence" could become a legally operative term, not just a rhetorical flourish. If Congress doesn't, the order remains what it started as: a language policy with no statutory teeth.

The Takeaway

Trump's "Super Intelligence" order is less a scientific statement than a political one. It says: we believe these technologies are extraordinary, and we want the government's vocabulary to reflect that belief. The order does not prove that superintelligence exists. It does not change what machines can do. It does not override existing law. What it does is stake a claim — about progress, about optimism, about America's place in a technological race it intends to win.

Whether that claim is vindicated depends not on the words in the order, but on what happens in laboratories, in Congress, and in the 60 days that follow.
