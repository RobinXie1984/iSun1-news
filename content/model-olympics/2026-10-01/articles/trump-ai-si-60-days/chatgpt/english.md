Three headline options

Suspenseful: Trump Ordered Washington to Replace “AI” With “SI.” The Crucial Detail Is in Section 3.
Emotional Resonance: Washington Is Renaming AI. The Rest of Us Still Need to Know What We Can Trust.
Counter-intuitive: “Super Intelligence” Is Becoming an Official Label—Without Requiring Smarter Machines.

The Government Can Rename AI. It Cannot Rename the Evidence.

The most consequential sentence in a presidential order is not always the one that announces a new era.

Sometimes, it is the sentence that tells you what has actually changed.

On September 29, 2026, President Donald Trump signed an order directing U.S. executive departments and agencies, to the extent permitted by law, to replace “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI” with “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in official communications, websites, reports and other non-statutory documents.

That is a striking instruction. A technology already wrapped in extraordinary expectations is getting an even more extraordinary official name.

But turn to Section 3(a), and the story becomes more interesting.

For purposes of the order, “SI” presently covers the same technologies included in the existing statutory definition of AI under 15 USC 9401(3), unless the law provides otherwise.

The terminology changes. The technologies covered, under that provision, remain the same.

There is no capability test embedded in that substitution. No new benchmark result follows from the signature. No scientific finding establishes that the machines being renamed have crossed into superintelligence.

Washington has ordered a change in vocabulary.

Whether the vocabulary describes a technological breakthrough is a separate question—and this order does not answer it.

That distinction is the center of the story.

Because a government can change what it calls a machine far more quickly than anyone can establish what the machine can do.

The accompanying White House fact sheet frames the change as recognition of technological progress. That is the administration’s stated rationale. It should be reported as such.

It should not be silently converted into an independently verified assessment of machine capability.

The difference may seem technical. It is anything but.

One statement tells us how an administration wants to describe technology. The other would tell us something demonstrated about the technology itself. Confusing the two turns an act of government into a claim about reality that the act does not substantiate.

And this particular name carries weight.

“Super Intelligence” invites a reader to hear superiority: something beyond familiar limits, perhaps beyond human performance. Whatever definition lawmakers might eventually adopt, the phrase arrives with expectations already attached.

That is an observation about the language, not a claim about the president’s private intentions.

The sources supplied for this story do not establish a private motive. They establish an instruction, its present scope, a forthcoming proposal and an official explanation.

Those facts are enough to support a substantial story.

They do not need a fictional backroom conversation to make them dramatic.

Consider what the order actually asks executive departments and agencies to do. Their official communications and specified non-statutory materials are to use the new terminology, subject to the law.

If implemented as directed, readers could encounter “SI” in federal material where they would previously have encountered “AI.”

The possibility is easy to understand. The implications require more care.

A reader might reasonably assume a new term marks a new category. Yet under this order’s present definition, the term covers the same technologies as the existing statutory AI definition, unless law provides otherwise.

That creates a potential gap between legal coverage and public interpretation.

The label sounds like a promotion. The operative definition preserves continuity.

To illustrate the distinction—not to report a documented agency action—imagine a federal report discussing a system that already falls within the statutory AI definition. Substituting “SI” for “AI” under the order would not, by itself, demonstrate any improvement in that system.

Its reliability would remain an empirical question. So would its limitations.

A new noun does not supply a new test result.

This is why the smallest qualification in the story may do the most work: for purposes of the order.

The order establishes how a term is used within its own framework. It does not settle every scientific or public debate about the meaning of superintelligence.

Nor does it establish that every covered technology satisfies whatever more demanding meaning a reader associates with the phrase.

Words can have defined meanings in government documents. Readers also bring everyday meanings to them. Trouble becomes possible when those meanings travel together without explanation.

There is another attention-grabbing element: a 60-day deadline.

Section 3(b) directs the president’s science and technology assistant to submit proposed legislative language for a federal definition of SI within 60 days.

That is a concrete instruction with a concrete time frame.

It is also easy to misread.

The clock is running on a legislative proposal. It is not running on a requirement that machines become more intelligent.

The deadline does not mean superintelligence must be achieved in 60 days. It does not mean a new statutory definition automatically takes effect when the period ends. It does not turn the requested language into enacted law.

A proposal is a proposal.

The distinction matters because deadlines lend stories momentum. They suggest a countdown toward an arrival: a launch, a transformation, an irreversible threshold.

Here, the specified deliverable is text.

Potentially consequential text, certainly. A proposed federal definition could become an important subject of debate. But the supplied material does not establish its eventual wording, whether it will be enacted, or what legal consequences any enacted version might carry.

The calendar measures the drafting assignment. It does not measure the intelligence of the machines.

That leaves two developments to follow separately.

The first is the executive branch’s terminology instruction, operating within the order’s stated legal limits.

The second is the requested proposal for a statutory definition.

Collapsing them into one event would obscure the story. The first concerns an instruction already signed. The second concerns language still to be proposed.

Neither constitutes scientific proof of superintelligence.

The order’s limits deserve equal attention.

Section 2(b) does not require alteration of earlier regulations, presidential actions, contracts, grants or historical documents.

That provision narrows the picture considerably. This is not an instruction to rewrite every earlier federal reference to AI. It does not require the historical record to be scrubbed of the old terminology.

And it is an executive-branch instruction, not a nationwide prohibition on saying “AI.”

Nothing in the supplied brief establishes a speech ban applying to everyone, or a criminal penalty for using the familiar abbreviation.

A journalist can report the change without describing the old term as forbidden. A reader can discuss artificial intelligence without imagining that the order has outlawed the phrase.

Those boundaries are not footnotes to be discarded once the headline is written.

They are part of what the event is.

So why care about a terminology order at all?

Because official language helps structure public understanding.

That is the analytical case for taking this seriously, even while rejecting inflated descriptions of what happened.

A federal website is not simply a collection of words floating in isolation. Readers approach it as an institutional source. Terminology used there can shape what they think the government recognizes, endorses or considers established.

If a reader encounters “Super Intelligence” without the definitional explanation, the reader could infer a level of capability that the naming policy itself does not demonstrate.

That is a risk of interpretation, not a documented outcome in these sources.

It is also a reason for careful reporting.

The more impressive the label, the more important the accompanying explanation becomes.

A useful explanation would tell readers what technologies the term covers, whether it is being used as an administrative designation, and what evidence supports any separate claim about performance.

Otherwise, the name may end up doing work that evidence should do.

A label can announce ambition. It cannot certify achievement.

There is a reasonable way to understand the administration’s stated position without overstating it. The White House presents the change as recognition of technological progress. Governments do make choices about the language through which they communicate priorities and describe developments.

But accepting that this is the stated rationale does not require accepting that the terminology proves the underlying claim.

Progress and superintelligence are not interchangeable propositions merely because a fact sheet places them in the same narrative.

If a system has become more capable, that improvement can be evaluated on its own terms. What tasks does it perform? Under what conditions? How consistently? With what limitations?

Those questions remain relevant after the acronym changes.

Indeed, a more ambitious name makes precise answers more valuable.

For the public, the practical issue is trust.

People trying to understand an unfamiliar technology need to distinguish what an institution calls it from what has been established about it.

A system described in official material as SI might still require the same scrutiny it required under the AI label. The order alone provides no reason to infer that its outputs have become more dependable.

Nor does it provide evidence that familiar limitations have disappeared.

That does not mean the technology cannot improve. It means improvement must be demonstrated independently of the renaming.

The distinction protects serious technological progress as much as it protects skeptical readers. Genuine advances deserve explanations grounded in what changed, not arguments that depend on the prestige of a new term.

If the machines improve, show the improvement.

If the terminology changes, explain the terminology.

Both can be news. They are different kinds of news.

For executive agencies, the order introduces an implementation task. But the supplied sources do not establish a documented cost, the extent of completed compliance, or a finished government-wide transition.

It would therefore be premature to attach a price tag to the change or declare that every relevant website has already been updated.

An instruction establishes what has been directed. It does not, by itself, establish everything that has been done.

This distinction is especially important in a story likely to attract both celebration and ridicule. Supporters could describe the order as a declaration of technological leadership. Critics could treat it as an exercise in branding.

Those are interpretations.

The record supplied here supports a narrower and more useful account: the administration has directed a legally qualified terminology change, retained existing statutory AI coverage for the order’s present purposes, and requested proposed legislative language within 60 days.

The most revealing next development may be the proposed definition.

Will it preserve the current scope? Will it introduce different distinctions? How will it explain the relationship between “SI” and the existing statutory term?

The supplied sources do not yet answer those questions.

They should remain questions.

What would make that proposal consequential is its actual language and any subsequent legal action—not the dramatic possibilities a commentator can attach to an unwritten definition.

Meanwhile, anyone encountering the new term can apply a simple reading habit: ask what kind of claim is being made.

Is this an official naming convention? A proposed legal category? A claim about measured capability?

Then ask what evidence supports that particular claim.

The same two letters could otherwise become a shortcut across three very different subjects.

That shortcut is where confusion thrives.

September 29, 2026, is the date of the signed order described in this brief, which was verified on October 1, Hong Kong time. It is an identifiable moment in executive-branch naming policy.

It is not, on the strength of these sources, an identifiable date on which machines became superintelligent.

That larger threshold cannot be established by replacing words in a report.

The order can direct the executive branch’s vocabulary, within the law. The requested proposal can put a possible definition before policymakers. Evidence must still do the separate work of showing what the technology can accomplish.

The story is compelling precisely because these processes move on different tracks: official language, legal definition and demonstrated capability.

For now, the name has an instruction behind it. The proposed definition has a deadline ahead of it.

The machines still have to show their work.

When you see “Super Intelligence” on a government website, what would earn your trust: a clear legal definition, independently demonstrated capabilities, or both—and what would you need those demonstrations to show?
