The Day AI Became "Super Intelligence" on Paper

On September 29, 2026, the most powerful technology in the world got a new name.

No model was retrained, no benchmark was broken, and no lab announced a breakthrough. A presidential signature changed the word.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order titled Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence. It directs U.S. executive departments and agencies, to the extent allowed by law, to replace "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" with "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in official communications, websites, reports and other non-statutory documents.

The order's own text then says that "SI" presently covers the same technologies as the existing legal definition of AI.

The rename is real, and it is narrow. To understand what happened, you have to hold both of those facts at once.

Part One: What the Order Actually Says

This story invites exaggeration, so the facts come first, separated from interpretation. Everything in this section comes from the signed order (s1) and the White House fact sheet (s2), both dated September 29, 2026, and verified October 1, 2026, Hong Kong time.

Fact 1: The rename. Under Section 2, executive departments and agencies are directed to swap "AI" for "SI" in official communications, websites, reports and other non-statutory documents. The order qualifies this with "to the extent allowed by law."

Fact 2: The definition stays the same. Under Section 3(a), "SI" presently covers the same technologies as the existing statutory AI definition in 15 U.S.C. 9401(3), unless the law provides otherwise. Whatever counted as AI under federal law on September 28 counts as SI on September 30. The set of systems covered has not changed.

Fact 3: There is a 60-day assignment. Under Section 3(b), the president's science and technology assistant must submit proposed legislative language for a federal SI definition within 60 days.

Fact 4: The past is left alone. Under Section 2(b), the order does not require changes to earlier regulations, presidential actions, contracts, grants or historical documents.

Fact 5: The administration's stated reason. The accompanying fact sheet frames the change as recognition of technological progress. That is the administration's rationale. The sources don't present it as the result of an independent capability test.

The verified record contains those five things and nothing more.

Part Two: What the Order Does Not Do

Many people will read the headline and stop there, so the limits of the order matter as much as its contents.

It does not prove machines are superintelligent. In research circles, "superintelligence" usually means systems that substantially exceed human ability across most domains. This order is evidence of a naming policy. It is not a scientific finding, and it does not cite a new benchmark.

It does not ban anyone from saying "AI." The instruction applies to the executive branch. Companies, universities, journalists and the public are not covered. If you run a startup called "Something AI," nothing in this order requires you to rebrand.

It does not create a criminal penalty. None appears in the source material.

It does not redefine AI in law. The statutory definition still stands. The 60-day deliverable is a proposal for legislative language, and a proposal is not an enacted law. Changing the definition in statute would require Congress.

It does not give machines a deadline. The 60 days apply to a policy office drafting text. They do not apply to any model's capabilities.

It does not document costs, compliance or motives. The sources do not establish how much the rename will cost, whether any agency has already complied, or any private motive behind it. Any claims you see on those points go beyond the verified record.

Takeaway: A label is not a benchmark. Renaming a capability does not change what the system can do.

Part Three: Why a Word Change Is Still Worth Your Attention

From here on, this is my analysis, not source fact.

It would be easy to dismiss the order as cosmetic. I think that would be a mistake. Words shape institutions, and in technology policy they also shape money and responsibility.

1. Names set expectations

"Artificial intelligence" has been a slightly awkward phrase for 70 years. It promises something that sounds like a mind while describing software, and that gap has fueled cycles of hype and disappointment.

"Super Intelligence" makes a much stronger claim. The word super signals that the system exceeds us. Once government websites, agency reports and official briefings consistently use that term, the public's sense of what these systems can do may shift, even though the order says the underlying category hasn't changed.

That cuts two ways. Supporters could argue the new term better conveys how fast the field is moving and how seriously it should be treated. Skeptics could argue it raises expectations that today's systems can't meet, which might lead people to over-trust them in some settings or become cynical when they fail.

2. One technology, two vocabularies

Because the order only reaches non-statutory executive-branch documents and doesn't touch prior regulations, contracts or grants, the U.S. government could end up using two terms at once.

New press releases and reports would say "SI." Existing law would still say "AI," as would the regulations, contracts and grants already on the books. Private companies, foreign governments and international bodies have no obligation to switch.

My expectation is a transition period in which readers have to translate. A procurement officer might read "SI" in a new memo and "AI" in the contract it refers to and need to confirm they mean the same thing. Section 3(a) says they do, for now. The phrase "for now" is doing a lot of work there.

3. The real story is the 60-day draft

The rename gets attention, but I think the more consequential piece is Section 3(b).

At present, "SI" simply borrows the existing statutory AI definition. Within 60 days, the science and technology assistant is supposed to propose new legislative language for a federal SI definition. That raises questions the order doesn't answer:

Will the proposed definition stay as broad as the current AI definition, or narrow it?
Will it tie "SI" to measurable capability thresholds, or remain descriptive?
If Congress adopts some version, which existing AI rules, programs or obligations would carry over, and how?

None of this has happened yet. The order creates a draft deadline. Whether the draft becomes law depends on Congress.

Takeaway: The 60-day clock is running for policy drafters, not for the models.

Part Four: Two Ways to Read the Same Signature

Still analysis. I'm laying out the strongest form of each view, not choosing a side.

The case supporters could make

The administration's framing, according to the fact sheet, is that the change recognizes technological progress. Supporters might extend that argument like this:

Capabilities have moved fast, and a 1950s-era term undersells what current systems can do.
Language affects ambition. Calling the field "super intelligence" signals national priority and seriousness, possibly with an eye on global competition.
The order is legally careful. It doesn't rewrite old contracts, doesn't touch statute, and explicitly keeps the existing definition until Congress acts. In that view, it's a low-risk symbolic step paired with a substantive drafting task.
The case skeptics could make

Critics might respond:

In the research world, "superintelligence" has a specific meaning tied to exceeding human capability broadly. Applying it to today's systems by policy rather than by evidence could blur an important distinction.
If the official vocabulary already says "super," it may be harder to recognize, communicate or prepare for a real future step change, when one arrives.
Two parallel terms could create confusion in practice during any transition.
What both sides should accept

Whichever reading you find more persuasive, the documents set a shared baseline. The order is a naming policy plus a request for a legislative proposal. The administration gives a rationale. No independent capability finding comes with it.

If someone tells you this order means machines are now smarter than humans, the order's own Section 3(a) contradicts them. If someone tells you it changes nothing, the 60-day legislative draft contradicts them too. The order's practical significance depends on what that draft proposes.

Takeaway: The order changes the vocabulary and leaves open what "SI" will mean in law.

Part Five: A Lesson That Applies Beyond Washington

Analysis.

Strip away the politics and this is a familiar pattern: when a capability claim moves faster than measurement, the label tends to get ahead of the evidence.

You see it everywhere. A startup calls its regression model "AI-powered." A fund calls a backtest "proven alpha." A product page calls a feature "intelligent." This executive order is a high-profile, legally careful case of the same tension, a name changing before any shared, external measurement of what the name promises.

The practical habit is the same whether you're reading a government memo, a pitch deck or a model release:

Read the definition clause, not the headline. Here, Section 3(a) shows that nothing about the covered technology has changed yet.
Separate stated rationale from tested finding. The fact sheet's framing is a rationale.
Check scope. Who does this bind, and who doesn't it bind? Here, it binds executive agencies' non-statutory documents only.
Find the real deadline and what it produces. Sixty days, for a proposal.
Notice what's missing. No benchmark, no cost estimate, no compliance record.

If you take only one habit from this story, make it this: before you believe a claim about intelligence, look for the measurement behind it.

What to Watch Next
The 60-day proposal. Does the draft SI definition keep the current AI scope, narrow it, or add capability criteria?
Congress. Without legislation, the statutory definition stays where it is.
Agency practice. The sources don't establish completed compliance. Watch how and when "SI" actually appears in official channels.
Everyone else. Industry, academia, media and foreign governments are under no obligation to follow, and their choices will decide whether "SI" becomes common usage or stays an official term.
The Bottom Line

On September 29, 2026, the U.S. executive branch was told to stop saying "AI" and start saying "Super Intelligence" in its own non-statutory documents. The technology covered is the same. Old contracts and regulations are untouched. Nobody outside the executive branch is required to switch. A proposal for a new legal definition is due within 60 days, and only Congress can make it law.

The systems themselves did not get any smarter that day. The vocabulary changed, and the effort to put that vocabulary into law has begun.
