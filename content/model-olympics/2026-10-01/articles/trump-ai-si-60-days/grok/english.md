Washington Changed the Word. The Capability Claim Did Not Arrive With It.

On September 29, 2026, President Donald Trump signed an executive order directing U.S. executive departments and agencies, to the extent allowed by law, to replace “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI” with “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in official communications, websites, reports, and other non-statutory documents.

That is the event. The rest is what people will try to make it mean.

The order is a naming instruction inside the executive branch. It is not a scientific finding that machines have crossed into superintelligence. It is not a statute. It is not a ban on the public, industry, universities, or the press using the letters A and I. The White House fact sheet published the same day frames the change as recognition of technological progress. That is the administration’s stated rationale. It is not an independently tested capability assessment.

If you only remember one distinction from this article, remember that one. Words can move in sixty seconds. Benchmarks cannot.

What the order actually does

The instruction applies to executive departments and agencies. The qualifier matters: “to the extent allowed by law.” Federal agencies cannot lawfully pretend a statute says SI when the statute still says AI. They also cannot, under this order, rewrite history.

According to the order summarized in the official presidential action posted September 29, 2026, earlier regulations, presidential actions, contracts, grants, and historical documents do not have to be altered. Old paper stays old paper. New executive-branch language, where law permits, is supposed to say SI.

For purposes of this order, SI presently covers the same technologies as the existing statutory definition of AI in 15 U.S.C. 9401(3), unless law provides otherwise. Read that again. The new label is attached, for now, to the old legal object. The order itself is evidence of a naming policy. It is not evidence that the underlying systems suddenly became a different class of mind.

That is not a cynical reading. That is the document’s own legal posture, as described in the official text the White House published.

What the 60 days are — and are not

The president’s science and technology assistant must submit proposed legislative language for a federal SI definition within 60 days.

A proposal is not an enacted law.

A drafting deadline is not a deadline for machines to become smarter.

Congress would still have to take up any language, amend it, pass it, and have it signed. None of that has happened in the materials provided. Treating a 60-day staff assignment as a countdown to “real SI” would be a category error: calendar theater mistaken for capability.

If a statutory definition eventually arrives, that will be a separate political and legal event. It should be reported as such. Until then, the federal government has an executive preference about vocabulary and a homework assignment about future wording.

The fact sheet’s claim, held at arm’s length

The accompanying White House fact sheet, also dated September 29, 2026, presents the rename as recognition that the technology has moved. Administrations are allowed to argue that. Readers are allowed to notice what the argument is not.

It is not a peer-reviewed demonstration.
It is not a new public benchmark.
It is not a finding that current systems meet any particular research community’s definition of superintelligence.
It is not proof that yesterday’s chatbots are today’s gods.

Progress in machine learning since the last decade has been real, large, and uneven. That sentence can be true at the same time as this one: a presidential naming order does not measure that progress. Branding can run ahead of evaluation. It often does.

Takeaway line: A government can rename the file folder without changing what is inside the folder.

Why language fights like this matter anyway

People will be tempted to treat this as a joke about stationery. Some of it is stationery. Agency homepages, report titles, speech drafts, and press pages are the kind of surface an executive order can actually reach.

Language is also how bureaucracies allocate seriousness. Once a term sits in the header of a budget justification, a research agenda, a procurement notice, or a risk memo, it starts to organize attention. “AI safety” and “SI safety” are not guaranteed to fund the same work. “AI workforce” and “SI workforce” will not automatically mean the same training programs. The object in 15 U.S.C. 9401(3) may be unchanged today. The political weather around that object can still shift because the label on the briefing slide changed.

That is analysis, not a leaked motive. The sources do not establish private intent, implementation cost, or how many pages have already been edited. No completed agency compliance record is in the brief. Anyone claiming a finished whole-of-government find-and-replace is inventing an operations report that has not been produced here.

Still, naming is a cheap lever with expensive downstream habits. If you work in a regulated industry, watch the next sixty days for the proposed definition, not the adjectives in the fact sheet. Definitions decide what gets counted. Counting decides what gets funded, restricted, or ignored.

Takeaway line: The fight is never only about the acronym. It is about which future the paperwork is allowed to assume.

The boundaries that keep this from becoming a rumor

A few claims are already wandering near this story. They do not survive the source material.

This is not a nationwide speech ban. Private companies, journalists, researchers, and ordinary speakers can keep saying AI. The order is an executive-branch instruction.

This is not a criminal penalty for using the old term.

This is not an enacted statutory redefinition. The current legal definition cited for the order’s interim mapping remains the AI definition in 15 U.S.C. 9401(3), unless some other law already says otherwise.

This is not a documented overnight model upgrade. No system card, eval leaderboard, or lab disclosure in these sources shows that models jumped a capability class on September 29.

This is not a new scientific benchmark adopted by the research community.

This is not proof that “superintelligence” now exists as a measured technical state rather than a policy label.

Preserve those qualifications and the story stays adult. Drop them and it becomes a trailer.

Superintelligence was already a loaded word

Before this order, “superintelligence” already lived a double life.

In research and forecasting debates, it usually points toward systems that substantially exceed humans across most economically and scientifically valuable cognitive tasks. People argue bitterly about timelines, preconditions, and whether current scaling paths get there. That argument is not settled by a White House rename.

In politics and marketing, the same word is a mood. It can mean “this is bigger than a chatbot.” It can mean “do not regulate us as a toy.” It can mean “fund the next era.” Mood is not measurement.

The order tries to drag a research-world lightning rod into routine federal diction while simultaneously saying, for now, that SI points at the same legal bundle as AI. That is an unstable pair. Either the word is mostly cosmetic, in which case the grandeur is marketing. Or the word is meant to preview a future statutory category, in which case the 60-day proposal is the real document and the rename is the teaser.

We do not have to guess which one “they really believe.” The honest position, given the sources, is simpler: the executive branch has chosen a more maximal label for an existing legal category and asked for draft legislative language. Belief, capability, and statute are three different files.

Takeaway line: If the technology were already self-evidently superintelligent, the government would not need a memo to make the vocabulary catch up.

What will actually change in the next two months

Watch process, not poetry.

First, agency communications. Some pages and reports will start saying SI where they used to say AI, where lawyers decide the statute allows it. Some will not, because law still says AI. Inconsistency is not sabotage. It is federalism plus preexisting text.

Second, the definition memo. Proposed legislative language can be narrow, sweeping, symbolic, or operational. Narrow would keep SI pinned close to 15 U.S.C. 9401(3). Sweeping would try to pull in future systems, new risk tiers, or new authorities. Symbolic would decorate the same object with a larger name. Operational would change what agencies must inventory, evaluate, or restrict.

Third, everyone else’s language. Labs, standards bodies, journalists, and other governments do not have to follow. Some will echo the term because power attracts mimicry. Some will refuse because “superintelligence” already means something more specific in their communities. Fragmented vocabulary is not a crisis by itself. It becomes a problem when two offices think they are regulating the same thing and are not.

None of that requires a hidden palace intrigue. It follows from how official words propagate.

The older pattern hiding under the news

States have always tried to stabilize new machines with old moral vocabulary, then with new technical vocabulary, then with slogans when the technical vocabulary escapes the building.

Electricity was a marvel, then a utility, then an infrastructure obligation.
Nuclear energy was a weapon, then a civilian promise, then a regulated exception.
The internet was a network, then an economy, then a speech and security problem wearing the same cables.
“Artificial intelligence” has been a research program, a product category, a stock-market season, and a governance object.

“Super Intelligence” is an attempt to skip to the last chapter in the title while remaining, legally, in the current chapter in the footnotes. That tension is the story. Not a sudden awakening. Not a speech code. A government reaching for a bigger noun.

There is a human reason this lands. People can feel the systems getting more fluent, more useful, more strange. They want a word equal to the unease and the awe. Administrations want a word equal to a mandate. Researchers want a word equal to a test. Those three wants are not the same word, even when they share letters.

The responsible public move is not to sneer at the unease or to baptize it as proof. It is to keep asking what, exactly, changed: the model, the law, or the letterhead.

On the available record dated September 29, 2026, and verified here as of October 1, 2026 Hong Kong time, the letterhead changed. The statutory object was mapped, not replaced. A definition was requested, not enacted. Progress was asserted as rationale, not published as a new measured threshold.

That is enough news. It is not the end of history in 12-point type.

How to read the next headline after this one

If a later story says “the U.S. has entered the era of superintelligence,” ask whether the author means:

a branding era,
a statutory era,
or a capability era.

Only the first is supported by the September 29 order and fact sheet as provided. The second is a future legislative question. The third would require evidence the current sources do not contain.

If a later story says agencies were “forced to erase AI from America,” check the scope. Executive communications are not the country.

If a later story says models “became SI in 60 days,” that would be a failure of reading comprehension. The 60 days belong to a staff proposal.

Precision is not pedantry here. Precision is how you avoid being conscripted by a slogan.

Takeaway line: Do not outsource your epistemology to a style guide.

The useful civic habit is older than this presidency and will outlast it. When power names a technology, separate three questions and refuse to let them collapse:

What does the government want people to say?
What does the law presently require?
What can the systems actually do?

On September 29, 2026, the first question received an answer from the executive branch. The second received a continuity clause and a drafting assignment. The third received a rationale, not a proof.

The era, if that word still means anything, will be decided by the last question. The paperwork is only rehearsing.

What should a government be allowed to rename first — the technology, the risk, or the proof that either one has changed?
