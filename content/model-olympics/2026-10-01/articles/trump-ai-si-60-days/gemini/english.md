The Era of "Super Intelligence" Begins on Paper

In Washington, the dictionary is often drafted by decree. On September 29, 2026, the language of the future received a sudden, sweeping rewrite.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order directing U.S. executive departments and agencies to officially replace the term "Artificial Intelligence" (or AI) with "Super Intelligence" (or SI) across official communications, websites, reports, and other non-statutory documents, to the extent permitted by law.

At first glance, the shift from "Artificial" to "Super" feels momentous—a rhetorical leap that suggests we have crossed the threshold from machine imitation into the realm of digital supremacy. But a closer examination of the executive order and its accompanying fact sheet reveals a complex administrative maneuver. We are upgrading the rhetoric, but for now, we are not upgrading the code.

To understand the weight of this moment, we must separate the bureaucratic reality from the science fiction. A rebrand is not a breakthrough, but in the corridors of power, a change of name often signals a profound shift in political posture.

Here is the breakdown of what the executive order actually does, what the 60-day deadline means, and why this renaming policy is sparking intense debate about the intersection of language, law, and technological progress.

The Stroke of a Pen: Rewriting the Government's Lexicon

The core mandate of the September 29 order is remarkably specific in its domain. The President has instructed the executive branch to scrub the acronym "AI" from its active public-facing vocabulary and replace it with "SI".

If you visit a federal agency's website, read a newly published departmental report, or review official executive branch communications in the coming months, you will likely see "Super Intelligence" in places where "Artificial Intelligence" used to sit.

However, this directive is bound by strict legal guardrails.

The technology itself has not changed: The order explicitly states that "SI" presently covers the exact same technologies as the existing statutory definition of AI found in 15 U.S.C. 9401(3), unless a specific law dictates otherwise.

It is a naming policy, not a scientific milestone: The executive order serves as evidence of a new administrative naming policy; it does not serve as scientific proof that machines have suddenly achieved actual superintelligence.

It is an executive instruction, not a nationwide ban: The directive applies to the executive branch's internal and public communications. It is not a ban on private citizens, corporations, or the media using the term "AI".

My analysis of this mandate is that it represents a calculated branding exercise by the executive branch. By tethering "Super Intelligence" to the existing 15 U.S.C. 9401(3) framework, the administration avoids the immediate legal chaos of redefining the technology for regulators, while still projecting a modernized, arguably more formidable vision of American technological prowess.

The 60-Day Countdown: A Legislative Proposal, Not a Singularity

Perhaps the most scrutinized element of the September 29 directive is the clock it starts ticking.

Under the order, the President’s science and technology assistant has been given a 60-day deadline to submit proposed legislative language that would create a formal, federal definition of "SI".

This 60-day window is ripe for misinterpretation. It is vital to clarify exactly what this timeline represents:

It is a deadline for paperwork, not for processing power: This is not a 60-day countdown for machines to become smarter.

A proposal is not a law: The science and technology assistant is simply submitting proposed legislative language. For that definition to become the law of the land, it would need to be passed by Congress and enacted as a statute.

This detail underscores a fundamental reality of American governance: the President can direct how the executive branch talks, but only Congress can change the underlying statutory definitions that govern the nation. The 60-day deadline is the administration's opening bid to Congress—a request to legally enshrine a term that the executive branch has already adopted.

If Congress rejects or ignores the proposal, the executive branch will likely continue to use "SI" in its reports and on its websites, but the legal framework regulating the technology will remain tied to the older definitions of "AI."

Grandfathering the Past: What the Order Doesn't Touch

When a government renames a foundational concept, the immediate fear is bureaucratic whiplash. Will thousands of past contracts need to be rewritten? Will old regulations be rendered legally void?

The order signed on September 29 actively prevents this kind of retroactive chaos. The directive clearly outlines its own limitations:

No alteration of historical documents: The mandate does not require the alteration of historical documents, prior presidential actions, or earlier regulations.

Grants and contracts remain intact: Existing federal grants and contracts are not required to be altered by this order.

By explicitly grandfathering in past documents, the administration ensures that the transition from AI to SI is forward-looking. The government is not attempting to erase the history of Artificial Intelligence; it is attempting to declare that the era of AI is over, and the era of SI has begun.

From an analytical perspective, this is a pragmatic move. A retroactive find-and-replace across decades of federal paperwork would cost millions of dollars and countless hours of labor. By strictly limiting the scope to future, non-statutory documents, the executive branch can achieve its desired rhetorical shift without paralyzing the agencies tasked with implementing it.

The Rhetoric of "Progress"

Why make this change now? The accompanying White House fact sheet, also released on September 29, provides the administration's official justification.

The fact sheet frames the shift from AI to SI as a necessary step in recognizing technological progress.

It is crucial to note that this is the administration's stated rationale. It is not a finding based on an independently tested capability of the technology.

This distinction between political narrative and scientific reality is the crux of the entire event. The words we use to describe technology inevitably shape how we interact with it, how we fear it, and how we regulate it.

Consider the semantic difference between the two terms.

"Artificial" implies something constructed, synthetic, and inherently subordinate to the "natural." It suggests an imitation of human thought.

"Super" implies transcendence, superiority, and dominance. It suggests an intelligence that has outgrown its creators.

By pushing the federal government to adopt "Super Intelligence," the administration is framing the technology not as a tool that mimics humanity, but as a formidable, superior entity that the nation must harness.

While the facts confirm that the underlying algorithms haven't changed overnight, the perception of those algorithms is absolutely being targeted. This framing—recognizing technological progress—serves as a political signal. It tells the public, the tech industry, and international competitors that the U.S. government views its digital capabilities as elite and advanced.

The Gap Between Lexicon and Law

As we look toward the 60-day deadline for the legislative proposal, a fascinating tension emerges. We are currently living in a linguistic gap.

Right now, an executive branch agency might release a report warning about the risks of "Super Intelligence," while the federal regulators tasked with mitigating those risks are legally bound to police "Artificial Intelligence." The executive branch is speaking the language of tomorrow, while tethered to the laws of yesterday.

This creates a unique challenge for the President’s science and technology assistant. When they draft the proposed legislative language within the 60-day window, they must define "Super Intelligence" in a way that satisfies the administration's desire for a modernized vocabulary without accidentally deregulating the technology or creating legal loopholes.

If "SI" is legally defined as something exponentially smarter than "AI," what happens to the current models? Do they remain classified as AI, while SI becomes a purely theoretical category for future machines? Or will the new legislative proposal attempt to legally reclassify today's large language models as "Super Intelligent"?

The executive order currently solves this by stating that SI covers the same technologies as the existing AI definition. But a permanent, statutory redefinition proposed to Congress could attempt to shift those goalposts.

The Final Takeaway: A Name is a Promise

The September 29 executive order is a masterclass in political framing. The Trump administration has not rewritten the code of the future, nor have they proven that machines have achieved a new state of being. There is no enacted statutory redefinition, no nationwide speech ban, and no criminal penalty for continuing to say "AI".

What they have done is fundamentally alter the tone of the conversation within the halls of the federal government.

They have decreed that the U.S. government will no longer speak of "Artificial" tools, but of "Super" intelligence. They have set a 60-day clock for a proposal that will attempt to etch this new mindset into federal law. They have framed this administrative rename as a recognition of technological progress.

Whether this rhetoric eventually matches the scientific reality of the machines sitting in server farms remains to be seen. But in the immediate future, the language of the United States government has been officially upgraded.

The code is the same. The laws are the same. But the title has changed, and in a world driven by perception, a title is sometimes all it takes to change the trajectory of an industry.
