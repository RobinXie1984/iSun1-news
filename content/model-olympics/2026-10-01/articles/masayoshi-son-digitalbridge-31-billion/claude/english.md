Masayoshi Son Didn't Buy Data Centers. He Bought the People Who Pick Them.
Three Headline Options

Suspenseful: Nine Months, Two Regulators, One Quiet Signature: What SoftBank Just Locked In on September 30

Emotional Resonance: The Man Who Keeps Betting the Future Just Placed Another Chip, and This One Is Made of Fiber and Concrete

Counter-intuitive: A $4 Billion Deal Closed at $3.1 Billion, and Nobody Got a Discount

The Short Version

On September 30, 2026 (U.S. time), SoftBank Group completed its acquisition of all of DigitalBridge's outstanding common stock for approximately US$3.1 billion.

DigitalBridge is now a controlled subsidiary of SoftBank. It continues as a separately managed platform, still led by Marc Ganzi.

That's the news. The rest of this piece explains why the headline number is easy to misread, what SoftBank actually bought, and why "AI infrastructure" is a promise rather than a result.

To keep things honest, I've split this into two kinds of sections. "What the record says" sections contain only facts from the issuer announcements. "My read" sections contain my own analysis, and you should treat them as opinion.

Part 1: The Number That Looks Like a Discount and Isn't
What the record says

When SoftBank and DigitalBridge first announced the deal on December 29, 2025, they described it in two ways:

approximately US$4.0 billion in enterprise value
US$16 cash per common share

At completion on September 30, 2026, the figure reported was approximately US$3.1 billion for all outstanding common stock.

My read

Here's the trap. If you put "$4.0 billion" in December next to "$3.1 billion" in September, your brain writes the headline for you: SoftBank negotiates $900 million off.

That headline would be wrong. The two numbers measure different things.

Enterprise value describes the value of a whole business, including claims beyond common shareholders. The formula varies by situation, but it typically accounts for things like debt, preferred equity, and cash.

Common-stock purchase price describes what was paid to the people holding ordinary shares, and nothing else.

A concrete way to see it is buying a house:

The house is "worth" $1 million. That's your enterprise value.
There's a $300,000 mortgage on it.
You pay the seller $700,000 for their equity and take over the mortgage.

You didn't get a $300,000 discount. You paid for two different slices of the same thing in two different ways.

I'm not going to reconstruct DigitalBridge's exact bridge from $4.0 billion to $3.1 billion. The source material doesn't provide that breakdown, and guessing would mean inventing numbers. The point is simpler. These figures are not interchangeable, and comparing them as before-and-after prices is a category error.

Takeaway #1: When a deal has two numbers, the first question isn't "which is bigger?" It's "bigger what?"

Part 2: The Nine-Month Walk From Handshake to Close
What the record says

The timeline, using only the announced milestones:

Date	Milestone
December 29, 2025	SoftBank and DigitalBridge announce the agreement (~US$4.0B enterprise value; US$16 cash per common share)
September 22, 2026	DigitalBridge announces it has received the required regulatory approvals, including from FERC and CFIUS
September 30, 2026 (U.S. time)	Completion: SoftBank acquires all outstanding common stock for ~US$3.1B
October 1, 2026 (Hong Kong time)	Completion verified
My read

Two different finish lines are easy to blur here.

September 22 was the approvals milestone. It meant the regulators said yes.

September 30 was the completion milestone. It meant the deal actually closed.

That eight-day gap is normal. Approval means you're allowed to do something. Completion means it's done.

The regulators involved are worth a sentence each, in plain terms.

CFIUS (the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States) reviews certain foreign investments in U.S. businesses for national-security implications. A Japanese group acquiring a U.S.-listed digital infrastructure investor is the kind of transaction that draws that review.

FERC (the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) oversees parts of the U.S. energy sector. Its involvement is a reminder that "digital infrastructure" increasingly touches the power grid.

The announcements tell us these approvals were received. They don't tell us what was discussed during review, and I won't pretend to know.

Takeaway #2: Approval is permission. Completion is commitment. Headlines usually collapse the two into one.

Part 3: What SoftBank Actually Bought

This is the part I think matters most, and it's where most casual coverage will go sideways.

What the record says

SoftBank acquired DigitalBridge's common equity. DigitalBridge invests in data centers, cell towers, fiber networks, and edge infrastructure.

DigitalBridge remains a separately managed platform led by Marc Ganzi, now as a controlled subsidiary of SoftBank.

My read

Read the verb carefully: DigitalBridge invests in infrastructure.

DigitalBridge is an asset manager. SoftBank bought the manager. That is not the same as SoftBank now owning every data center, tower, and fiber line that DigitalBridge's funds have invested in.

Think of it like a restaurant group versus its restaurants.

If you buy a company that runs and invests in restaurants on behalf of outside investors, you own the company. You don't automatically own every kitchen it manages, every building it leases, or every stake its investors hold. Those belong to the funds and their investors, under their own agreements.

So when you see a phrase like "SoftBank now controls a global network of data centers," slow down. What SoftBank controls is the platform: the team, the relationships, the investment process, and the management business. How much influence that translates into over the underlying assets depends on fund structures and agreements that the brief doesn't spell out.

It's also worth naming what this deal is not:

It's not Masayoshi Son spending his personal money. The buyer is SoftBank Group, the company he leads as chairman and CEO.
It's not ownership of everything DigitalBridge's funds manage.
It's not proof that AI infrastructure will pay off.

Takeaway #3: SoftBank didn't buy the buildings. It bought the people who decide which buildings get built.

That's not a smaller bet. In some ways it's a more interesting one.

Part 4: The AI Story, and Where the Evidence Ends
What the record says

SoftBank's stated rationale is to expand the infrastructure needed for AI.

The benefits SoftBank describes are the buyer's own claims. They are not independently established results.

My read

The logic is easy to follow. AI models don't run on enthusiasm. They run on physical things:

Data centers, where compute lives
Fiber networks, which move data between places
Cell towers, which carry data to devices
Edge infrastructure, which brings compute closer to where data is used

DigitalBridge invests across exactly those categories. On paper, the fit with an AI-infrastructure thesis is obvious.

But "on paper" is doing a lot of work there.

What we know is that SoftBank says this acquisition supports AI infrastructure. What we don't know yet:

whether it will expand capacity, and by how much
whether it will lower costs or improve efficiency
whether it will produce returns for SoftBank shareholders
whether the AI demand it assumes will materialize at the expected scale and pace

None of those outcomes are in the announcements. None of them could be, since the deal closed on September 30. Anyone quoting specific gigawatts, savings, synergies, or job changes from this transaction is going beyond the record.

This is the gap I'd urge every reader to sit with. A rationale is a forecast. Forecasts deserve respect, scrutiny, and patience, in that order.

Takeaway #4: "Bought for AI" is a reason. "Worked for AI" is a result. Only the first one exists today.

Part 5: Why Keep the Manager in Charge?
What the record says

DigitalBridge remains separately managed, and Marc Ganzi continues to lead it.

My read

This detail is small in the announcement and large in implication.

Acquirers often fold companies in, merge teams, and impose the parent's way of doing things. Here the announced structure points the other way. The platform keeps its own management and its own leader, inside SoftBank's control.

Why would a buyer do that? I can think of some plausible reasons. To be clear, these are possibilities, not anything SoftBank has stated:

In asset management, the people are the product. Relationships with investors, partners, and operators can be harder to replace than any single asset.
Separate management can reassure outside investors. They committed capital to a manager they know, and a stable team may matter to them.
It keeps the platform's identity legible. A distinct DigitalBridge may be easier to explain and grow than a unit absorbed into a much larger group.

Whether any of these motivated the structure, I can't say. The source doesn't explain it. But structure is a decision, and decisions tell you what a buyer thinks it's paying for.

Takeaway #5: You can learn more from what an acquirer leaves alone than from what it changes.

Part 6: Five Lines Worth Keeping

If you remember nothing else, here are the ideas I'd pin to the wall:

Two numbers aren't a discount until you know they measure the same thing. Here, they don't.
Approval and completion are different days for a reason. September 22 and September 30 are both real milestones, but only one of them is the close.
Owning the manager isn't owning the assets. The deal is for DigitalBridge's common equity.
A stated rationale is a forecast, not a scoreboard. AI-infrastructure benefits remain the buyer's claims.
The company made the bet, not the man personally. Son leads SoftBank Group; SoftBank Group is the buyer.
Part 7: How to Read the Next AI Infrastructure Deal

This deal won't be the last of its kind, so the method is worth more than the specific story. Here's the checklist I'd run on any similar headline, using this deal as the worked example:

1. Which number is it?
Enterprise value, equity value, or per-share price? In this case: ~$4.0B enterprise value (Dec 2025), $16 per common share (Dec 2025), ~$3.1B for common stock (Sept 2026).

2. Which milestone is it?
Announcement, approval, or completion? In this case: Dec 29, Sept 22, and Sept 30 respectively.

3. What exactly changed hands?
The operating assets, or the company that manages them? In this case: the manager's common equity.

4. Who is the buyer?
The famous founder personally, or the institution? In this case: SoftBank Group.

5. Is the benefit claimed or demonstrated?
In this case: claimed by the buyer, not independently established.

Five questions. Most of the misleading versions of this story fail at least one of them.

The Bottom Line

On September 30, 2026, SoftBank Group closed its acquisition of all of DigitalBridge's outstanding common stock for about US$3.1 billion. The deal followed regulatory approvals, including FERC and CFIUS, announced on September 22.

DigitalBridge, which invests in data centers, towers, fiber, and edge infrastructure, is now a controlled subsidiary. It continues as a separately managed platform under Marc Ganzi.

The headline number is not a markdown from $4 billion. The asset is a manager, not a mountain of servers. The AI payoff is a thesis, not a result.

That doesn't make the deal less interesting. It makes it more honest. We're watching a large company make a structured bet on the physical plumbing of AI, and the scoreboard hasn't been posted yet.
