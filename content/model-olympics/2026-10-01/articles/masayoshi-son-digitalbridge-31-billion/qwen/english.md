The Cloud is Actually Made of Concrete
We like to think of artificial intelligence as an ethereal force. We talk about "the cloud," "neural networks," and "machine learning" as if they exist in a frictionless vacuum, floating somewhere above our heads. But the truth of the modern digital economy is far heavier, far louder, and far more grounded. AI does not live in the cloud; it lives in concrete, steel, cooling systems, and thousands of miles of fiber-optic glass.
The race to build the most advanced artificial intelligence models is no longer just a software competition. It has violently collided with the physical world. The algorithms of tomorrow require an unfathomable amount of compute power, and that compute power requires a physical home.
This brings us to a massive, quiet shift in the tectonic plates of global technology infrastructure. On September 30, 2026 (U.S. time)—which registered as October 1, 2026, in Hong Kong—SoftBank Group officially closed a monumental transaction. The Japanese multinational investment holding company completed the acquisition of all outstanding common stock of DigitalBridge, an infrastructure asset manager, for approximately US$3.1 billion.
This is not just another line item in a quarterly earnings report. It is a definitive signal of where the smartest capital in the world believes the next decade of technological dominance will be won. SoftBank isn't just betting on the software that will think; it is betting on the physical real estate where that thinking will happen.
The Anatomy of a $3.1 Billion Deal
To understand the magnitude of this acquisition, we first have to clear up a persistent piece of financial confusion that often plagues mega-mergers. When news of this deal first broke in the original agreement on December 29, 2025, headlines widely circulated a figure of approximately US$4.0 billion in enterprise value, alongside a US$16 cash per common share offer.
Now that the deal has closed at a $3.1 billion figure for the common stock, it is incredibly important to understand the financial mechanics at play. It is a common journalistic and public error to look at the $4.0 billion original headline and the $3.1 billion closing figure and assume SoftBank negotiated a "price cut" or a discount.
The Analytical View: Do not call the $3.1 billion completion figure a price cut from $4 billion. These two figures measure fundamentally different things.
Enterprise Value (the $4.0 billion figure) is a theoretical measure of a company's total value. It is the calculated price to acquire the entire business, which includes the cost of the common equity plus the cost of assuming the company's debt, minus its cash on hand. It is the "total price tag" of the corporate entity.
The $3.1 billion figure, however, represents the actual cash paid specifically for the outstanding common stock—the equity slice of the pie that changes hands in this specific transaction. The gap between the two numbers is accounted for by DigitalBridge's balance sheet, including its debt load and other financial structures that remain on the books. SoftBank did not get a discount; they simply paid the equity value of the company, while the enterprise value remains a separate, broader financial metric. The $16 cash per common share metric from the December 29, 2025 agreement remains the foundational math of the equity purchase.
By separating the equity purchase from the total enterprise value, we get a much clearer picture of the capital actually deployed by SoftBank Group’s Chairman and CEO, Masayoshi Son, to secure control of this vital platform.
Surviving the Regulatory Gauntlet
Closing a multi-billion-dollar cross-border acquisition is never as simple as signing a check and shaking hands. When you are dealing with the physical infrastructure that powers a nation's digital economy, you are dealing with national security and critical resources.
The journey from the December 2025 announcement to the September 2026 closing was defined by a rigorous regulatory gauntlet. DigitalBridge announced on September 22, 2026, that it had finally received the last of the required regulatory approvals. Specifically, the company cleared hurdles set by FERC (the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) and CFIUS (the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States).
Why do these two specific acronyms matter so much in the context of AI?
The Analytical View: The regulatory clearances tell us exactly what is at stake in the AI infrastructure war.
CFIUS is a U.S. federal interagency committee that reviews the national security implications of foreign investments in U.S. companies. SoftBank is a Japanese conglomerate. DigitalBridge manages assets that include cell towers, fiber networks, and data centers—the literal nervous system of American communications and data sovereignty. For a foreign entity to take control of the management company overseeing these assets requires intense scrutiny to ensure no foreign adversary could potentially exploit U.S. communications infrastructure. Clearing CFIUS means the U.S. government has formally signed off on SoftBank’s stewardship of this digital backbone.
FERC, on the other hand, regulates the interstate transmission of electricity, natural gas, and oil. Why is an energy regulator looking at a digital infrastructure deal? Because data centers are the new industrial power plants. The AI boom has triggered an unprecedented surge in electricity demand. Digital infrastructure is inextricably linked to power grids, transmission lines, and energy procurement. FERC’s approval signifies that the transfer of ownership does not violate federal energy regulations or create monopolistic bottlenecks in the power supply required to keep these massive server farms running.
September 22 was the regulatory milestone; September 30 was the financial and operational completion milestone. The fact that it took nearly nine months to clear these specific agencies underscores the immense strategic value—and the intense geopolitical sensitivity—of the assets DigitalBridge controls.
The Shovels in the AI Gold Rush
So, why does Masayoshi Son want this? What is SoftBank’s stated rationale for absorbing an infrastructure asset manager?
The answer lies in a fundamental shift in the technology investment thesis. During the early days of the internet boom, the most valuable companies were the ones building the portals, the search engines, and the e-commerce sites. Today, in the generative AI boom, the bottleneck is not the software. The bottleneck is the hardware.
SoftBank’s stated rationale for the acquisition is to aggressively expand the infrastructure needed for AI. DigitalBridge is not a company that writes code or trains large language models. Instead, it invests in and manages the physical platforms that make AI possible: data centers, cell towers, fiber networks, and edge infrastructure.
Think of the AI boom as a modern-day gold rush. While thousands of startups are panning for gold in the river of generative AI, SoftBank is executing a classic, highly lucrative strategy: they are buying the company that sells the shovels, the pickaxes, and the land the river sits on.
Data Centers: The massive, heavily secured warehouses filled with thousands of GPU servers where AI models are actually trained and run.
Cell Towers and Fiber Networks: The high-speed arteries that move the massive datasets required for AI from the data center to the end user.
Edge Infrastructure: Smaller, localized computing hubs placed closer to the user to reduce latency, which is critical for real-time AI applications like autonomous driving and advanced robotics.
Boundary Check & Analytical Caveat: It is vital to distinguish between strategic intent and guaranteed outcomes. SoftBank’s promised benefits—that this acquisition will seamlessly expand AI infrastructure and generate massive synergies—are strategic rationales. They are not independently established results. Purchasing an asset manager does not automatically guarantee that AI returns will materialize, nor does it prove that the current AI boom will sustain its current trajectory. We are observing a massive capital allocation bet on a specific thesis: that physical infrastructure will be the primary bottleneck and therefore the most valuable asset in the AI era. Whether that thesis yields the promised financial benefits remains to be seen.
The Architecture of Control: Marc Ganzi and the Asset Management Model
One of the most fascinating aspects of this transaction is what happens the day after the deal closes. In many hostile or aggressive corporate takeovers, the acquiring company sweeps in, fires the legacy management team, and attempts to micromanage the new asset from headquarters in Tokyo.
That is not what is happening here.
According to the completion announcement, DigitalBridge has become a controlled subsidiary of SoftBank, but it will remain a separately managed platform. Crucially, it will continue to be led by Marc Ganzi.
This distinction is the key to understanding the true nature of the acquisition. SoftBank did not just buy a portfolio of buildings; they bought an asset management engine. DigitalBridge operates as a real estate investment trust and an alternative asset manager. They raise capital from institutional investors, deploy it into digital infrastructure, and manage those assets on behalf of their funds.
The Analytical View: SoftBank bought the asset manager's common equity. This is not ownership of every single asset its funds manage.
By keeping Marc Ganzi at the helm and maintaining the platform as separately managed, SoftBank is acknowledging a hard truth about the infrastructure business: relationships and specialized knowledge matter. DigitalBridge has spent years cultivating relationships with municipalities for zoning permits, negotiating complex power purchase agreements with utility companies, and managing the intricate day-to-day operations of cell towers and server farms.
SoftBank wants the strategic control, the financial upside of the management fees, and the ability to align DigitalBridge’s massive capital deployment with SoftBank’s broader AI vision. But they do not want to micromanage the physical maintenance of a cell tower in rural Ohio or the cooling systems of a data center in Virginia. By keeping Ganzi and the existing management structure intact, SoftBank secures the "brain" of the operation without disrupting the "muscle."
This structure also respects the boundaries of the funds DigitalBridge manages. SoftBank now controls the corporate entity of DigitalBridge, but the underlying assets are often owned by specific, legally distinct investment funds with their own limited partners. SoftBank has acquired the manager, gaining control over the strategic direction of the firm, but they have not executed a blanket takeover of every single physical asset sitting inside DigitalBridge's various third-party funds. It is a highly sophisticated, surgical strike for control of the management platform itself.
The Bigger Picture: The Hardening of the Digital World
When we zoom out and look at the completion of this $3.1 billion acquisition, a broader narrative about the evolution of technology begins to emerge.
For the last fifteen years, the technology sector has been obsessed with the "asset-light" model. Software companies were valued at astronomical multiples precisely because they didn't have to worry about the messy, expensive realities of the physical world. They could scale infinitely with just a few lines of code and a credit card for Amazon Web Services.
But the AI revolution is forcing a harsh correction. The computational demands of training and running next-generation AI models are so vast that the asset-light model is breaking down. You cannot run a trillion-parameter AI model on a metaphorical shoe-string. You need gigawatts of dedicated power. You need specialized liquid cooling systems. You need millions of miles of dark fiber.
The digital world is hardening. It is becoming heavier, more capital-intensive, and more deeply intertwined with the physical realities of energy grids and real estate.
Masayoshi Son and SoftBank are placing a massive, multi-billion-dollar chip on the table, betting that the companies that control the physical manifestations of the internet will hold the ultimate leverage in the AI economy. By absorbing DigitalBridge, SoftBank is ensuring that when the next great AI breakthrough happens, they won't just own a stake in the software that achieved it—they will own the landlord of the building where the servers live.
