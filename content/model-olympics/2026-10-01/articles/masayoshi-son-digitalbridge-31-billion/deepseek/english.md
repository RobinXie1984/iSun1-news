The Numbers That Don't Match (And Why That's the Point)

Let's clear up something that will almost certainly get mangled in headlines and social media threads.

The December 29, 2025 agreement described a transaction with approximately US$4.0 billion in enterprise value and US$16 cash per common share-
31
. The September 30 completion announcement reported approximately US$3.1 billion for the common stock.

These are not the same number. They are not a price cut. They are not a renegotiation. They measure different things.

Enterprise value includes debt and other obligations. The common-stock figure reflects what SoftBank paid for the equity. Comparing them as if they're interchangeable is like saying you bought a house for $500,000 when the listing price was $700,000—without mentioning that the $700,000 included the mortgage you assumed.

This distinction matters because it's exactly the kind of thing that gets lost when a deal becomes a headline. And in the AI infrastructure space, where the numbers are already stratospheric, precision is not a luxury. It's a necessity.

What This Deal Is Not

It's worth being explicit about the boundaries here, because the AI infrastructure space is already drowning in hype.

This is not Masayoshi Son spending his personal fortune. This is SoftBank Group, a publicly traded conglomerate, acquiring a company.

This is not SoftBank owning every asset that DigitalBridge's funds manage. DigitalBridge is an asset manager. It manages capital on behalf of institutional investors. SoftBank is buying the manager, not the managed assets.

This is not proof that AI investments are paying off. The promised benefits of the acquisition—strengthening the foundation for next-generation AI data centers, advancing SoftBank's vision to become a leading ASI platform provider—are stated rationales, not independently verified results-
42
.

And this is not a signal that the AI infrastructure buildout is guaranteed to succeed. It's a signal that one of the world's most aggressive technology investors believes it will.

The Uncomfortable Question

Here's where the story gets interesting, and where I'll flag my own analysis rather than reporting.

The AI infrastructure boom is built on a simple premise: if you build the compute, the intelligence will come. Data centers, power, fiber, edge—these are the prerequisites for the AI services that are supposed to transform everything from healthcare to transportation to how we work.

But prerequisites are not guarantees.

SoftBank is not the only player making this bet. Nvidia has invested in SB Energy-
. OpenAI has partnered with SoftBank on the Stargate initiative-
. The entire technology industry is converging on the same conclusion: AI needs physical infrastructure, and someone has to build it.

The question is whether the demand will materialize at the scale that justifies the investment. Son believes it will. He's betting billions on that belief. DigitalBridge is now part of that bet.

What makes this deal notable is not its size—$3.1 billion is significant, but not unprecedented for SoftBank. What makes it notable is what it represents: a shift from investing in AI models and applications to investing in the physical layer beneath them.

The AI revolution will not be won by the best chatbot. It will be won by whoever controls the power, the connectivity, and the data centers that make the chatbot possible.

Masayoshi Son just bought a piece of that control.
