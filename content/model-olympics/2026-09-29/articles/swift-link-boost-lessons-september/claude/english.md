# Swift Turned Away From the Sky to Buy Time. Could LINK Reach Swift in Time?

For almost 22 years, NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has circled Earth watching for sudden explosions in deep space. By 2025, the faint outer edge of Earth's atmosphere was pulling it steadily lower, so NASA tried something unusual for a science satellite: a house call. In September 2025, the agency hired Katalyst Space, a company based in Flagstaff, Arizona, to build a separate robotic spacecraft called LINK. LINK's job was to fly up to Swift, grab hold of it and lift it into a higher, safer orbit. While that machine was being built, the Penn State team that operates Swift began turning the observatory away from many of its astronomical targets to slow its fall. On September 28, 2026, NASA published its account of how the effort went and updated it on September 29. LINK reached orbit, then struggled with communications and steering, and the planned lift for Swift remained undone.

iSun1 researched and first published this historical account on November 27, 2026, to cover that September report. iSun1's November 3 coverage of Swift's final countdown describes a later stage. The account here covers the rescue attempt itself and the trade-offs made to give it a chance. The suspense lies in how many pieces had to fit together for a rescue to become possible, and how much Swift gave up while it waited.

## An observatory with a falling floor

Swift launched in November 2004. Its original assignment was to study gamma-ray bursts. These are brief, ferociously bright flashes of gamma rays, the most energetic form of light, that erupt from distant parts of the universe and fade quickly. Over the years, astronomers also turned Swift toward many other changing objects in the sky: things that brighten, flicker or dim over days, hours or minutes. The observatory became a dependable responder for events that reward a fast look.

Swift travels in low Earth orbit, a few hundred kilometres above the ground. A satellite stays in orbit by moving sideways so fast that, as it falls toward Earth, the planet's curved surface keeps dropping away beneath it. At Swift's height the air is extraordinarily thin, but a trace of it remains. Every lap through that trace acts as a gentle brake. Each loss of speed lets the orbit sag a little, and lower altitudes hold thicker air, so the braking strengthens over time. Scientists call this atmospheric drag.

The Sun adds to the problem. When the Sun is more active, it heats Earth's upper atmosphere, which then swells outward and thickens at the heights where satellites fly. NASA says increased solar activity magnified the drag on Swift. In its September 2026 account, the agency described a risk that Swift could re-enter the dense lower atmosphere by the end of 2026. That date was NASA's forecast at the time, an estimate of risk based on the orbit and the Sun's behaviour.

That is where LINK entered the story.

## Two clocks running at once

NASA's contract with Katalyst arrived in September 2025. It left roughly a year to design, build, test and launch a servicing spacecraft, an extremely short schedule for a machine meant to approach and handle another satellite in orbit.

A second clock was running inside Swift's orbit. NASA says its planning forecast placed a difficult threshold for a boost at around 300 kilometres in the fall of 2026. This number is a planning marker. It reflects where, by NASA's estimate, a rescue for this particular spacecraft would become much harder as the air thickened and the remaining margin shrank. Other spacecraft in other situations face different limits, and the figure describes a forecast rather than a measured low point.

So the teams faced a race. The rescue craft needed time to come into existence. The observatory needed to stay high enough, long enough, to still be worth rescuing when the rescue arrived. The people who fly Swift found a way to slow their side of the clock, and it came with a real cost.

## Buying time by turning

Every spacecraft points in some direction. Engineers call that direction its orientation, or attitude. Swift's telescopes are fixed to its body, so whatever direction the body faces is what the telescopes see. For two decades, the observatory's orientation had served science: point at a burst, point at a flaring star, point at whatever the day's schedule required.

A spacecraft's orientation also changes how much of its body meets the oncoming air. Facing one way, it presents a broad side; facing another, a slimmer profile. Less surface pushing into the thin atmosphere means less braking. A choice that once served astronomy alone now also decided how quickly Swift sank.

An everyday picture may help. This is an illustration only, and it compresses real orbital physics into something simpler. Imagine a little cart rolling around a circular track with a camera mounted on top, filming things beside the track. A steady breeze blows against it, slowing it until it may roll off the track. If the cart turns so its narrow end faces the breeze, it loses speed more slowly and stays on the track longer. The price is that its camera now faces somewhere other than the subjects it was built to film. Meanwhile, a helper cart is racing to catch up, latch on and give it a push. In the real story, the track is an orbit, the breeze is the upper atmosphere, and the helper is LINK.

Swift's Penn State mission operations team normally chooses the observatory's science targets day by day. Beginning in December 2025, according to NASA, the team replaced around a quarter of those daily science targets with directions chosen to reduce drag. That 25 percent figure refers to the share of daily target assignments. It describes how the schedule changed; it is separate from a count of observing hours or a measure of how much science the mission lost overall. By February 2026, the team was using the drag-reducing approach entirely.

NASA describes a pause in pointed science observations from mid-February to late August 2026. Pointed observations are the deliberate kind, in which the observatory aims its telescopes at a chosen object and studies it. For roughly six months, the instrument built to swing toward the universe's brightest flashes spent its days facing directions chosen to keep it in orbit. NASA's account frames that pause in terms of pointed observing; it leaves the activity level of each individual instrument undescribed.

Turning to cut drag also involved several other constraints at once. The operators had to keep Swift's sensitive telescopes away from the dazzling Earth, Moon and Sun, any of which can harm or blind instruments designed to detect faint light. They also had to manage the observatory's exposure to particles in the upper atmosphere streaming against its surfaces. Each day's orientation was a balance among these demands: slim to the air, safe for the optics, mindful of particle exposure. The puzzle shifted as Swift moved around Earth and as Earth moved around the Sun.

NASA says this balancing kept Swift above the critical threshold for several more months. That phrase is NASA's own assessment of the benefit. The account offers it as a broad estimate, and it gives the operations team's work a concrete meaning: the turned-away telescopes bought time for the rescue to arrive.

## The rescue ship

LINK's plan, as NASA describes it, had three parts: meet Swift, grapple it and raise its orbit. In plainer terms, LINK would first need to reach the same orbital path as Swift and match its speed closely enough that the two spacecraft drifted together almost motionless relative to one another. That is delicate work, because both craft travel at several kilometres per second. LINK would then need to approach slowly and safely, take hold of the observatory, and fire its engines to carry the pair upward. Each of those steps was a plan. The September record shows how far the sequence progressed.

LINK launched in July 2026 aboard a Northrop Grumman Pegasus XL rocket from Kwajalein in the Marshall Islands. LINK reached space and completed its initial checks, the routine early tests in which a new spacecraft reports its health and engineers confirm its systems respond. In about ten months from contract to orbit, a servicing spacecraft had been designed, built, tested and launched.

Then the trouble began. NASA reports that LINK experienced intermittent losses of communication and problems controlling its orientation.

Both problems strike at the core of a rescue. Communication is the line between a spacecraft and its controllers, carrying commands up and status reports down. When that line drops in and out, the team on the ground is directing the spacecraft in fragments, and any careful approach to another satellite depends on timely, steady instructions and data. Orientation control is the spacecraft's ability to face where it intends to face. A rescue craft has to aim its engines to change its path, aim its sensors to track its target, and hold itself steady while it closes in. Trouble with orientation makes every one of those steps harder.

NASA's account names these symptoms and stops short of tracing them to a single root cause. It describes what the teams observed; a detailed public fault investigation lies outside its scope.

Faced with those difficulties, NASA and Katalyst scaled the mission back to technology-demonstration goals. A technology demonstration aims to try out equipment in space and learn how it behaves. The revised goals included exercising LINK's xenon propulsion and its three robotic arms. Xenon propulsion is a kind of electric engine. It uses electricity, typically from solar panels, to give xenon gas an electric charge and then fling it out the back at very high speed. Each push is tiny, but it uses fuel very efficiently and can keep working for long periods. The robotic arms were the mechanisms meant to reach for, handle and hold another object, the parts of LINK that would have grasped Swift. NASA's account presents these as goals of the reduced mission. It leaves detailed measurements of each exercise's results for other records.

One image from the period captures the distance that remained. NASA's account includes a photograph in which Swift appears as a streak of light, taken when LINK was, by NASA's report, roughly 12 to 15 kilometres away. In terms of the cart analogy, it is the helper cart coming near enough to film the first one from across the track. Seeing a target and holding it are separate achievements, and the September record documents the first.

NASA ended its involvement in the mission on September 3, 2026. The agency reports that LINK re-entered Earth's atmosphere on September 25. That date belongs to LINK alone. Swift stayed in orbit as a separate spacecraft, its own forecast still governed by the drag described earlier, and LINK's rescue attempt ended with Swift's orbit unchanged by the visit.

## What the attempt left behind

NASA's September account frames the effort as ambitious and high-risk from the start, and it focuses on lessons learned. Some of those lessons concern the schedule. Building a servicing spacecraft in about a year meant NASA and Katalyst had to rework their milestones, the agreed checkpoints for progress, and their approval processes, the formal steps that decide whether a design is ready to move forward. NASA's Engineering and Safety Center, a group of agency specialists who give technical advice on demanding projects, advised the team during integration and testing. Integration is the stage when separate parts are assembled into one working spacecraft; testing is the stage when engineers check that the spacecraft works as intended before launch.

Other lessons come from the Swift side. The Penn State team developed a working method for flying an aging observatory in a way that slowed its descent while protecting its instruments. That required daily judgment about the shape of the spacecraft, the positions of the Earth, Moon and Sun, and the flow of particles in the upper atmosphere. NASA reports that the method added several months of margin.

These lessons are experience. NASA reports them as engineering and operational knowledge gathered under pressure, and they stand apart from the boost itself, which stayed undone. NASA's account offers its numbers as markers of time and choice: dates, a share of daily targets, an approximate planning altitude, a broad estimate of months gained and a count of robotic arms. They tell the story of the race. The September account records LINK's development, launch, initial operation and the learning NASA reports; subsequent missions will establish how reliable future servicing becomes.

## The question the race leaves open

Swift's September story describes two spacecraft and two teams working toward a single meeting point. One machine sacrificed months of its main job, turning away from the sky so the other would still find it in reach. The other machine came into existence remarkably fast, reached orbit and came within about a dozen kilometres. It also ran into reported problems with communication and orientation control, and its mission was scaled back to demonstration goals.

That raises a natural question for anyone curious about keeping old observatories alive: which capabilities have to work together before a servicing visit becomes reliable?

The September record suggests the outline of an answer, at least as far as its evidence reaches. The rescue craft has to arrive while the target still has orbit to spare, which links the speed of building and launching to the observatory's own patience. It has to keep a steady conversation with the ground and hold firm control over the direction it faces. It has to match motion with its target, approach safely, take hold and then perform the push. Throughout all of it, the old observatory has to stay safe, and its scientists have to accept that its usual work will bend around the rescue. LINK showed the early links in that chain: a spacecraft built in about a year, launched and checked out in orbit, close enough to photograph Swift. Then came the reported communication and orientation problems, and the mission narrowed to demonstration goals; NASA's account leaves the full cause of the unrealized boost unassigned.

How a future rescue would carry the sequence through to a boost remains an open question, and the September account leaves it there. What the record does preserve is a clear picture of the cost of waiting: an observatory built to chase the universe's fastest flashes, spending half a year facing the oncoming air, watching for a helper that came close enough to see.
