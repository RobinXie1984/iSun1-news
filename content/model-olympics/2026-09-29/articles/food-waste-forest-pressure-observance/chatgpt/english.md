# Can a Meal Saved Today Leave a Forest Standing Tomorrow?

Food reaches a table through a long series of commitments: land for growing, water for crops, work for harvesting, and energy for processing and transport. The seventh International Day of Awareness of Food Loss and Waste, observed on September 29, 2026, brought that journey into a discussion about forests. Its organizers, the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization, or FAO, and the UN Environment Programme, or UNEP, connect food production with environmental protection. Their shared question reaches from the kitchen back to the landscape: could helping more food reach people ease the pressure to expand farmland?

This feature was researched and first published on November 27, 2026, for the September 29 historical edition. The occasion’s forest connection deserves attention because it asks readers to follow food beyond the familiar sight of a full rubbish bin. UNEP identifies agricultural expansion as a leading driver of deforestation. Food that loses its usefulness along the way has already drawn on resources; reducing those losses could help existing production meet more of people’s needs. Turning that possibility into a forest outcome involves several further decisions.

The observance was scheduled for 12:30–14:00 CEST at the Sheikh Zayed Centre at FAO headquarters in Rome, with participation in person and online. It coincided with the twenty-eighth session of FAO’s Committee on Forestry and World Forest Week. That setting brought the movement of food and the future of forests into the same discussion. The event’s documented theme concerned the transformative potential of reducing food loss and waste. Its environmental promise remains a proposed route to change, with any resulting reduction in forest pressure still to be established through outcome evidence.

To understand that route, begin with an imagined orange. This example illustrates possible paths through the food system. It describes a hypothetical piece of fruit, carrying hypothetical consequences.

Before the orange reaches a shop, a tree occupies land and draws water. Someone tends the crop and harvests the fruit. Handling, packing and transport move it towards a buyer. By the time the orange enters a kitchen, much of the work that makes eating it possible has already happened. Its purchase price is one visible part of that history. The resources committed along the way form another part, spread across places the person eating it may never see.

Think of the journey as a relay carrying food towards a person who can eat it. Each handover preserves or weakens the chance of reaching that destination. Damage during handling may end the journey early. An ordering decision may send more fruit to a shop than its customers will use. Time in a kitchen may carry an edible orange past the point at which anyone wants it. The relay is an analogy: the real system contains many routes, businesses and choices, often operating at once.

These stages matter when reading the numbers. Food loss can occur during production and the supply chain before food reaches shops or consumers. The large UNEP estimate commonly used to describe food waste covers a later stretch: retail, food service and households. Keeping those boundaries visible helps explain both the scale of the problem and where particular actions might work.

A UNEP and WRAP press release published on March 27, 2024, reported an estimated **1.05 billion tonnes of food waste in 2022**, including inedible parts, across those three sectors. Households accounted for 60%, food service for 28%, and retail for 12%. A tonne is 1,000 kilograms. These figures are attributed here to the press release; independent verification against the underlying *Food Waste Index Report* tables and estimation method remains pending, including assessment of geographic coverage and uncertainty.

The estimate measures material across specified stages of the food journey, with a defined reference year. Because the total includes inedible parts, such as an orange peel, identifying the amount that could have nourished people requires a separate assessment of edible material and its condition. A weight of discarded food and a count of usable meals answer different questions.

That distinction changes how the number can guide action. A peel and an edible orange can both add weight to a waste collection. Their presence points towards different practical questions. One concerns handling material left after eating; the other concerns why food missed its chance to nourish someone. The reported aggregate gives an estimate of the measured stream’s size. Understanding opportunities within it requires looking at its contents and the circumstances that produced them.

The US Environmental Protection Agency’s Wasted Food Scale helps organize those opportunities by when they occur. Its environmental ranking places prevention first. Donation and upcycling, which turns suitable food ingredients into other food products, retain the purpose of feeding people. Other pathways include animal feed, anaerobic digestion and composting. Anaerobic digestion uses microbes to break down material in an oxygen-free setting, producing biogas and a remaining material; composting produces a soil amendment through decomposition.

The timing of each pathway explains much of the ranking. Prevention acts before a surplus becomes waste. In the imagined orange’s journey, better alignment between purchases and actual use could mean that a kitchen buys an amount it can eat. If that pattern persists and travels back through ordering decisions, a retailer may adjust its supply. The potential resource benefit grows when the change reaches decisions made earlier in the journey.

A single purchase adjustment has a small and uncertain influence on a wider market. Repeated changes across buyers and businesses could create a clearer signal. This is causal reasoning about how the proposed mechanism might work. Its strength depends on whether decisions at one stage actually influence the next.

Donation addresses a different moment. Suitable surplus already exists, and its remaining useful life creates an opportunity to feed someone. In the orange example, getting edible fruit to a person in time preserves its intended purpose. Timing, transport and food-safety conditions determine whether that route is suitable. Coordination becomes part of the food’s journey: the available fruit, a recipient and a workable delivery arrangement must meet while the opportunity remains open.

That human outcome deserves its own attention. Food reaching someone is a meaningful result even while its effect on future production remains uncertain. The person may gain nourishment they otherwise struggled to obtain. Alternatively, the donated food may replace something they would have purchased. Those possibilities have different implications for overall demand. They illustrate why the amount redistributed and the amount of production displaced answer separate questions.

Composting also has a role, especially for material whose useful future lies in returning nutrients and organic matter to soil. Yet its position comes later in the journey. The orange’s earlier cultivation, processing and transport remain part of its history. EPA explains that wasted food carries the resources used to grow, process, distribute and prepare it. A beneficial use for discarded material addresses what happens next; prevention can influence how much material enters that journey in the first place.

EPA’s scale ranks environmental pathways. Its stated scope leaves economic and social factors for separate consideration. A donation arrangement, for example, may require people, vehicles and coordination whose costs vary with circumstances. Suitability also depends on the food itself. The scale offers a way to understand environmental priorities while practical decisions remain tied to the particular material and setting.

The forest question begins where those immediate actions meet future production. The reasoning proceeds through a chain: more of the food already produced reaches people; the same nourishment can potentially be delivered with less additional production; businesses adjust purchases; producers respond; and land-use decisions change. Every link matters because a different response can alter the final outcome.

Suppose, as another explicit illustration, improved handling allows more of a crop to reach buyers. That gain could support the same amount of food reaching people from a smaller initial harvest. It could also make more food available for sale while production stays steady. Which result follows depends on demand and the choices of businesses and producers. An improvement in the food journey creates possibilities whose eventual direction needs observation.

Even a demonstrated reduction in production demand would leave a further question about land. Producers could respond by changing output on existing farmland, adjusting which crops they grow, or revising plans for expansion. A claim about forests requires evidence connecting the response to land-use decisions in the relevant place. The relationship between saved food and spared forest therefore varies with circumstances. Each outcome must be traced through the decisions that produce it.

This is why measuring discarded material provides a starting point. A kitchen’s lighter bin could reflect more careful purchasing and fuller use of ingredients. It could also reflect surplus being transferred to another location, where its eventual use remains to be established. Following the material to its destination helps distinguish those possibilities. The measurement becomes more informative when it records both the amount discarded and how much edible food reaches people.

Comparability matters as well. Measurements over time become useful when they cover the same stages, use consistent categories and relate the totals to the population being assessed. A changing boundary can make a trend look better or worse through accounting alone. Separating edible material from inedible parts also helps connect a change in weight with a change in food’s usefulness.

An imagined growing canteen offers a simple illustration. As it serves more people, its total discarded food could rise while waste per person served falls. A shrinking canteen could record a smaller total because fewer diners arrive. Both totals describe something real, but interpreting progress requires knowing how the number of diners changed. The example explains the idea of a denominator—the quantity used as the basis for a rate. The global policy target uses population as its per-capita basis.

That target is Sustainable Development Goal 12.3. By 2030, it aims to halve global food waste per person at retail and consumer levels, and to reduce food losses along production and supply chains. The verbs carry distinct commitments: halving applies to the per-capita retail and consumer measure; the upstream clause calls for reduction. Assessing either requires comparable evidence across time and within the appropriate boundary.

The dates tell separate parts of the story. The UN General Assembly proclaimed the international day in December 2019. The waste estimate discussed here describes 2022. The seventh observance took place in September 2026, and the policy deadline falls in 2030. The estimate offers a reference point, while the observance offers a setting for attention and coordination. Progress towards the target requires measurements that connect the intervening years.

UNEP and WRAP were already discussing improved measurement and partnerships in their March 2024 release. Those efforts show how institutions frame the work of reducing waste. The September 2026 event’s effect on later discards, purchasing or land use remains an open outcome question. Its documented contribution is the framing of a possibility that crosses several parts of the food system.

Following an orange makes that possibility easier to see. Eating it completes a journey sustained by land, water and work. Preventing an unnecessary surplus may influence the next order. A changed order may eventually influence production. A producer’s response may alter pressure to expand cultivated land. The distance between those decisions gives the forest claim its significance—and gives measurement its task.

When food leaves a field, how much of its journey ends in nourishment, and what evidence would show that a better journey left forests under less pressure?
