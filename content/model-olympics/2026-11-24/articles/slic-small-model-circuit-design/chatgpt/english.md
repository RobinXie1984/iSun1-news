# A Small AI Learns to Tune Amplifiers. Then the Factory Model Changes.

An amplifier can have its connections settled and still leave an engineer with a difficult problem: choosing the sizes and settings of the devices inside it. SLIC, a research system described by Yan Xu and eight coauthors in a paper dated 10 November 2026, brings a small language model into that search. It adjusts existing amplifier designs, checks their predicted electrical performance and tries again. The work explores a practical question for engineering AI: how much useful expertise can a compact model acquire through repeated feedback?

Analog circuits work with signals that vary continuously, including the electrical signals produced by microphones and sensors. An amplifier strengthens a signal so other circuitry can use it. Its usefulness depends on several properties working together. A strong output may be valuable, but the circuit also needs to handle the relevant signal frequencies, stay within its power allowance and remain stable. A designer must find settings that satisfy the whole assignment.

This creates a search full of coupled effects. Changing a transistor’s size can alter several aspects of a circuit’s behavior at once. A promising adjustment may improve amplification while increasing power consumption. Another may widen the range of frequencies the amplifier handles while reducing its stability. Engineers use circuit simulators to calculate these effects before committing a design to manufacture. Each calculation offers evidence for the next adjustment.

SLIC places a language model inside this cycle. Its scope is device sizing within an existing circuit topology: the pattern of connections among components. The connection pattern is supplied; the model chooses changes to device sizes and settings. Complete chip design encompasses many additional decisions. Here, the researchers isolate a bounded task with measurable results, giving the model a clear opportunity to learn from the consequences of its proposals.

An illustrative analogy is an already assembled radio with adjustable controls. The wiring represents the topology. Turning the controls represents sizing. A test instrument reports signal strength, the frequencies handled, power consumption and signs of instability. Several controls affect several readings, so finding a good combination takes care. In SLIC’s experiment, the instrument is a circuit simulator, and the results describe predicted electrical behavior. A manufactured radio or chip would require a further chain of engineering and testing.

The four targets in the study are gain, bandwidth, power consumption and phase margin. Gain describes how much a signal is amplified. Bandwidth describes the range of frequencies the amplifier can handle. Power consumption measures its electrical demand. Phase margin is a measure associated with stability, helping engineers assess whether feedback in the circuit could lead to unwanted oscillation. Success requires all four targets to be satisfied together.

The main model is Qwen3.5-4B, with roughly four billion parameters. These parameters are learned numerical settings inside the AI model; transistor sizes are separate quantities that the model proposes changing. Four billion parameters makes SLIC a relatively compact specialist in a field where larger general-purpose models also perform engineering tasks. Its appeal lies partly in the possibility of running that specialist close to the confidential design information it uses.

MIT News brought the research to a wider audience on 23 November 2026, following the paper’s release earlier that month. Its report describes the trained model as small enough for laptop use. That deployment claim concerns running an already trained model. The paper reports approximately 39 hours on two B200 graphics processing units for the main training run, placing the learning process in a substantially more powerful computing environment.

Training began with demonstrations generated by a teacher model using open process models. The researchers filtered those examples and used them to teach the smaller model through supervised fine-tuning, a process that adjusts a model toward demonstrated behavior. They then applied reinforcement learning across multiple turns. In this stage, the model received rewards tied to simulator results, allowing training to favor sequences of adjustments that produced successful circuits.

The reward system matters because an engineering task offers several tempting ways to achieve an incomplete victory. An amplifier might achieve impressive gain while drawing excessive power. SLIC’s training requires the four targets to be satisfied jointly before awarding an additional benefit for exceeding them. Validity checks reject unsuitable circuit states. These rules make the target more closely resemble the engineer’s assignment: a usable combination of properties within the defined constraints.

During an optimization attempt, SLIC proposes relative changes to circuit parameters, receives simulated electrical results and can revise its proposal. The system also produces a written rationale, giving an engineer an inspectable account of the proposed action and its stated purpose. That account provides material for review. Its physical accuracy and its fidelity to the model’s internal reasoning remain separate questions. The electrical assessment rests on the simulator and the device information behind it.

The researchers also tested ways of steering the sequence of attempts. Their method chooses among candidate continuations using feedback, with policies called Random, Softmax and Greedy. These are different procedures for deciding how to continue the search. The policy names become important when reading the results: the strongest reported SLIC configuration changes across evaluations, so each percentage belongs to a particular combination of tasks, steering method and allowed work.

The benchmark covers 41 amplifier topologies. Thirty-five appear in training and in the evaluation of familiar structures, while six are held out to test different connection patterns. The familiar-structure evaluation contains 840 tasks; the held-out evaluation contains 240. Each task supplies a starting design and target specifications. Familiarity therefore refers to the circuit structure encountered during training, while the evaluation still asks the model to carry out a defined sizing assignment.

The paper calls its success measure Pass@1. Here, one attempt means an entire optimization sequence, potentially involving several exchanges with the simulator. Success means meeting all four targets within the allowed turn budget, under the validity checks and a prescribed 5% tolerance. The percentage counts successful benchmark tasks. Manufacturing yield, by comparison, concerns the share of manufactured devices that meet requirements and would need physical production evidence.

With those definitions in place, the first result is encouraging for the specialist. On the 840 familiar-structure tasks, with a budget of four turns, SLIC using Random steering achieves 74.9% Pass@1. GPT-6-Astra, the larger comparison model, achieves 67.1%. Under this short interaction budget, the smaller trained system succeeds more often. It offers evidence that specialization can produce useful engineering behavior when the task and feedback are carefully defined.

Giving both systems eight turns changes the ranking. On the same familiar-structure benchmark, SLIC reaches 87.1%, while Astra reaches 90.6%, a lead of 3.5 percentage points for Astra. The additional opportunities to inspect feedback and revise a design affect the outcome. Model size alone provides an incomplete account of performance; training, search procedure and the number of allowed attempts all help determine what the system achieves.

The comparison also carries a measurement qualification. Most method results are averages across three evaluation runs, while Astra was evaluated once per task. Those repetition schedules differ, so the percentages deserve treatment as reported experimental results with their stated conditions. They establish competitive performance for the compact model in this study. A broad ranking across engineering work would require a much wider body of tests.

The six unfamiliar connection structures create a harder examination. On the 240 held-out tasks, within eight turns, SLIC using Softmax steering achieves 72.6%, against Astra’s 86.2%. The gap grows to 13.6 percentage points. SLIC retains substantial success on these new structures, while the larger model handles more of the assignments. The result gives the specialist’s promise a useful boundary: its performance changes as the circuit moves farther from the structures used in training.

Then the researchers change another foundation of the task: the information describing how the transistors behave.

SLIC’s policies were trained using open Predictive Technology Models, or PTM, at the 65-nanometer process node. A nanometer is one billionth of a meter. A process-node label identifies a manufacturing technology, whose devices have particular electrical characteristics; individual chip features have their own dimensions. The authors evaluated the trained policies across five unseen process nodes spanning 22 to 130 nanometers, keeping the learned policies fixed during transfer.

This probes a different kind of adaptability. A connection diagram can remain familiar while the devices available to implement it behave differently. The same proposed sizing adjustment can consequently produce different electrical results. Simulator feedback offers the model a way to respond, although the usefulness of its learned habits depends on how well they carry into the new setting.

The most consequential transfer test uses a TSMC 65-nanometer process design kit, or PDK, with Cadence Spectre simulation. A PDK supplies foundry-specific device information and design rules. It connects a design task to the characteristics of a particular manufacturing process. Both this commercial kit and the training model carry the 65-nanometer label, yet their electrical descriptions produce materially different outcomes in the experiment.

On the same held-out task set, with eight turns and Random steering, SLIC succeeds on 49.9% of tasks under the commercial PDK, compared with 70.6% under PTM. That is a decline of 20.7 percentage points. In the familiar-structure comparison, success falls from 86.9% under PTM to 55.8% under the commercial kit, a decline of 31.1 points. Many tasks remain solvable, and the process-specific change sharply reduces measured success.

The 86.9% PTM figure belongs to this commercial-transfer comparison. Table 1’s familiar-structure result is 87.1%; the inspected materials leave the precise evaluation details behind that small difference unresolved. Keeping each number attached to its reported comparison preserves the meaning of the larger finding. The commercial process information introduces a substantial difficulty even where the underlying circuit structures are familiar.

More attempts can also help under particular conditions. The paper reports 83.2% on a held-out PTM benchmark with a 32-turn budget. That result describes a longer search using the predictive process model. The commercial PDK result remains 49.9% under its eight-turn conditions. A longer commercial evaluation would answer a further question: how much of the transfer loss can additional feedback recover?

Local deployment gives that question practical significance. Circuit designs and foundry information can be commercially sensitive. Running the trained model where those inputs already reside keeps the inference workflow inside that environment and reduces the need to send design inputs to an externally hosted language-model service. The teacher-assisted training described in the paper used open process information; later local evaluation can use proprietary process data.

This architecture addresses a particular data-flow concern. Its overall security still depends on the surrounding software, access controls and operating environment. Likewise, laptop suitability is one part of usability; measured response times, energy consumption and the cost of the full simulation workflow would help establish its practical value. A model’s compact size and its benchmark success illuminate different parts of that assessment.

This account draws on the paper, institutional reporting and author-provided project resources inspected as of 24 November 2026. Its performance evidence consists of the authors’ reported simulations; independent reruns and physical implementation would provide further validation. Establishing production value would require deployment evidence and measured engineering outcomes. The project pages link code, data and traces for further examination. MIT News lists support including Analog Devices, IBM and the John L. Ocampo Endowment Fund of MACOM.

The paper’s acknowledged next steps extend beyond sizing against four specifications. A fuller design flow would include choosing circuit topologies, checking robustness across manufacturing variation, supply voltage and temperature, and arranging the actual physical layout. Noise and offset add further requirements: unwanted signal disturbances and shifts away from an intended electrical level. Engineers would still need verification and signoff appropriate to the final application.

SLIC’s contribution is a concrete demonstration of a compact model learning to adjust circuits through measurable feedback, together with evidence of where that ability weakens. Its strongest results emerge inside a clearly defined simulation task. The commercial process test brings the research closer to a consequential engineering setting and reveals a substantial performance drop. As specialists like this move toward practical use, the question becomes precise: how much useful tuning ability survives when the simulator begins describing the devices an engineer actually plans to manufacture?
