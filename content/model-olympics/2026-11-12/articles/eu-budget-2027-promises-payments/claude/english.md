# Europe's Provisional 2027 Budget Deal Would Promise €199.7 Billion and Pay Out €211.5 Billion. Why Is the Second Number Bigger?

Early on 11 November 2026, in Brussels, negotiators for the European Union reached a provisional agreement on how the EU will spend money in 2027. The negotiations brought together two institutions. The Council of the EU speaks for the governments of the 27 member states. The European Parliament speaks for lawmakers elected by voters across the Union. The European Commission, the EU's executive body, wrote the draft budget and helped steer the talks. Ireland currently holds the Council's rotating presidency, so Ireland's finance minister and Tánaiste (deputy prime minister), Simon Harris, chaired the budget talks for the member states.

The EU budget is a shared layer of public money that sits alongside national budgets. It pays for things the member states have agreed to fund together, including regional development, agriculture and environment programmes, research, border management and help for neighbouring countries. Each year's budget fits inside a longer plan called the multiannual financial framework, or MFF. The MFF is a seven-year spending framework that sets ceilings, which are upper limits on how much the EU may spend in each broad area. The current framework runs from 2021 to 2027, so this deal covers its final year.

The agreement remains a negotiated draft. According to the Council, the Council and Parliament have 14 days from the 11 November agreement to approve it formally. Within that period, the Council expects member-state governments to endorse the deal on 23 November, and that endorsement forms the Council's part of the approval. The vote requires a qualified majority, a double threshold that counts both the number of member states in favour and the share of the EU population they represent. Until both institutions complete approval, the 2027 budget exists as a provisional agreement, and its figures describe what the negotiators agreed the budget would authorise once approved.

The headline figures contain a puzzle. Under the deal, the budget would provide €199.7 billion in commitments and €211.5 billion in payments. Subtract the first figure from the second and the payments column comes out about €11.8 billion higher. That €11.8 billion is an arithmetic comparison between two accounting measures of the same budget; it describes the distance between two columns, and it stands apart from any extra pot of money or new spending line. A reader could reasonably wonder whether the higher payments figure signals a shortfall, an overspend or a hidden loan. The answer lies in a feature of public accounting that turns out to be surprisingly ordinary: the budget runs on two clocks.

## Two clocks: promises and invoices

The Council's release defines the two terms. A commitment is the legal authority to make a spending promise in a given year, such as signing a grant agreement or a contract. The activity that promise pays for can stretch across several years. A payment is money that actually leaves the budget in a given year. Payments in 2027 cover bills arising from commitments made in 2027 and from commitments made in earlier years.

An everyday picture helps here. It is purely an illustration and describes how timing works in general; it describes no actual EU contract. Imagine a family that signs a contract in January to renovate its house over three years. The signature is a promise covering the whole job. The builder then sends invoices as each stage finishes: some this year, more next year, the rest the year after. Now imagine the family signs a much smaller contract the following year, perhaps for a new garden fence. In that second year, the family's new promises are small, while its payments are large, because invoices from the big renovation keep arriving. Someone looking at only that second year would see payments exceeding new promises. The explanation is simply that the bills for an older promise are coming due.

The EU budget behaves in a similar way. Many EU programmes fund multi-year activities, such as a regional infrastructure project or a research consortium, and the money flows out in stages as work progresses. Some commitments are settled within the same year; others are paid over time. The 2021–2027 framework is now in its last year, and the payments column for 2027 includes money owed under promises made earlier in that period. The Council's materials state the general rule. The documents reviewed for this article leave the year-by-year breakdown open, so how much of the 2027 payments total traces back to each earlier year sits outside what they report.

This timing question is different from the question of whether the books balance. On that second point, EU rules are direct: the Council's explanation of the annual budget states that the EU's yearly revenue and expenditure must balance. So the €11.8 billion gap between the two columns reflects the fact that they measure different things on different clocks: the payments column includes bills from earlier years' promises, while the commitments column counts the new promises for 2027. The gap compares new commitments with planned payments; whether the budget balances depends on a separate comparison, between the year's revenue and its expenditure. The two headline figures are also two views of one budget. Adding them together to describe a €411 billion budget would count the same plan twice.

There is a useful way to keep the scale in mind. The Council expresses the budget relative to the EU's gross national income, or GNI, a broad measure of the total income earned by people and businesses in the member states over a year. Commitments come to 0.99% of GNI and payments to 1.05%. Put in everyday terms, for every €100 of income across the Union, the 2027 budget would commit about 99 cents in new promises and plans to pay out about €1.05. These percentages describe size relative to income. They are separate from tax rates, from shares of GDP and from national budgets, which remain far larger and fund most public services such as pensions, schools and hospitals.

## What the table would distribute

The Council published a table with the agreed figures, set out in millions of euros. Its exact totals are €199,707.5 million in commitments and €211,525.8 million in payments, which round to the headline €199.7 billion and €211.5 billion.

Two headings dominate. The heading for cohesion, resilience and values, which covers regional development and related programmes, would receive €75.8 billion in commitments and €81.8 billion in payments. Natural resources and environment, the heading that includes agriculture and environmental spending, would receive €57.1 billion in commitments and €61.3 billion in payments. By simple arithmetic on the table, those two headings together account for about two thirds of all new commitments.

Next come the single market, innovation and digital heading, at €22.0 billion in commitments and €22.9 billion in payments, and the neighbourhood and the world heading, which funds the EU's external action, at €15.5 billion and €16.7 billion. Public administration, which covers the running costs of the EU institutions, shows €13.9 billion in both columns.

Smaller headings show the two clocks running the other way. Migration and border management has €5.8 billion in commitments and €3.9 billion in payments. Security and defence has €3.1 billion in commitments and €2.2 billion in payments. In these areas, new promises in 2027 would exceed the money expected to flow out that year. Read alongside the Council's definitions, that pattern is consistent with activity whose invoices are expected to arrive later, much like the renovation in the illustration at the start of its schedule.

Two lines deserve careful reading. The first is labelled as a Ukraine support loan and shows €650.0 million in both columns. That figure is the annual budget line attached to that label. The size of the loan itself, and the precise purpose of this yearly amount, belong to documentation beyond the Council's release, so the €650 million is best read as one year's budget line and kept separate from any estimate of the loan's principal. The second is a line for special instruments, at €5.9 billion in commitments and €8.3 billion in payments. These are separately labelled tools whose detailed workings also sit in further documentation.

The headings show where the negotiators have agreed that money would go once the budget is formally approved. They measure budget capacity. They leave open which projects will succeed, who will receive payments, and what results will follow. A large figure for cohesion describes the scale of an intention; the outcomes arrive later, if they arrive, through thousands of individual projects.

## How the compromise was reached

The annual budget follows a set sequence. The Commission proposes a draft. The Council and the Parliament then each adopt a position and negotiate. Under EU rules, the two institutions agree the annual budget on an equal footing, so neither can impose its version on the other. If they reach a deal, both must formally approve it.

The two sides represent different constituencies. Member-state governments, which pay most of the contributions that fund the budget, often press for restraint. Parliament, whose members answer directly to voters, often presses to protect or expand programmes. Both work within the ceilings set by the MFF.

This year's sequence followed that pattern. On 21 October 2026, Parliament adopted its negotiating position by 400 votes to 189, with 84 abstentions. According to the Parliament's Legislative Observatory summary, that resolution criticised cuts the Council had made to the draft, sought to restore them and proposed reinforcing selected programmes. Three weeks later, on 11 November, the two sides reached their compromise. The October document records what Parliament asked for at the start of the bargaining; the November table records what the negotiators agreed. The amounts Parliament requested in October belong to that earlier stage, and the final figures come from the November table.

The Irish Presidency's account confirms the timing, the Commission's role in facilitating the talks and Harris's role in leading them for the 27 governments. It is the account of a participant in the negotiation. It describes how agreement was reached; evidence about how well the money is spent will come from later stages.

The Council of the EU, the body in these talks, is made up of national ministers. It is distinct from the European Council, where the EU's heads of state and government meet, and from the Council of Europe, a separate organisation focused on human rights.

## The narrow room under the ceiling

The Council's release adds one more figure that matters for the year ahead. Under the agreement, €710.1 million would remain available under the MFF ceilings to respond to unforeseen needs. The release describes this as room kept available in the deal.

A rough comparison conveys its scale. Divided by the €199,707.5 million in commitments, the margin works out to about 0.36%. That is roughly 36 cents of headroom for every €100 of new promises. This ratio is a simple calculation for perspective; it is separate from any official reserve measure.

The margin's meaning needs precision. It is authorised room under spending limits, much like the unused portion of an approved spending limit on a household plan. It describes how much more the EU may commit before reaching its ceilings. It is separate from a pile of cash sitting in an account, and separate from the special instruments line in the table. It also describes one source of flexibility among the tools the EU may have for responding to events; it falls short of a complete inventory of crisis resources.

Still, the figure raises a natural question. 2027 is the final year of a seven-year framework, and the ceilings were set years ago. If floods, an economic shock or new security needs arrive during 2027, how much space will the regular budget have to respond?

## The tests still ahead

The agreement marks the end of one stage and the beginning of several others.

The first test is formal approval. Both institutions have 14 days from the 11 November agreement to approve the deal, and the Council's endorsement, expected on 23 November, is its part of that approval. If a budget is ever adopted after the year begins, EU rules provide a fallback known as provisional twelfths. Under that arrangement, spending continues month by month, with each budget chapter limited to one twelfth of the smaller of the previous year's amounts or the draft amounts. Spending continues, at a constrained pace, while the institutions finish their work. This mechanism is a contingency set out in the rules; the current timetable points toward approval before the year starts.

The second test is implementation. According to the Council's explanation, member states implement about three quarters of EU spending, through national and regional authorities that select projects and pay recipients, while the Commission retains ultimate responsibility for implementation. That three-quarters figure describes how the system generally works; it is separate from a measured share for 2027. Implementation is where commitments turn into contracts and contracts turn into invoices, so this is where the two clocks keep ticking. A promise made in 2027 may produce payments in 2028 or later, under the next long-term framework.

The third test arrives after the money is spent. Each year, the European Court of Auditors examines the EU's accounts and reports on them. The Council then issues a recommendation, and the European Parliament decides whether to grant discharge, its formal sign-off on how the Commission implemented the budget. That process considers the accounts, the results achieved and the auditors' findings. For the 2027 budget, it lies years away.

So far, the record shows a provisional deal, a published table, a timetable for approval and a small reported margin. Detailed programme allocations lie beyond the documents reviewed here. Formal adoption, actual payments and audited results belong to the weeks, months and years ahead. Political statements about the budget's value describe the intentions of the people who negotiated it; measured evidence about results will come from implementation reports and audits.

The puzzle in the headline figures, then, has a clear answer: the 2027 payments figure can exceed the year's new commitments because the payments column also covers bills arising from earlier promises, while the commitments column counts the promises made for 2027, and the rules require the year's revenue and expenditure to balance. The more consequential question begins where that explanation ends. In the final year of a seven-year plan, with €710.1 million of reported room under its ceilings and older promises continuing to turn into bills, will this budget have enough flexibility to meet surprises, and will the money that flows out in 2027 produce results that the auditors, and the public, eventually recognise as worth the cost?
