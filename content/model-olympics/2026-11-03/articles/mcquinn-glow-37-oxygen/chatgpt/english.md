# These Galaxies Still Have Their Gas. Where Did Their Oxygen Go?

A galaxy’s gas carries the history of its stars. Stars form from it, manufacture heavier elements and return enriched material to their surroundings as they age or explode. Later generations inherit some of that material. Across billions of years, this exchange changes both the galaxy and the space around it. Oxygen offers astronomers a way to follow the process: it is relatively abundant, measurable and closely connected to the lives of massive stars.

Small galaxies make this history especially intriguing. Their weaker gravitational grip gives material propelled by stellar explosions a greater chance of escaping. Even so, many dwarf galaxies remain rich in gas and continue forming stars. How much of their stellar handiwork survives inside them?

A census of 37 nearby dwarf galaxies puts a striking number on that question. Their inventoried stars, stellar remnants, gas and dust contain, on average, about 11% of the oxygen their stars are estimated to have produced over their lifetimes. In an arithmetic illustration using 100 units of estimated production, roughly 11 remain in those components and 89 fall outside the inventory. That illustration represents the sample average; individual galaxies have their own histories and results.

The central mystery concerns the address of those other 89 units. Some oxygen may surround the galaxies in diffuse gas. Some may eventually return. Some may have travelled into the wider space between galaxies. The census establishes a substantial gap inside the inventoried galactic bodies, while the destination of most of the oxygen remains unresolved.

NASA brought the research to public attention on November 2, 2026, reporting publication that day in *The Astrophysical Journal*. The numerical findings had already appeared in the August 13 version of *GLOW II: A Census of Oxygen in Low-Mass Galaxies*, by Kristen B. W. McQuinn, O. Grace Telford and colleagues. The figures discussed here come from that August preprint; independent verification against the final journal text remains pending.

McQuinn leads GLOW, short for Galaxies Losing Oxygen via Winds. She is an associate professor at Rutgers University and heads the Roman Space Telescope’s Science Operations Center at the Space Telescope Science Institute. Telford, a co-investigator, is at the University of Utah. Their NASA- and National Science Foundation-funded project brings together several observatories to reconstruct how these small galaxies make, hold and exchange chemical elements.

The selected galaxies lie within six megaparsecs of Earth. A megaparsec equals about 3.26 million light-years, placing the outer limit at roughly 20 million light-years. Their stellar masses range from about three million to three billion times the Sun’s mass. These are units of mass: individual stars can weigh considerably more or less than the Sun.

Proximity matters because Hubble can distinguish individual stars in these systems. Those separate points of light provide clues to a galaxy’s past that become harder to recover as distance blends the stars together. The sample therefore offers a detailed view of a particular population: nearby, gas-rich, star-forming dwarf galaxies with relatively low concentrations of heavy elements.

Astronomers call elements heavier than hydrogen and helium “metals,” placing oxygen in a category that sounds surprising in everyday language. Here, oxygen means the chemical element present in astronomical material. Its atoms can occur in several physical and chemical states. Breathable oxygen molecules belong to a different setting.

Hydrogen and helium account for much of a galaxy’s gas. A galaxy can consequently possess a substantial supply of gas while retaining a small share of the oxygen its stars have made. That combination is physically possible because fresh oxygen begins its journey near powerful sources of energy. Massive stars and supernova explosions release enriched material and can help drive it outward.

Following that journey requires distinguishing three places. The main galactic body contains stars, dust and the gas between stars, much of it organized into a disk. Beyond that body lies a diffuse gaseous halo, called the circumgalactic medium. Farther afield lies the intergalactic medium, the material between galaxies. Oxygen leaving the disk may still remain within its galaxy’s gravitational reach, with a possible route back into future stars.

GLOW’s achievement is to turn part of this movement into a historical inventory. Doing so requires two calculations: how much oxygen the galaxy’s stars probably made, and how much can currently be assigned to its principal visible components.

Archival Hubble images supply the colors and brightnesses of individual stars. By comparing their patterns with calculations of stellar evolution, researchers infer when stars formed and how their chemical compositions changed. These histories help estimate the oxygen stored in stars and stellar remnants.

Infrared observations from the retired Spitzer Space Telescope help establish stellar mass. The team uses light measured at a wavelength of 3.6 microns, beyond the visible range, and a model-based conversion between light and mass. Radio observations from the Very Large Array and the Australian Telescope Compact Array constrain the supply of atomic hydrogen. Measurements of oxygen abundance in gas, together with dust estimates, fill out the retained inventory.

The production side requires a reconstruction of all the stellar mass formed over time. Present-day stellar mass supplies a starting point, adjusted for material that stars have returned to gas. The researchers then apply an assumed constant oxygen yield: an amount of newly manufactured oxygen per unit of material turned into stars.

That calculation depends on assumptions about the mix of stellar birth masses and the proportion of material returned by aging stars and explosions. Its result is an inferred lifetime production total. Both sides of the final comparison therefore combine observations with models, and the meaning of the result depends on those ingredients.

One check involved two sets of stellar-evolution calculations, called PARSEC and MIST. They gave similar final oxygen budgets. This agreement helps assess sensitivity to the stellar models, especially because stars hold a relatively small portion of the oxygen retained in these galaxies. Other assumptions, including oxygen production and gas composition, retain their own uncertainties.

Gas dominates the retained oxygen inventory. Stars and their remnants contain less; dust contains the smallest share. Gas also contributes the largest uncertainty among those reservoirs.

The difficulty extends beyond measuring how much hydrogen exists. A galaxy’s outer gas may have a different oxygen concentration from its inner regions. Applying one concentration across an extended hydrogen disk can change the inferred oxygen total. The team therefore chooses a defined measurement boundary that balances coverage against the available information about gas composition. The oxygen fraction belongs to that inventory and its assumptions.

Within this framework, the mean retained fraction is 10.7%. The standard deviation across the galaxies is 5.1 percentage points, describing their spread around the mean. Individual estimates range from approximately 3.8% to 24.8%, and 29 of the 37 galaxies retain 15% or less. The highest fractions carry particularly large uncertainties.

The pattern is thus shared by much of the sample, with meaningful variation between galaxies. It also raises a second puzzle: familiar measurements of galactic chemistry can look convincing while the historical inventory tells a more difficult story.

Astronomers often compare a galaxy’s stellar mass with its gas-phase oxygen abundance—the concentration of oxygen relative to hydrogen. More massive galaxies generally have higher abundances. This pattern, called the mass–metallicity relation, is an important test for simulations of galaxy evolution.

An everyday illustration helps separate that test from GLOW’s. Imagine a pot of salty soup. A spoonful reveals the salt concentration in the soup at that moment. Working out what share of all the salt ever added remains in the pot also requires the amount of soup left and the history of additions and removals. Concentration and historical retention answer different questions.

GLOW applies the second kind of question to galaxies. In this sample, a galaxy’s position above or below the usual mass–metallicity relation gives an unclear guide to its retained oxygen fraction. The authors find an absence of a clear correlation between those quantities. A familiar chemical concentration can accompany a substantially depleted lifetime inventory.

That distinction becomes consequential when the researchers compare their results with three families of computer simulations. These simulations calculate how gas moves, forms stars, receives energy from stellar activity and exchanges material with its surroundings. All three reproduce the observed mass–metallicity relations. They nevertheless generally predict higher retention of oxygen or heavy elements than GLOW infers.

As reported in the GLOW paper, simulations by Muratov and colleagues predict retention about two to four times higher across the relevant mass range. Those by Christensen and colleagues lie mostly within a factor of two of the observations, while remaining higher overall. Simulations by Piacitelli and colleagues show substantial variation; a few approach the GLOW results, and most retain a few times more material.

The comparison has several moving parts. Two simulation sets report heavy elements collectively, while the Piacitelli comparison is adjusted specifically for oxygen. Stellar yields differ, as do assumptions about material returned by stars and the treatment of energy that drives outflows. The physical region counted as a galaxy’s gas also varies.

These differences affect the precise size of the disagreement. They also identify what the census adds: a model can reproduce a relationship between mass and concentration while giving a different answer about how much stellar-produced material remains. The historical inventory provides an additional test of the physical exchanges behind the familiar graph.

Gravity offers part of the explanation for oxygen loss, although the 37 galaxies resist a simple ranking. The paper describes modest tendencies toward greater retention with higher stellar mass, greater gas content and indicators of stronger gravitational confinement. Substantial scatter limits how firmly those tendencies can be read.

Neighborhood may matter too. Galaxies more than one megaparsec—about 3.26 million light-years—from a neighbor exceeding a billion solar masses in stars consistently have low retention in this sample. Closer systems show a wider spread, with some galaxies in denser surroundings retaining more oxygen. Returning material, incoming enriched gas and interactions are possible explanations. The small environmental subsamples leave those mechanisms tentative.

The authors also explore a simpler model that can reproduce several observational constraints when winds preferentially carry enriched material away. Its successful settings include outflowing material with twice the oxygen abundance of the interstellar gas, a wind strength that varies with stellar mass and reduced gas accretion. This is a possible explanation fitted to the evidence. The actual wind compositions and lifetime flows of the 37 galaxies remain separate observational questions.

Beyond the disks, astronomers already have clues. Earlier studies examined ultraviolet light from bright background objects as it passed through diffuse gas around dwarf galaxies. Oxygen ions—atoms whose electrons have been stripped away—can absorb particular wavelengths, leaving signatures in that light.

One such form is called O VI. GLOW’s summary of earlier research places oxygen observed in this state at roughly 1–7% of estimated stellar oxygen production. Translating that figure into all the oxygen in a halo requires knowing what fraction occupies the O VI state. Temperature, density and radiation influence that fraction.

Under a conditional assumption that O VI represents 20% of the oxygen, the inferred total halo reservoir could amount to about 5–35% of production. Those percentages describe an assumption-dependent interpretation of comparison studies. The studies cover different galaxy populations and distances, including some seen at earlier cosmic times.

The available beams of background light also sample only a small number of paths through each halo. Many nearby measurements involve other elements or yield upper limits. Together, these constraints leave considerable room for oxygen in difficult-to-measure states and locations. The majority of the missing disk inventory still has an unsettled address.

NASA reports that McQuinn has an approved Roman program to search broad areas of sky for even fainter nearby galaxies. She hopes to follow suitable discoveries with deeper Hubble and Webb observations, extending the reconstruction of stellar histories to smaller systems. Telford is studying massive stars in nearby GLOW targets to investigate the objects responsible for producing oxygen and helping propel it outward. These efforts represent planned and ongoing work.

For now, archival starlight, infrared measurements, radio maps and chemical estimates have supplied a new constraint across 37 galaxies. Their gas remains plentiful, their recorded oxygen retention is low, and simulations face a sharper test of how material moves through them. The next part of the story turns on a question that reaches beyond each visible disk: how much of a galaxy’s chemical history remains close enough to come home?
