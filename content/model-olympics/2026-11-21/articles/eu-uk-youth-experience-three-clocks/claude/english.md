# Britain and the EU Promise 100,000 Youth Places Each Way. When Does the Door Open?

On 20 November 2026, the leaders of the European Union and the United Kingdom met in Brussels and announced a figure that sounds simple. Negotiations had concluded on a Youth Experience Scheme. It would let 100,000 young people from the EU live in the UK, and 100,000 young people from the UK live in the EU, for up to three years. Three leaders represented the two sides. António Costa is President of the European Council, the gathering of the EU's national leaders that sets the bloc's political direction. Ursula von der Leyen is President of the European Commission, the EU's executive arm, which handles much of the bloc's negotiating and day-to-day policy work. Andy Burnham is the UK's Prime Minister. This was the second EU–UK summit. Its youth announcement raises a practical question for any young person reading the news: when could someone actually pack a bag and go?

The answer depends on a story that began years earlier. In a 2016 referendum, the UK voted to leave the EU, and Costa referred to that vote in his remarks after the summit. The UK formally left in January 2020. A transition period then kept free movement in place until the end of 2020. When that period finished, the arrangement under which British and EU citizens could move to each other's countries to live, work and study with relatively few formalities came to an end. A set of treaties now governs the relationship. The Withdrawal Agreement settled the terms of departure. The Windsor Framework adjusted the arrangements for Northern Ireland. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement, usually shortened to TCA, became the main rulebook for trade and cooperation between the two sides.

Since then, both sides have looked for practical ways to work more closely while the UK remains outside the EU. The first summit, held in London on 19 May 2025, began that effort. The Brussels meeting continued it and built on the existing treaties. The EU's official account describes deeper cooperation between the bloc and a neighbouring country. UK membership lies outside the scope of the talks.

## One number, two directions

The youth figure comes from the joint statement that both sides published after the meeting. It is an 18-page document that the EU's Council records as document 16174/26. Its youth paragraph attaches 100,000 places to young people from the EU and 100,000 to young people from the UK, respectively. That last word changes the arithmetic. The figure applies in each direction: one allowance of 100,000 for EU youth heading to Britain, and a separate allowance of 100,000 for British youth heading to the continent. Across both directions, the scheme's total scale reaches 200,000 people.

That figure measures capacity, meaning how many people the negotiated scheme is designed to cover. Other details are left for later documents. The statement describes the overall size and leaves any yearly quota undefined. The age range, the way places would be allocated, any application fee, any salary requirement and the application process itself also await future rules. A reader therefore learns how big the scheme is meant to be. Whether those places would be used over a few years or spread across a longer period remains a question for the detailed rules.

The second number is three years. Under the scheme, a young person could live in the other territory for up to three years. The statement lists five purposes: work, study, au pairing, volunteering and travel. An au pair is a young person who lives with a host family and helps with childcare, usually in return for a room and a modest allowance. Each side would run the route under its own legal framework. British rules would govern stays in Britain, and the EU side's framework would govern stays in EU countries. Three years is the longest stay one person could have. The length of any individual permission would depend on the final rules, and three years answers a separate question from when the scheme begins.

So the youth paragraph contains two different measures. One is a count of people. The other is a length of time for a single person. Read together quickly, they can give the impression of a ready-made programme. Read carefully, they describe the shape of a scheme whose opening date sits on a separate clock.

## Two dates, two different goals

That clock is where the suspense lies. The joint statement gives two target dates for the youth scheme, and each one answers a different question.

The first target concerns the agreement. Both sides aim to apply it no later than 2028. Applying an agreement means bringing its legal terms into operation, so that both governments are bound to carry them out. The second target concerns people. The two sides will make efforts to have opportunities available to young people no later than January 2030. That date points to the moment when a young person could actually take up a place.

Both dates are written as intentions, a goal for the first and a commitment to make efforts for the second. Each one marks the latest point for its own milestone. The agreement could apply earlier than 2028, and opportunities could become available earlier than January 2030, so the actual interval between the two steps remains open. The published statement records both deadlines and leaves the reason for their different dates unstated. One reasonable interpretation, offered here as analysis, is that a new residence route needs immigration rules, application systems and trained staff, and those steps take time after the legal agreement itself. The statement offers that pattern as a possibility only through its two dates. It gives no explanation of its own.

The status in November 2026 is therefore clear. Negotiations on the scheme have concluded. Legal application is targeted for 2028 at the latest. Usable opportunities are targeted for January 2030 at the latest. The published documents leave the youth scheme's application process and opening date for a future announcement, and the 2028 date describes an aim rather than a confirmed launch.

A simple, hypothetical comparison helps keep these clocks apart. Imagine a town council that votes to build a new public library designed to handle 100,000 visits. The council sets a target date for signing the contract with the company that will run the building. It then sets a later target date for opening the doors. Each borrower may keep a book for up to three weeks. A resident who reads about the vote in the local paper learns the library's planned size and the borrowing rule. Opening day belongs to a later announcement. In this imagined town, size, contract and opening day are three separate facts, and the three-week loan is a fourth. The comparison serves only to clarify order and timing. Demand for the youth scheme, the way its places would be shared out and the pace of its rollout are matters for the real rules.

## Three tracks that are easy to blur

Families and students following the summit will meet three separate subjects in the same news reports. Each one moves on its own track.

The first is Erasmus+. This is the EU's long-running programme that funds exchanges and cooperation in education, training, youth work and sport. Its reach is wide. It covers school, vocational training, higher education and adult education, as well as youth activities and sport. The joint statement places UK participation in Erasmus+ in 2027. It also records that a funding call opened on 18 November 2026, two days before the summit. That call is aimed at eligible institutions, meaning the organisations that run exchanges and apply for money to support them. Individuals usually take part through those organisations. Opportunities beyond 2027 are described in the statement as something both sides will explore. This dated call opens a door for institutions.

The second track is the Youth Experience Scheme itself. It is a proposed residence route, meaning a legal basis for living in the other territory, and it covers several kinds of activity. Its timetable, as described above, points to 2028 at the latest for the agreement and January 2030 at the latest for opportunities.

The third track is tuition fees, the charges that universities set for teaching. Fees matter greatly to cross-border students because a student's fee status can change the cost of a degree considerably. The joint statement says both sides will work towards an agreement, supplementing the TCA, on fees for EU students in the UK and UK students in the EU. The intention is for it to apply whatever immigration route a student uses. That means a student arriving through the youth scheme and one arriving on another study route would come under the same fee arrangement. Both sides aim to conclude these negotiations within six months of the summit, which places the target around May 2027. That deadline applies to the talks. Fee levels, any entitlement to the lower fees charged to domestic students, any cap or discount and any start date all await the final agreement. Enrolment and charging would follow later steps.

Progress on one of these tracks gives information about that track alone. An open Erasmus+ funding call tells institutions they can apply for money. A concluded youth scheme negotiation tells readers what the route is designed to look like. A six-month fee target tells readers when talks are meant to finish. A young person might care about all three. Each still needs its own confirmation before it shapes anyone's plans.

## The bigger pattern: cooperation in pieces

The youth scheme belongs to a larger pattern in which the two sides reconnect selected parts of their relationship, one field at a time, with each piece carrying its own legal steps and target date.

The clearest example concerns food and farming. Negotiations have concluded on a common sanitary and phytosanitary area. The term covers animal health, plant health and the related controls on food. In plain terms, it deals with the checks that make sure meat, plants and other farm products crossing a border are safe and disease-free. Both sides aim to apply this arrangement on 1 March 2028. It would remove existing certificates and checks for a specified, very large majority of these movements. Customs procedures, taxes and the border itself would remain.

A second example concerns climate policy. Both the EU and the UK run emissions trading systems. Under these systems, certain companies need a permit, called an allowance, for each tonne of carbon dioxide they emit, and they can buy and sell those permits. Negotiations on linking the two systems have concluded, with the aim of applying the link in 2028. Recognising each other's allowances and arranging exemptions from carbon border charges, which are levies on some imports based on their carbon content, remain subject to each side's laws and procedures. The joint statement also speaks of bringing the food-controls, emissions-trading and youth agreements into force jointly and in good time. That wording suggests the three are meant to travel as a package, which links the youth scheme's timetable to the progress of the others.

Security forms part of the same picture. Ukraine was on the summit agenda, and negotiations have concluded on a framework for UK participation in EU civilian missions. These are operations that send advisers such as police officers, legal experts and border specialists to help other countries. The framework sets the terms for taking part. Joint work on any particular mission would follow later decisions.

Costa's remarks after the summit set out how far this pattern can go. He said a closer future relationship with the UK remains possible. He also restated the EU's founding principles for its single market, the shared economic area where four freedoms apply: the free movement of goods, services, people and capital. In the EU's view, those four freedoms form one indivisible package, rights must be balanced with obligations, and relations with the UK must stay consistent with relations with other countries outside the bloc. That is the EU's negotiating position, stated by the EU's side, and any future choices by the UK are for the UK to make. It also helps explain the shape of the youth scheme. It is a capped, reciprocal route with a fixed upper stay, which places it well short of the open free movement that comes with full single-market membership.

## What the evidence shows so far

The sources behind this account all come from EU institutions: the European Council's summit page, the joint statement, Costa's press conference remarks and the official summit announcement. They reliably show what the two sides jointly declared and what the EU said. A separate UK government account, the final legal texts and the rules for putting them into practice are still to be examined. The material contains forecasts of neither participation levels nor economic effects, and the 100,000 figures are provisions in a political statement. They describe a design, and the number of people who actually take part will become measurable once the scheme operates. The EU's summary page and the detailed joint statement agree with each other. The detailed statement supplies the timetable that the shorter summaries compress.

Several documents would turn the headline into something a young person could use: the final legal text of the youth scheme; published rules on age, eligibility, fees and the method for counting the 100,000 places in each direction; an announced date when applications open; and, on the separate fees track, a concluded agreement with a stated start date. Around May 2027 sits the six-month target by which both sides aim to conclude the fee negotiations. Meeting that target would complete the talks; a signed agreement and a fee arrangement students could use would come in later steps. The Erasmus+ arrangement points to 2027, the youth agreement's application to 2028 at the latest, and the youth opportunities to January 2030 at the latest.

Taken together, the timetable sets up the central question. The capacity is announced, the maximum stay is set, and two latest dates are on the calendar. The open question is whether the separate legal and administrative steps arrive in time for the door to open by January 2030.
