# Can a Dry Patch Keep Ultrasound Connected to the Body in Motion?

Ultrasound gives doctors a view inside the body by sending in sound and reading the echoes. Keeping that view steady as a person moves is a challenge for wearable imaging. On November 19, 2026, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology reported a dry, stretchy material designed to maintain the connection between an ultrasound probe and skin. Researchers at MIT, a US engineering and research university, and its Singapore research partnership developed the material as an ultrasound interface: the part through which sound must pass on its journey into the body and back.

The familiar gel used during an ultrasound examination serves this same essential purpose. Skin and a probe can appear to touch while tiny gaps remain between them. Air in those gaps impedes the sound. Gel fills the spaces, giving the sound a more continuous path. The probe’s transducer, the component that sends and receives sound, then collects returning echoes. Their timing helps the system work out where structures lie and build an image. As the US National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering explains, diagnostic ultrasound uses sound above human hearing; X-ray imaging uses ionising radiation.

During an examination, keeping the probe in useful contact is part of the operator’s work. A wearable device takes that practical task into a changing setting. Skin stretches, sweat gathers, and the person carrying the device chooses the movements. An interface has to accommodate those changes while continuing to let sound through. That makes the material touching the body a central engineering problem, even though most attention naturally goes to the image on the screen.

A clearly illustrative comparison is a flexible gasket fitted between two moving surfaces. A good gasket follows small irregularities and helps maintain contact as the surfaces shift. An ultrasound interface has an analogous job, with an extra demand: sound must travel through it efficiently. The image still depends on a transducer generating sound, electronics receiving the echoes, and a system interpreting them. The dry material occupies one particular place in that chain.

This distinction gives the advance its scale. Better contact could make sustained imaging more practical. Its value would come from helping the rest of the imaging system continue doing its job while the wearer goes about an activity. The central question begins at a small boundary, where a manufactured surface meets living skin.

The researchers approached that boundary through polymer design. Polymers are materials built from long molecular chains. According to MIT’s account, tangled chains in an ordinary solid polymer can dissipate sound energy through internal friction. The team used a bottlebrush structure, with side chains extending from a main molecular backbone, to reduce that friction. Early larger samples crumbled under their own weight. Chemically joining a second, loosely connected bottlebrush network improved toughness and stretchability while retaining the desired transmission properties.

The study was led by MIT postdoctoral researcher Shu Wang, with fellow postdoctoral researcher Runze Li as co-lead, in Xuanhe Zhao’s laboratory. The collaborators include SMART WITEC, the Wearable Imaging for Transforming Elderly Care project within the Singapore-MIT Alliance for Research and Technology.

The crumbly samples reveal why this is a materials problem with competing demands. Sound consists of travelling pressure changes. As those changes pass through a material, some of their energy can be spent moving its internal structure. Friction turns part of that energy into heat, leaving less to continue along the sound path. For ultrasound imaging, the returning echo also has to cross the interface, so the material participates in both directions of the journey.

At the same time, a useful patch needs enough strength to survive handling and movement. A substance that carries sound well in a small sample can still fall apart as a larger piece. Conversely, making a material mechanically stronger can change how it responds to sound. The engineering task is to obtain a combination of properties in the same object: sufficient softness to follow a surface, sufficient strength to remain intact, and sufficiently low loss to preserve useful signals.

The bottlebrush description helps make the molecular design imaginable. Picture a main chain with many smaller chains projecting from it, like the bristles of a brush. This describes the arrangement of molecules inside the material. At the scale of the patch, the user encounters a continuous solid. The second network reinforces that solid through its molecular connections. Its contribution is structural; sound generation and image processing remain elsewhere in the device.

The team’s reported demonstrations then move through three different questions: how long transmission can remain stable in a controlled setup, how the material behaves against sweating skin, and whether imaging can continue during a real journey.

For the long-duration test, credit-card-sized samples connected a probe to a phantom, an artificial object made to mimic tissue. MIT reports clear continuous imaging for around nine days.

A phantom offers an important experimental advantage: researchers can study an interface using an artificial tissue model under laboratory conditions. This helps them examine sustained sound transmission separately from the many changes a living wearer introduces. The duration therefore provides evidence about sustained imaging through the material under the reported conditions of that setup.

The surface in this experiment matters as much as the clock. Nine days against synthetic tissue describes a material demonstration. Human wear brings a separate set of interactions, including skin response, changing contact pressure and repeated movement. The phantom result supports the possibility of a lasting sound path; translating that duration into a period of comfortable human use remains a further step.

The second demonstration brought the material onto a person. During a ten-minute treadmill run, a volunteer wore it on the chest. MIT reports that a conventional silicone-rubber comparison became waterlogged with sweat, while the new material wicked away the buildup.

Sweat makes the contact problem more dynamic. Moisture arrives at the very place where the device is trying to maintain a consistent connection. The surface can change while the person is moving, and a material’s behaviour against skin can differ from its behaviour against an artificial tissue model under the reported laboratory conditions. A short exercise comparison therefore addresses a practical condition that the long phantom test leaves to a different experiment.

The treadmill result concerns how the materials handled sweat during those ten minutes. Its relevance lies in the interface’s response to a changing surface. Diagnostic performance requires another kind of evidence: whether the images or measurements accurately answer a medical question. Keeping these purposes distinct allows the sweat test to carry its proper weight as an engineering observation.

The third demonstration connected contact performance to imaging during movement. A volunteer wore a key-fob-sized ultrasound patch on the neck, linked to a small portable tablet, during a thirty-minute bicycle ride from MIT in Cambridge to Bunker Hill in Charlestown and back, including steep inclines. MIT reports continuous, accurate blood-flow imaging during the ride.

Here, the experimental setting becomes closer to the activity a wearable system might eventually accompany. The wearer travels through an environment, while the patch and its connected equipment continue collecting information. The tablet is part of that described arrangement. The demonstration concerns a working combination of an interface, an ultrasound patch and supporting equipment.

Imaging blood flow also changes the practical question. A static picture offers a view at one moment. A continuing stream of measurements can potentially show how a process changes over time. In an imagined future exercise application, for example, a clinician or researcher might be interested in the relationship between activity and a person’s measured response. The bicycle demonstration establishes a reported ability to keep acquiring blood-flow images during that particular ride. The medical meaning of such a stream would depend on the purpose for which it was collected and the evidence supporting its interpretation.

Continuity and accuracy deserve separate attention here. Continuity concerns whether information keeps arriving. Accuracy concerns how closely that information represents what is happening in the body. A system may produce a visually steady stream while its measurements still require comparison with an appropriate reference. MIT describes the ride’s imaging as accurate; the basis and scope of that assessment would be important to understand when evaluating potential clinical use.

The source boundary is clear. MIT’s report is an institutional account of its own team’s work. It identifies a *Science* paper, *Interpenetrating Bottle-Brush Elastomer for Low-Loss Soft Ultrasonic Interfaces*, with DOI 10.1126/science.aef9553. Independent access to the full paper was unavailable for this article. A link labelled open access led to an unrelated 2004 thesis, which is excluded from the evidence. The account here therefore rests on MIT’s descriptions of the experiments, with the federal institute’s explainer supplying general ultrasound background.

Taken together, the three demonstrations offer a more useful picture than a single combined claim about long-lasting exercise monitoring. The phantom experiment contributes duration under controlled conditions. The treadmill comparison contributes an observation about sweat on a moving person. The bicycle ride contributes reported blood-flow imaging during travel. Each adds a different piece of evidence, and each has its own surface, timescale and purpose.

That progression also explains why an interface can deserve attention as a scientific advance. Medical technology often depends on several components working together, and a small component can determine whether the whole arrangement remains usable. A sound-producing element may operate correctly while poor contact degrades the information reaching it. Better contact could allow existing capabilities to function in a wider range of situations.

The potential benefit of longer observation is similarly specific. A brief examination captures a limited period in someone’s day. A wearable system could, in principle, extend observation into selected activities or longer intervals. Whether that additional information proves useful depends on the question being asked. More images can reveal a changing process, while also creating more material for a person or software system to interpret. A reliable interface would make collection more feasible; the value of the collected information would still need to be established for each intended use.

MIT identifies athlete monitoring and care outside clinics as hoped-for applications, and says work to streamline fabrication continues. Those aims connect the laboratory problem to two everyday settings: following a body during physical activity and gathering useful information closer to where someone lives. Both would place demands on the complete device, including how consistently it can be made and how well people tolerate wearing it.

Longer human use would therefore test a different kind of durability from nine days on a phantom. The material must remain functional as part of a relationship with skin. Repeated use across people would show how broadly the reported behaviour carries over. Clinical evaluation would ask whether the resulting measurements help answer a defined health question. These are successive questions about usefulness, arising from the engineering result itself.

For now, the strongest story is the one the experiments support: a fragile first material became a tougher dry interface, and that interface carried ultrasound through controlled testing and short human demonstrations. The small area beneath the probe has become a place where longer observation looks more plausible. Can that connection remain dependable through enough of ordinary life to make the images it enables useful beyond the examination room?
