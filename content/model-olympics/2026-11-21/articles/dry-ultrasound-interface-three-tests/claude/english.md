# A Dry Patch Kept an Ultrasound Image Going on a Bike Ride. Could It Last Beyond the Exam Room?

On November 19, 2026, researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the American engineering and research university known as MIT, reported a dry, stretchy material that carries ultrasound from a probe into the body. In an ordinary clinic, that job belongs to the cool, slippery gel a technician spreads on the skin. The work comes from the laboratory of Xuanhe Zhao, an MIT engineer whose group designs soft materials, together with the Singapore-MIT Alliance for Research and Technology through a project called Wearable Imaging for Transforming Elderly Care. Shu Wang, an MIT postdoctoral researcher, led the study. A postdoctoral researcher is a scientist who has finished a doctorate and is building an independent research career. Fellow postdoc Runze Li served as co-lead. Their invention is a contact layer, a thin sheet that sits between an ultrasound device and the skin. The larger hope behind it is that ultrasound, a tool most people meet for a few minutes on an exam table, might one day keep watching the body while a person walks, runs or rides through an ordinary day.

To see why a sheet of material matters so much, it helps to start with how ultrasound works. An ultrasound machine makes pictures with sound. A handheld part called a transducer sends out short pulses of sound pitched far above the highest note a human ear can hear. Those pulses travel into the body and bounce off boundaries between different tissues, such as the wall of a blood vessel or the edge of an organ. The transducer then listens for the returning echoes. An echo that comes back quickly began at a shallow boundary, and an echo that takes longer travelled deeper. A computer turns the pattern of echo timings and strengths into an image. The National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering, part of the US National Institutes of Health, describes diagnostic ultrasound as a sound-based method that avoids the ionising radiation used in X-rays. That quality is one reason ultrasound appears so often in pregnancy checks and heart scans.

Sound has one troublesome enemy on its way in: air. Even tiny pockets of air between a probe and the skin get in the way of the sound passing across that boundary, and the image weakens or breaks up. Gel solves this by filling every small gap and pushing the air out. With the gel in place, the probe, the gel and the skin form one continuous path for sound. Engineers call this coupling. The gel supplies coupling and the machine does everything else: the transducer makes and hears the sound, the computer builds the image, and a trained person interprets it. MIT's new material belongs entirely to the coupling step. It joins the probe to the skin, and the probe, the software and the clinician carry out the rest.

For a quick scan in a clinic, gel works well. A wearable ultrasound device faces a longer and busier version of the same task. Skin bends and stretches with each breath and step. Sweat collects. Over hours, a water-based gel can dry out or slide. The MIT team had met this limit in its own earlier wearable designs, which relied on hydrogels, soft materials made mostly of water. Those earlier hydrogels could dry out within a day or two. That experience set the challenge for the new work: build a contact layer that stays dry and solid, holds onto moving skin, and still lets sound pass through with little loss.

## A solid that lets sound slip through

That challenge contains a real engineering tension. Solids are sturdy, which suits something worn on the body. Solids also tend to soak up sound energy as it passes through them, which works against a clear image.

The materials here are polymers. A polymer is a substance built from very long molecules, each one a chain of many small repeating units, a little like a string of beads. Rubber and many plastics are polymers. In an ordinary solid polymer, the long chains tangle together like a heap of cooked spaghetti. When a sound wave passes through, it makes those chains jiggle, and the chains rub against their neighbours. That rubbing is friction, and friction drains away some of the sound's energy. Less energy reaches the tissue, and fainter echoes return. Adding a liquid or a lubricant can ease the rubbing, and that liquid is exactly what the team wanted to leave out.

Their answer was a molecular shape called a bottlebrush. A bottlebrush polymer has a central chain, called the backbone, with many shorter side chains sticking out along its length, much like the bristles on a brush used to clean a narrow bottle. The name describes the molecule's shape. According to MIT's account, this bristled design reduced the friction between chains, so sound could pass with less energy lost. The bristles gave the material a kind of built-in slipperiness at the molecular scale while it stayed a dry solid.

Then came a setback. When the team made larger pieces of this low-friction material, the samples were so crumbly that they collapsed under their own weight. A material that carries sound beautifully and falls apart in the hand has little use as a patch on a running chest.

The researchers strengthened it by chemically fusing a second bottlebrush network into the first. A network, in this sense, is a web of polymer chains linked to one another at various points. The second web was loosely connected, and it wove through the first. The title of the team's paper in the journal *Science* calls the result an "interpenetrating" bottlebrush elastomer, which means two molecular webs threaded through each other inside one stretchy solid. MIT reports that this combination made the material tougher and stretchier while keeping the low-friction behaviour that let sound pass easily. The second network is a dry molecular web, chemically locked into the first and made of the same bottlebrush-style polymer chains, so the whole contact layer remains one solid material.

An everyday comparison, offered purely as an illustration, can make the balance clearer. Imagine trying to hear someone tapping on the far side of a door while you hold a cushion against it with your ear pressed to the cushion. If the cushion is too stiff, it slips whenever the door shifts. If it is too soft and spongy, it swallows the vibration and the taps grow faint. The ideal cushion would cling to the door through every wobble and still pass each tap to your ear. The MIT team was searching for the molecular version of that cushion. The comparison stops at the trade-off; tapping on a door is a far simpler thing than sending ultrasound into living tissue.

## Three tests, three questions

MIT's report opens by bringing together long-lasting imaging and good performance during exercise. The underlying evidence comes from three separate tests, each with its own setting, its own length of time and its own question. Reading them one at a time shows what each one adds.

The first test asked whether the material could keep an image clear for a long time. The researchers cut credit-card-sized samples and used them to join an ultrasound probe to a tissue-mimicking phantom. A phantom is an artificial tissue model, a manufactured block designed to respond to sound in a way that resembles body tissue, so engineers can test imaging equipment on it. MIT reports that the setup produced clear, continuous imaging for around nine days. That is a striking figure for a contact layer, especially next to the team's earlier hydrogels that could dry out within a day or two. The nine days belong to this bench setup, with the material coupling a probe to an artificial tissue model. The result describes the material's durability as a sound bridge on that manufactured block, and the length of comfortable wear on a person remains an open question.

The second test added a human body, sweat and movement. A volunteer wore the material on the chest during a ten-minute run on a treadmill. For comparison, the team also tested silicone rubber, a common soft, rubbery material. MIT reports that the silicone rubber became waterlogged with sweat, while the new material wicked the buildup away. Wicking describes how some materials draw moisture along and away from a surface, the way a cotton towel pulls water from wet skin. Sweat matters here because a film of moisture trapped under a contact layer can disturb the steady, even contact that sound needs. MIT's account of this test centres on how the two materials responded to sweat during a short run. Its findings concern that comparison between the new material and silicone rubber, over that ten-minute window.

The third test brought imaging and movement together. The team placed a key-fob-sized ultrasound patch on a volunteer's neck, where large blood vessels run fairly close to the surface. The patch was connected to a small portable tablet. The volunteer then rode a bicycle for thirty minutes, travelling from MIT in Cambridge, Massachusetts, to Bunker Hill in the neighbouring Charlestown district of Boston and back, along a route that included steep inclines. MIT reports that the patch maintained continuous, accurate imaging of blood flow throughout the ride. Ultrasound can track moving blood because echoes from flowing blood shift slightly in a way the machine can measure, which lets it show how blood moves through a vessel. A bicycle ride up and down hills shakes the body and changes the angle of the neck, so a steady blood-flow image during that ride shows the contact layer holding up under real activity. The documented setup links the patch to a tablet, and MIT's account describes this as a volunteer demonstration on this route.

Placed side by side, the three tests build in demands. The phantom showed staying power. The treadmill run showed sweat handling during movement. The bicycle ride showed imaging during sustained activity outdoors. Each number stays attached to its own experiment: around nine days on a synthetic model, ten minutes on a running chest, thirty minutes on a riding neck. Adding them together or treating the nine-day figure as time spent on a person would blend three different experiments into a single imagined one.

This account draws on MIT's own report of its team's work and on the National Institutes of Health's general description of ultrasound. The *Science* paper, identified by its digital object identifier, holds the detailed methods and measurements; its full text and data tables sit outside the material reviewed for this article, so specific figures such as exact sound-loss values and participant or sample counts remain with the paper itself.

## Leaving the exam room

Why would anyone want ultrasound on the go? An ordinary scan is a snapshot. A patient lies down, a technician moves a probe across the skin for a few minutes, and the images capture that moment. Many things in the body change over hours and with activity, such as how blood flows during exercise, how the heart responds to effort, or how an older person's circulation behaves through a normal day. A contact layer that holds steady for long periods could help an ultrasound device collect images across those stretches of time.

The team has named some of the uses it hopes for. One is monitoring athletes, whose bodies are under the most strain during training and competition, far from any clinic. Another is care outside clinics, a theme that fits the elderly-care focus of the Singapore partnership behind the work. The researchers are currently working on simpler, more streamlined ways to make the material, a necessary step for anything meant to leave a laboratory in useful numbers. MIT's report presents these applications as directions the team envisions, and it describes the material's present status as laboratory research with fabrication work under way.

The material addresses one specific bottleneck: keeping good contact between a device and moving, sweating skin. A wearable ultrasound system has several other parts that must also work. The transducer needs power and a way to send its data somewhere useful; in the bicycle demonstration, that meant a tablet connected to the patch. Someone, or some carefully tested software, needs to interpret the images and decide what they mean for a particular person. Doctors and health systems look for evidence that continuous imaging leads to better decisions or better health. Improved coupling helps a device gather a cleaner signal. Understanding what a blood-flow image says about a patient, and whether watching it continuously improves care, are separate questions answered by separate kinds of research.

Several of those questions follow directly from the three tests as MIT describes them. The nine-day result on an artificial tissue model raises the matter of how the material behaves on human skin over days, including comfort and skin tolerance. MIT's account describes a volunteer in each exercise demonstration, which makes performance across many people, with different body shapes, skin types and activity levels, a natural next question for readers of this report. The team's fabrication work bears on whether the material can be produced consistently and cleaned or replaced practically. Clinical effectiveness, meaning a measurable benefit to patients, lies beyond the scope of the tests MIT's report describes. Within this account, these form the natural next chapter for a promising material.

What the work has shown so far is a genuine piece of materials ingenuity. The team faced a solid that needed to be strong and slippery at once, found a molecular shape that reduced internal friction, watched that first version crumble, and then strengthened it by weaving a second molecular web through the first. In MIT's reported tests, the result kept an image clear on an artificial tissue model for around nine days, wicked away sweat buildup during a ten-minute treadmill run in which a silicone-rubber comparison became waterlogged, and supported continuous blood-flow imaging on a volunteer's neck through a hilly half-hour bike ride.

All of that rests on a small patch of material where a device meets the skin. For decades that boundary has been handled with a squeeze of gel and a few minutes of stillness. The MIT team has offered a dry, durable alternative that stayed in place through sweat and motion in its reported demonstrations. The question now hanging over it is a physical one with large consequences: can that small patch of contact stay reliable across many bodies, many activities and many days, well enough for ultrasound to follow people out of the exam room and into their lives?
