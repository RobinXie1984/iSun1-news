# Lost in Transit: Is the Trip to the Lab Hiding the Deep Sea's Microbes?

On November 12, 2026, the University of Manchester published a report about a machine built to investigate a long-standing puzzle in ocean science. The deep seafloor is crowded with microbes, living things made of a single cell and far too small to see, and these organisms help drive the chemistry of the largest habitat on Earth. Scientists can detect their genetic fingerprints in seafloor mud. Researchers also broadly estimate that less than 1% of deep-sea microbes grow under conventional laboratory methods. The machine, called DISCUSS, comes from a team spread across several institutions, with Manchester researcher Zhengguang Liu among its members. The work appeared earlier, in a paper by J.-C. Feng, M. Zhu, G. Yang and colleagues published in the journal *Nature Sensors* on September 9, 2026. Their idea fits in one sentence: perhaps many deep-sea microbes stay out of reach because the trip from the seafloor to the laboratory changes the world they need.

That idea turns a familiar story around. The usual picture of deep-sea science centres on the descent: cables, cold, darkness and the enormous weight of water overhead. Reaching the seabed is genuinely hard. The DISCUSS team focuses on what happens after a sample is in hand. A scoop of seafloor mud rises through more than a kilometre of water, passes into containers, gets divided and sorted, and finally lands in growth chambers. Each of those steps is a moment when the conditions around the microbes can shift. If the most sensitive organisms fade somewhere along the way, the laboratory ends up studying the hardy survivors of the journey. The sensitive ones then look rare or absent in the scientific record.

The system's full name spells out the ambition: the deep-sea intelligent in situ sampling, cultivation and sorting system. "In situ" is Latin for "in its original place." The aim is to carry something of that original place along with the sample through every stage of the laboratory work.

The publication timeline is clear. The paper reached the journal on February 5, 2026, was accepted on July 20 and appeared online on September 9. Manchester's report followed about two months later as an explanation for a wider audience. The published materials leave the dates of the fieldwork and laboratory experiments unstated, so this story concerns a reported study whose experiments took place at some earlier point.

## A seafloor that leaks methane

According to the paper, the microbes came from a cold seep about 1,300 metres below the surface of the South China Sea. A cold seep is a patch of seafloor where fluids and gases, often including methane, ooze upward from buried sediment. The name distinguishes it from its better-known relative, the hydrothermal vent, where water heated by volcanic rock gushes out at high temperatures. At a seep, the fluid emerges close to the cold of the surrounding deep water.

Methane is the main ingredient of the natural gas that fuels many kitchen stoves, and it is also a potent heat-trapping gas once it reaches the atmosphere. At seeps, whole communities of microbes make a living from it. In sediment where oxygen is absent, some microbes consume methane while others use sulfate, a substance dissolved in ordinary seawater, and the chemistry of each group supports the other. Scientists call these linked processes anaerobic methane oxidation and sulfate reduction. "Anaerobic" means "happening in the absence of oxygen." Here, "oxidation" refers to methane being broken down for energy, and "sulfate reduction" refers to sulfate being chemically transformed as part of the same partnership.

That partnership is part of what makes seep communities so hard to study. An organism that thrives alongside its partners may struggle once separated from them. A laboratory routine that rewards fast, independent growers will fill its dishes with exactly those. A cold seep therefore sets a demanding test for any system claiming to keep the original world intact. Success means keeping a cooperative community going for a long time, and then drawing individual members out of it.

## Where the chain breaks

Three conditions change as a sample travels upward. The first is pressure, the push of all the water above. Scientists measure pressure in megapascals, written MPa. One megapascal is roughly ten times the air pressure people feel at sea level, so deep-sea organisms live under many times the pressure of a beach. The second is temperature: the sample rises from cold bottom water into warmer layers and then into a ship or laboratory. The third is chemistry. As pressure falls, gases dissolved in water escape, much as they do when any pressurised container is opened, and the chemical balance around the microbes shifts.

Specialised samplers can hold pressure during the ascent, and that protection matters. The DISCUSS team argues that it covers one link in a longer chain. After the ascent come transfers between containers, the slow work of encouraging growth, and the sorting of individual organisms. Each handoff is another chance for pressure, temperature or chemistry to drift.

An everyday comparison helps show why the whole chain matters; it is an illustration, separate from anything in the study. Picture a tub of ice cream shipped across a country. The truck has a freezer, so the tub stays solid for the long drive. Then it sits on a warm loading dock, gets carried through a heated warehouse and waits on a counter before reaching the shop's freezer. When a customer finally opens it, the ice cream has melted and refrozen into grainy slush. The truck did its job well. The damage happened at the handoffs. Food companies guard against this with a "cold chain," where every link keeps the temperature steady from factory to shelf.

The deep-sea version is far more demanding. Ice cream needs one thing, cold. Seafloor microbes respond to pressure, temperature and dissolved chemistry together, and to the presence of their partners. The comparison captures a single idea: protection has to stretch across every handoff, from seafloor to final dish.

## A machine that watches and adjusts

DISCUSS links three stages that are often handled separately. A pressure-retaining sampling module collects material from the seafloor and holds it under pressure. An enrichment stage then gives the community time to grow. In microbiology, "enrichment" means providing conditions that encourage the organisms of interest to multiply until they become easier to study. Finally, a robotic isolation stage works to separate individual kinds of microbe, with the goal of growing each as its own pure culture.

Sensors tie the stages together. They measure conditions inside the chambers, and a feedback controller adjusts the system when readings drift away from their targets. The principle matches the one a home thermostat uses: measure, compare, correct, repeat. The difference lies in what is being held steady, and in how many conditions are tracked at once.

For the sorting work, cameras photograph the growing microbes, and computer-vision software, programs trained to recognise shapes in images, identifies colonies. A colony is a visible cluster of cells growing on a surface. It may start from a single cell or from a small clump of cells, so one colony can sometimes contain more than one kind of microbe. Robotic picking therefore helps isolate candidate cultures, and confirming that a culture is pure, meaning it holds one kind of microbe, takes further checks. A robotic arm moves the selected material while the surrounding conditions stay under control.

The team also built what it calls a digital twin. This is a computer representation of the system, updated with live sensor measurements and used to guide adjustments: a running model of the chambers' state. It reconstructs a chosen set of conditions. The real seep holds countless additional details, from the texture of the sediment to the full cast of living neighbours, and the twin models the parts the engineers selected to monitor and control.

## What the researchers report recovering

The researchers report two main outcomes, and they describe different stages of the work. First, the system kept an enriched microbial community going for 180 days, roughly six months. Second, the team recovered 34 isolates. An isolate is a recovered culture or strain of microbe, a population descended from one kind of organism and grown on its own. The paper separately describes an 11-day robotic isolation phase. The 180 days belong to the community; the 34 isolates came out of the sorting work. Reading the figures as 34 organisms, each grown for six months, would blend the stages together.

How new are those 34 isolates? Here the evidence calls for care. Scientists often compare microbes using a gene called 16S rRNA, which bacteria and archaea, two major branches of single-celled life, both carry, and which changes slowly over evolutionary time. It works a little like a family surname: closely related microbes share very similar versions. Researchers compare a newcomer's sequence against "type strains," the official reference specimens that anchor named species. Of the 34 isolates, 11 showed low 16S rRNA similarity to known type strains. That result marks them as candidates for further investigation. Formally naming a new species requires additional evidence, so the accurate description today is 11 promising candidates.

The two strains the paper examines most closely, labelled HP_SR9 and HP_SR10, belong to species that scientists have already described. Their value lies in what they revealed about pressure. In the team's tests, HP_SR9 tolerated a range of pressures. HP_SR10 behaved very differently. It grew best at around 13 MPa within the tested range, and its growth was severely impaired at ordinary atmospheric pressure. The paper's discussion concludes that conventional cultivation at atmospheric pressure would miss this strain. The 13 MPa figure is a laboratory test setting, and the 1,300-metre figure describes where the sample came from; the paper presents the two numbers for separate purposes.

Together, the two strains illustrate the study's central argument in miniature. Two organisms can respond to the same change in strikingly different ways. In principle, a tolerant microbe like HP_SR9 could come through a rough journey and appear in a standard laboratory, while a pressure-dependent microbe like HP_SR10 would fall behind. Repeat that pattern across a whole community and the shape of what reaches the laboratory starts to depend on the journey itself. The tests cover two strains, and how often the pattern holds across the wider deep sea remains a question for further work.

The less-than-1% figure from the opening belongs to a different category of evidence. It is a broad background estimate describing how hard deep-sea cultivation has been across the field as a whole. The study's own results are the specific community and cultures described here, and the two kinds of figure stay separate, with any success rate for DISCUSS resting on the researchers' detailed definitions.

## What remains open

The strongest conclusion the evidence supports concerns access. Living cultures let scientists do things a genetic sequence can only hint at: watch an organism grow, measure what it consumes and produces, and test how it responds to changes in its surroundings. Sequences from mud suggest which microbes are present and what they might do. Cultures turn some of those suggestions into experiments. A maintained community adds a further layer, because it keeps partnerships intact that disappear once a single strain is isolated. The 180-day community and the 34 isolates are complementary results.

The researchers and Manchester's report point to possible future uses: understanding microbial functions, and investigating enzymes, materials or other biotechnology. These remain possibilities. The published sources establish living cultures in a research setting; any medicine, product or industrial advantage would require measured functions and much further development. The methane and sulfate findings have a similar status. They are scientific observations about how a seep community works. The study measured the community's chemistry, and any effect on ocean carbon or the climate lies outside what it measured; the machine itself is a research instrument.

The team proposes adapting the sensor-and-feedback design to other extreme environments, and identifies faster sorting, parallel cultivation and additional sensors as future work. The paper's authors describe their architecture in broad terms. The reported demonstration covers one cold-seep community in the South China Sea, and its evidence comes from the researchers' own paper and the account of a participating university. Broader performance claims would rest on repeat studies by other teams and on measurements of the time, cost and resources the system requires.

Those limits leave the most interesting question standing. For decades, the gap between the microbes scientists can detect and the microbes they can grow has looked like a fact about the organisms themselves: stubborn, slow, mysterious. DISCUSS suggests that part of the gap may be a fact about the journey. One pressure-dependent strain, recovered through a continuous chain of controlled conditions, offers a small, concrete piece of evidence for that idea. The larger question now becomes testable at other sites and with other communities: how many of the deep sea's apparently missing microbes were simply lost in transit, changed by a trip that rearranged their world before anyone had a chance to meet them?
