# Before Energy Warms a Home or Moves a Car, Much of It Slips Away. How Much Could the World Keep?

On November 12, 2026, seven researchers led by energy analyst Jan Rosenow published a study in the journal *npj Clean Energy* that begins with a puzzle. Every year, the world's energy system mines coal, pumps oil and gas, spins turbines at dams and reactors, and gathers wind and sunlight. All of that effort delivers a familiar list of results: warm rooms, moving vehicles, lit streets and working machines. The researchers estimated the amount of these services the world received in 2022 and imagined delivering exactly the same amount through a heavily electrified system powered mainly by wind and solar. Their model finds that this imagined system would need 48 percent less primary energy, a drop of about 288 exajoules per year. It would also lose 61 percent less energy along the way. The result is a thought experiment measured against a baseline built from historical 2022 energy data. It raises a natural question: how can the same work need so much less energy going in?

The authors are Rosenow, N. Eyre, J. Rissman, K. K. Mayfield, H. M. Goldstein, Z. Ammar and C. Bruce-Lockhart. Their paper addresses a habit common in energy statistics. Governments, analysts and journalists often describe a country's or the world's energy use with one large figure called total primary energy. When people ask how much wind and solar power the world would need to replace fossil fuels, they often compare new clean supply against that total. The authors argue that this comparison misleads, because a large share of today's primary energy leaves as waste heat before it does anything useful. They call the mistake the primary energy fallacy. The label is their argument, a proposal for better measurement that readers can judge on its evidence.

To build their picture of 2022, the team used energy balances from the International Energy Agency, an international organisation that compiles national energy statistics. They also used the world energy-flow framework from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, a US government research laboratory known for diagrams that trace energy from its sources through to its uses and losses. The energy balances record historical supply and use. The amount of useful service, such as the heat and motion that energy finally delivered, is an estimate: the team applied assumed average efficiencies for each sector to that recorded energy use. This estimated amount of service is the quantity the comparison holds fixed. The team then constructed a second, stylised world. Here, electricity does most of the work, wind and solar supply most of that electricity, and some hard-to-electrify fossil fuel uses remain. The scenario describes a configuration of technology and leaves the calendar aside. Questions about what might happen by 2030 or 2050, and how fast anyone could build such a system, belong to other kinds of study.

## Four ways to count the same energy

The puzzle makes sense once energy is counted at four different points.

*Primary energy* is energy counted where it enters the system: coal at the mine, crude oil at the well, the heat content of fuel entering a power station, or, for wind and solar, the electricity they generate. *Final energy* is what reaches users, such as electricity arriving at a building or petrol in a car's tank. *Useful energy* is the service itself: the heat that warms a room, the motion that carries a truck forward, the light from a lamp. *Rejected energy* is everything lost along the way, mostly as waste heat drifting into the air or water.

The energy unit also needs explaining. A joule is a small amount of energy, roughly what it takes to lift a small apple one metre. An exajoule is a billion billion joules, written as 10 to the power of 18. The 288 exajoules in the study describe a quantity of energy flowing through the modelled system over a year. That is a different kind of measurement from the size or capacity of power stations.

An everyday comparison can make the four counts concrete. The comparison is an illustration of the idea, offered here as explanation. Picture a parcel delivery. You could record the fuel bought at the depot, the fuel that actually reaches the van's engine, the movement that carries the parcel to the door, and the heat that escapes from the engine and brakes along the route. Suppose a different van delivers the same parcel to the same door using much less fuel and producing much less wasted heat. The smaller fuel bill reflects a more efficient trip. The job itself, a delivered parcel, stays the same size. The study applies this logic to the whole world's energy system: the job is held fixed at the estimated 2022 level, and the question is how much fuel and heat loss the trip requires. The analogy covers energy flows only. Useful energy, in the study's sense, measures physical services such as heat and motion, and it is a narrower idea than everything people value about comfort or convenience.

## Following the energy through the system

Much of today's waste begins at power stations that burn fuel. A coal or gas plant burns fuel to make heat, uses that heat to drive turbines, and turns the turbines' motion into electricity. Physics sets limits on that conversion, so a large portion of the fuel's heat leaves through cooling towers and exhaust stacks. In energy statistics, the primary energy of a thermal power station is the full heat content of the fuel it burns, including the share it discards.

Wind turbines and solar panels are counted differently. Under the common convention, their primary energy equals the electricity they produce. Swapping a coal plant for a wind farm therefore shrinks the primary-energy total for two reasons. One is physical: the combustion step, with its large heat losses, disappears from that part of the system. The other comes from the accounting rule, which defines the starting point for wind and solar at the electricity stage.

This is why the authors lead with rejected energy, the 61 percent figure. Rejected energy measures losses directly, so it stays the same whichever convention someone uses to count wind and solar inputs. It still depends on the model's other assumptions, such as how efficient various machines are. The 48 percent drop in primary energy and the 61 percent drop in rejected energy are related findings with different reference points. The first compares the amount of energy entering the system; the second compares the amount lost. Both percentages describe modelled energy flows; changes in household bills and carbon emissions require separate estimates. Part of the primary-energy difference comes from how the accounting is set up. Part comes from losses the model physically avoids. A fair reading keeps both parts in view.

The changes in the model reach well beyond power stations. They also happen where energy is finally used.

Take transport. A petrol or diesel engine is itself a small heat engine, and much of its fuel energy warms the engine block, radiator and exhaust. An electric drivetrain, under the study's assumptions, turns a larger share of the energy it receives into motion. When the model replaces combustion vehicles with electric ones, the same amount of useful motion requires less energy and produces less waste heat.

Heating involves a more surprising mechanism. A heat pump works on the same principle as a refrigerator. A refrigerator moves heat out of its insulated box and releases it into the kitchen. A heat pump moves heat from the outdoor air, the ground or water into a building, or into an industrial process. It uses electricity to run the pump, and the heat it delivers comes largely from the surroundings. So a heat pump can deliver several units of heat for each unit of electricity it consumes. This fits the law of conservation of energy, because the environment supplies the extra input.

The study makes that extra input visible. For a single heat pump, the bookkeeping is simple: the electricity it uses plus the ambient heat it draws from its surroundings equals the heat it delivers. At the scale of the whole world, the paper's first table balances inputs against three outputs, one of them nonenergy use, meaning fuels used as raw materials, such as oil turned into plastics. In rounded figures for the 2022 baseline, 606 exajoules per year of primary energy plus 2 of ambient heat equal 181 of useful energy, 388 of rejected energy and 39 of nonenergy use. In the electrified scenario, 318 of primary energy plus 52 of ambient heat equal the same 181 of useful energy, 150 of rejected energy and the same 39 of nonenergy use. The paper keeps nonenergy use as its own category, separate from useful and rejected energy. Ambient heat captured by heat pumps thus rises from roughly 2 to roughly 52 exajoules per year, an increase the authors describe as about twentyfold. These figures are approximate. They rest on assumed heat-pump performance for each type of application. Real performance in a particular house depends on its climate, insulation, equipment and installation, and its running costs depend on local energy prices.

A third change happens upstream, before fuel reaches anyone. Turning crude oil into petrol takes energy at refineries. Processing natural gas, moving coal, and pumping fuels through pipelines and onto ships all take energy too. When the model uses far less fossil fuel, much of this supporting activity shrinks with it.

The paper's tables show where the modelled losses fall. Losses in electricity generation, together with losses in transmission lines, local distribution networks and storage, drop from 135 exajoules per year to 24, a reduction of 111. Losses in transport fall from roughly 94 to 28 exajoules per year. The paper reports this reduction as 67 exajoules, calculated from unrounded values; the rounded endpoints give 66 by simple subtraction. Losses from transforming fossil fuels, such as refining, fall from 46 to 12 exajoules per year, a reduction of 34. All of these are model outputs for an imagined system. The categories have their own boundaries, and the paper calculates its 61 percent total from a full balance of the whole system, so adding these three numbers together would give a misleading figure.

The overall finding therefore combines several changes. Wind and solar generation, electric motors, heat pumps and a smaller fossil fuel supply chain each contribute. The 61 percent figure belongs to that whole package of changes. It also describes the world in aggregate, and individual machines will perform above or below the averages the model assumes.

## Choices built into the imagined world

Every model requires decisions, and this one makes several that shape its numbers. The scenario keeps today's hydroelectric and geothermal generation. It then divides the remaining electricity equally between wind turbines and solar panels. In this stylised setup, wind and solar also take over the electricity currently produced by nuclear power and by bioenergy, meaning power from burning plant material and other organic matter.

These are modelling decisions. The study offers them as one simple, illustrative configuration for exploring energy flows, and it leaves aside questions of which mix would be cheapest, safest or most practical. The authors tested how much one of these choices matters. Nuclear plants, like coal and gas plants, produce electricity from heat and release a large share of that heat as waste. According to the paper, keeping current nuclear generation in the scenario would add about 20 exajoules per year of waste heat and would change the overall reduction in waste heat by less than 10 percent. That estimate is a sensitivity test inside the same model, and it sits apart from any engineering assessment of future nuclear plants.

## Where the model stops

The study adds up energy over a whole year. That approach captures how much energy flows through the system and how much is lost. Power grids, however, operate minute by minute. Demand for heat peaks in winter evenings. Solar output follows the sun. Wind rises and falls with the weather. A grid that runs mostly on wind and solar needs transmission lines, storage, flexible demand and backup arrangements that keep supply and demand matched at every hour and through every season.

The model includes losses from transmission, distribution and storage as yearly totals. Its storage-loss assumption draws on an analysis of a renewable-powered electricity system in the United Kingdom. It leaves the hour-by-hour operation of a global grid outside its calculations. The authors themselves list the challenges that remain: building networks, storing energy across seasons, managing flexibility and weather variability, finding materials and land, securing permits, raising capital, building supply chains and winning public acceptance. The study also leaves the cost and the speed of a transition outside its scope.

Demand is the other major boundary. The model freezes useful energy services at their estimated 2022 level, which makes the comparison clean. Populations and prosperity keep growing. When something becomes cheaper or more efficient, people sometimes use more of it, an effect economists call rebound. The authors discuss rebound and say they expect people in lower-income communities to use more useful energy in the future as their living standards rise. These points appear in the paper's discussion as expectations; the central calculation leaves them out. The figure of 288 exajoules therefore applies to the estimated 2022 level of service. The energy needs of a larger, wealthier future world remain an open question.

The scenario also keeps some fossil fuel uses that are hard to replace with electricity. A complete path to net-zero emissions would need additional solutions for those uses, and those solutions could change the energy flows the model reports. The findings come from one peer-reviewed study. This account rests on its published text and rounded tables as inspected on November 13, 2026, the day after publication.

## What the measurement reveals

Within these limits, the study makes a clear point. A system's energy input and the services it delivers are separate measurements. In today's system, a large share of input energy turns into waste heat at power stations, in vehicle engines and at refineries. The model shows that substituting electricity, wind and solar, electric drivetrains and heat pumps for combustion would sharply reduce those losses while delivering the same estimated heat, motion and light. In the authors' telling, comparing future clean supply with today's primary-energy total exaggerates the new energy the world would need; the more informative comparison looks at services delivered.

That changes the starting point for planning. Measured in useful services, the gap between today's supply and the needs of an electrified system looks substantially smaller than raw primary-energy figures suggest. A smaller energy requirement still has to be built, financed, connected, stored and kept reliable through cold, windless weeks, and it has to grow as the world's demand for comfort, mobility and industry grows.

So the puzzle that opened the paper leads to a larger one. The study shows how much energy could stay in the system under one set of technologies. What combination of grids, storage, complementary technologies and investment could turn those physical savings into a system that reliably serves a growing world, hour by hour and year after year?
