# The Gallium Is in the Rock. Who Can Get It to the Factory?

Every electrical grid, communications network and defense radar begins with materials drawn from the earth. Some arrive in enormous quantities: steel for structures, aluminum for conductors, copper for wiring. Others enter a device in tiny amounts and perform a highly specialized job. Gallium belongs to this second group. Its journey helps explain why securing mineral supplies involves much more than finding a promising deposit.

In a November 3, 2026, MIT News interview, materials researcher Elsa Olivetti described the connections that make those journeys possible: extraction, separation, refining and manufacturing. Olivetti co-directs MIT’s Minerals Stewardship Consortium, which brings researchers and mining companies together to study mineral supply. Her interview sets out research priorities and an explanation of industrial dependence. Its subject is the work required to build reliable supply, with measured gains from those approaches still an open question.

Gallium makes that question unusually concrete. It occurs in bauxite, the ore processed into alumina, an intermediate material used to make aluminum. The U.S. Geological Survey estimates that the world’s bauxite resources contain more than a million tons of gallium. Yet its February 2026 summary attributes an estimated 99% of worldwide primary low-purity gallium production in 2025 to China.

The mineral is widely present in rock. The industrial ability to recover it is concentrated at a particular stage. Between those two facts lies a practical question for governments and manufacturers spending money on new mines and factories: which part of the journey has become more dependable?

Gallium’s role helps explain the stakes. Manufacturers combine it with other elements to make materials such as gallium arsenide and gallium nitride. These compounds are semiconductors, materials whose electrical behavior can be controlled to perform useful tasks inside electronic devices. Their applications include communications equipment, defense systems and devices that produce or detect light. Gallium reaches these products through carefully prepared compounds and components, each with its own manufacturing requirements.

Its supply also follows the economics of another metal. According to USGS, primary gallium recovery occurs predominantly during bauxite processing, with another route through zinc processing. “Primary” here means recovery from an ore-processing stream. “Low-purity” identifies an early form that can undergo further cleaning before more demanding uses. Gallium production therefore depends partly on operations built around the much larger aluminum market.

A kitchen provides an everyday illustration. Ingredients pass through purchasing, sorting, preparation and cooking before a meal reaches the table. A new oven improves one part of that sequence; its usefulness still depends on suitable ingredients and the people who prepare them. Mineral processing has a similar sequence of handoffs, alongside stringent chemical standards, substantial equipment costs, and environmental and community responsibilities.

For gallium, the first difficult handoff begins with concentration. USGS gives bauxite an average gallium content of 50 parts per million. In everyday units, that is roughly 50 grams of gallium distributed through one metric tonne—1,000 kilograms—of bauxite. This conversion illustrates the average amount present. The quantity a particular operation can recover depends on the ore’s chemistry, its processing equipment and the economics of collecting that small fraction.

A geological resource estimate answers a question about presence. An economic reserve concerns material that can be recovered under specified conditions. Annual production measures material actually produced during a period. The estimate of more than a million tons of gallium in bauxite resources belongs to the first category. Turning any portion into saleable supply requires a workable route through the other categories, including a plant capable of recovering it at an acceptable cost.

This dependence on a host industry complicates expansion. An aluminum-processing operation makes decisions around its main product, its equipment and its operating conditions. Recovering gallium adds a separate purpose to that stream. Olivetti’s analysis points toward coordination between those activities: the smaller market needs access to a process whose scale and investment decisions are shaped by the larger one. A rich-looking resource total leaves that coordination problem intact.

The USGS production figures show the resulting concentration. Its estimated 2025 output table identifies China, Japan and Russia as primary low-purity gallium producers, and its accompanying account assigns China a 99% global share. That percentage describes this specific production stage. Finished semiconductor compounds, electronic components and complete radar systems belong to later stages, with different producers and trade relationships.

Precision matters even within the production table. China’s estimated output and the rounded world total both appear as 900,000 kilograms, while Japan and Russia also have positive estimated output. The rounding supports use of USGS’s stated 99% share; it offers limited scope for calculating a more exact percentage. The figures describe 2025 through a report published in February 2026, giving the November discussion a dated industrial baseline.

Capacity adds another layer. USGS lists estimated world primary low-purity gallium production capacity of 1.7 million kilograms in 2025, compared with estimated output of 900,000 kilograms. Capacity describes potential production under industrial conditions. Output records what was produced. The gap leaves several questions open, including how much additional production could operate economically and how much could reach a particular buyer. A capacity figure alone supplies only part of that answer.

The United States illustrates how genuine domestic activity can coexist with dependence on imported material. According to USGS, domestic primary low-purity gallium recovery has been absent since 1987. At the same time, a company in New York recovered and refined high-purity gallium using imported low-purity metal and fresh manufacturing scrap.

Both statements describe useful parts of the same chain. The New York operation performs refining and recovery work inside the country. Its imported feedstock links that work to earlier production overseas. The report leaves the operation’s output quantity unspecified, so its contribution can be described by activity while its scale remains unresolved.

The scrap distinction also matters. New scrap is waste generated during manufacturing, including the production of gallium arsenide devices. Old scrap comes from products that have already been used. USGS describes substantial reprocessing of new manufacturing scrap at the New York facility and records old-scrap recycling as absent in its gallium chapter. These are separate material streams, with different collection and processing challenges.

The trade statistics reveal another distinction that can easily disappear in public discussion. USGS reports U.S. net import reliance of 100% in 2025 relative to reported gallium consumption. Its measure uses imports minus exports and excludes gallium arsenide wafers, the thin pieces of semiconductor material used in device manufacturing. The figure describes the import dependence of that defined consumption measure.

A separate set of figures describes where imported metal arrived from directly. Over 2021–24, Canada accounted for 28% of reported U.S. gallium metal imports, Japan 22%, China 18%, Germany 16% and other sources 16%. These shares concern an earlier period and a different question. A shipment’s direct trade origin identifies a point on its route; tracing the location of initial gallium recovery requires additional evidence.

That is how a diversified list of trading partners can coexist with concentrated primary production. The U.S. import-source chart describes trade into one country. China’s estimated 99% share describes worldwide production at the primary low-purity stage in 2025. Shipment-level histories would be needed to connect individual imports to their upstream recovery sites. The available figures establish dependence and concentration at distinct boundaries.

Annual import data offer further context. USGS estimates U.S. gallium metal imports at 25,000 kilograms in 2025, up from 11,000 kilograms in 2024. Average customs value rose from $439 to $580 per kilogram. These figures show changes in recorded annual imports and their average value. Their dates and measurement method limit their use: a manufacturer seeking material in November 2026 would still need a current offer, a specified purity and a delivery commitment.

Trade policy creates a further source of uncertainty. The February 2026 USGS account traces Chinese export controls introduced in August 2023, a ban on gallium exports to the United States in December 2024, and a reported one-year lifting of that ban in November 2025. As of this article’s November 4, 2026 evidence cutoff, the operative rule remains unresolved in the supplied sources. The earlier chronology places the issue close to an important anniversary; a current official notice would establish its legal status.

Government funding marks another point along the route from interest to supply. USGS records up to $6 million announced by the U.S. Department of Energy for domestic gallium recovery research. It also describes a $29.9 million U.S. defense award to develop a demonstration facility intended to recover gallium and scandium from industrial waste. Those are research and development commitments. The evidence here establishes their announced purpose, while production results remain an open question.

A demonstration facility serves a different purpose from a commercial supplier. It can help establish whether a process works beyond the laboratory and how it behaves with real material. Commercial supply adds the requirement to deliver the required quality repeatedly, at useful quantities and workable cost. Funding, a pilot, installed capacity and actual shipments each provide evidence about a different point in that progression.

Olivetti’s broader argument extends beyond gallium. She identifies deeper or lower-grade deposits, environmentally sensitive locations, water availability, extreme weather, community concerns and changing costs as pressures on mineral production. Each can constrain a different part of the chain. A new mine can increase the amount of material entering a system while a separation plant, water supply or manufacturing process limits what eventually reaches customers.

The research she describes addresses several of those pressures. Engineered microbes and proteins may selectively attach to particular metals. Electrochemical methods use electrical processes to help extract or separate materials. Remote sensing can help observe changes in land use, while tighter temperature control can improve the management of sensitive separation processes. Community knowledge can inform planning around the places where this work occurs.

Some separation challenges arise especially clearly with rare-earth elements, a group distinct from gallium. Their chemical similarities make individual elements difficult to separate. Selective-binding research seeks ways to pick out a desired metal from a mixture. Commercial scale and performance under demanding operating conditions remain tests for such approaches. The interview presents these efforts as research directions, with their eventual contribution to reliable supply still to be demonstrated.

The institutional setting helps readers understand that agenda. MIT says the Minerals Stewardship Consortium launched in September 2025 with BHP, Vale and Rio Tinto as its three inaugural industry members. Olivetti co-leads it with economist Christopher Knittel. MIT’s December 16, 2025 account described nine faculty across four schools and its computing college, working on mining decisions, social and ecological questions, environmental monitoring, markets and policy. The arrangement brings industrial participants into a research program whose outcomes require their own evidence.

Olivetti also points to ventures pursuing different sources: Lithios works on lithium from brines, RockZero on lithium from hard rock, and SiTration, NthCycle and FoundIndustries on metals from mining or industrial waste. Their variety illustrates how supply can begin with several kinds of feedstock. The interview discusses movement toward pilot testing, including support through the Department of Energy’s Genesis Mission. Independently verified output, cost and environmental-performance measures remain unresolved in the evidence presented here.

Across these examples, the central issue is the reliability of the handoffs. As an illustration, a domestic magnet factory may depend on imported separated rare-earth oxides. Its opening establishes manufacturing capability at one stage, while its material requirements connect it to others. Gallium’s New York refining activity gives a documented example of that broader relationship between domestic capability and imported inputs.

A mine opening, a recovery process and a factory can each represent meaningful progress. Their significance becomes clearer when the announcement identifies the material entering, the quality leaving and the quantity delivered over a stated period. Gallium shows why those details deserve attention: a large geological resource, concentrated primary recovery, overseas refining and international trade can all describe the same material at successive moments.

The next mineral announcement will arrive with a location, an investment and a promise. The question earned by gallium’s journey is more precise: which stage can now supply the required material, at what quality and dated scale—and which dependency still determines whether it reaches the factory?
