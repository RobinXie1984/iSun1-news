Three headline options

Suspenseful: NASA Just Mapped a Path Back to Starliner. The Next Crew Flight Is Not Until 2028.

Emotional Resonance: After 61 Recommendations, NASA Is Asking Starliner to Prove Itself Empty Before It Carries Anyone Home.

Counter-intuitive: The Plan to Fly Astronauts on Starliner Again Starts With Leaving Them Off the Next Flight.

The empty seat is the whole point.

On September 28, 2026, NASA and Boeing published an update that sounds, at first, like a timetable. Uncrewed Starliner-1 could fly in December 2026 or January 2027. The current plan is to return astronauts on Starliner-2 by 2028. Options for a fifth and sixth station flight are intended. Vulcan is next after Atlas V’s last ride.

Read it again. The empty seat is the strategy.

This is not a victory lap, and it is not a funeral. It is a certification story with a date on it — and the date is a plan, not a boarding pass.

Fact check: September 30, 2026, Hong Kong time, against NASA release 26-079 and FY2027 budget background. What follows treats 2028 as an announced plan, not a certified flight.

Fiction, labeled as fiction. Imagine a hangar at night. A spacecraft that has already flown people sits under work lights. No countdown. No crew. Technicians move around a service module that once ran hotter and harder than its paperwork allowed. Someone checks a valve that is not the valve that flew before. The room is quiet in the way rooms get quiet when the next test is allowed to fail in public.

That scene is invented. The engineering problem is not.

What NASA actually said

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman put the agency’s reason in plain language: NASA has been committed to multiple crew transportation options since the start of the Commercial Crew Program, has worked with Boeing on issues identified on previous Starliner flights, and intends to see the vehicle return to flight in support of the International Space Station and future commercial destinations.

Then the sequence:

“We are starting with an uncrewed Starliner-1 mission to the International Space Station to validate the improvements made to the spacecraft and gather the flight data we need,” Isaacman said. “From there, we will use what we learn, continue implementing the corrective actions identified by our Program Investigation Team, and complete the testing and certification required for crewed flight. Our current plan is to return astronauts on Starliner-2 by 2028.”

Dana Weigel, manager of NASA’s Low Earth Orbit Program, framed the next flight as a safety gate, not a public-relations reset. Starliner’s next flight, she said, is a critical step toward full system certification and a sustained human presence in low Earth orbit. With crew and public safety as the highest priority, NASA will test the propulsion system through targeted demonstration objectives and disciplined operational controls. Those steps, she said, are essential to validating Starliner’s thermal performance — a key element for certification.

Three words do the most work in that paragraph: current plan. Not “scheduled.” Not “certified.” Not “guaranteed.”

The number that will get misquoted

NASA says its investigation into the 2024 crew flight identified 61 recommendations.

That sentence will travel farther than the rest of the release. It should travel with a warning label.

Sixty-one recommendations are not 61 proven unresolved defects. A recommendation can be a redesign, a test, a process change, a qualification limit, a training note, or a demand for more data. Some items may already be closed. Some may still be open. NASA did not publish, in this update, a public scoreboard of “fixed / not fixed.” Treating the number as a defect count is a category error.

What NASA did specify is narrower, and more useful.

Service-module reaction control thrusters operated outside their engineering qualification. That condition contributed to the loss of control experienced during the Crew Flight Test. Thruster performance problems came from a combination of factors, including the thermal environment and features inherent in the design.

Boeing made thermal modifications to the service module. NASA says it will evaluate those changes for improved performance on Starliner-1.

An additional thruster valve redesign remains part of certification work for future crewed flights. NASA ties that redesign to poppet seal extrusion and its adverse effects on thruster performance.

Other certification-supporting work named in the same update includes installation of new crew-module thrusters and batteries, plus minor parachute-system modifications for performance and reliability.

The through-line is not mystery. It is heat, qualification, and a propulsion system that has to be trusted twice: once to get to the station, and again to bring people home.

Why the next flight has no astronauts

Starliner-1 is described as an engineering evaluation mission. Potential window: December 2026 or January 2027. Destination: the International Space Station. Purpose: verify improved thermal environments, obtain performance data for system qualification, and identify residual risk before crewed missions.

That is a different job from “fly the next crew rotation.”

If you want a memorable line, use this one:

An empty capsule is not a demotion. It is a measuring instrument.

Crewed flight hides problems behind courage. Uncrewed flight hides nothing behind a press kit. The vehicle either stays inside its new thermal story or it does not. The thrusters either behave inside the box the engineers drew or they do not. Residual risk either shrinks in flight data or it stays on the list.

NASA is not asking the public to believe a briefing. It is asking the spacecraft to generate a dataset.

Weigel’s language about “targeted demonstration objectives and disciplined operational controls” is the adult version of that idea. Demonstration objectives mean the flight has specific tests it must run, not just a destination it must reach. Operational controls mean the team is not treating the mission as a casual cargo run with extra cameras.

2028 is a horizon, not a handshake

After Starliner-1, the plan is to use what is learned, keep implementing corrective actions from the investigation team, and finish the testing and certification required for crewed flight. The current plan is to return astronauts on Starliner-2 by 2028.

Hold the sentence at both ends.

“Return astronauts on Starliner-2” means NASA still intends Starliner to be a crew vehicle, not a museum piece and not a cargo-only afterthought.

“By 2028” and “current plan” mean the calendar can move. Certification is not a press conference. It is a stack of tests, reviews, and remaining design changes — including that valve work — that have to close before people buckle in.

A useful way to read the gap between late 2026 / early 2027 and 2028 is not “delay for delay’s sake.” It is the time NASA says it needs to fly the fix, study the flight, finish remaining changes, and complete certification. Whether that interval proves long enough is a future fact, not a present one.

Do not confuse intention with inventory. NASA also said it intends to exercise options for fifth and sixth Starliner flights to and from the station. Those are options the agency says it intends to exercise. They are not evidence that those missions have already flown. They are not proof the vehicle is already certified for a six-flight cadence. They are a statement about how many rides NASA wants in the portfolio if certification holds.

The rocket problem hiding under the spacecraft problem

Starliner does not climb to orbit by wishing. It rides a booster.

NASA said it will work with Boeing and United Launch Alliance to certify the Vulcan rocket for use after Atlas V’s final flight.

That sentence is easy to skip and expensive to skip. A crew vehicle and its launcher are a system. If Atlas V is reaching the end of its run, Starliner’s future is not only a thruster-and-thermal story. It is also a human-rating story for the rocket that replaces Atlas V.

NASA’s stated reason for the broader posture is simple: maintain multiple crew-transport options.

That is policy, not poetry. One provider can have a great year and still leave an agency with a single point of failure. Two certified paths, if both actually work, give NASA a way to keep station crews rotating when one vehicle, one factory, or one rocket family is down.

The FY2027 budget material, used here only as background, describes the present tense more bluntly than the September 28 update does. U.S. crew transportation currently uses SpaceX Crew Dragon alongside Russian Soyuz, while Starliner works toward certification.

That is the map as of the budget language: two operating crew paths, one American and one Russian, and a second American vehicle still on the certification road.

Do not turn that sentence into a claim that another provider has no competition, or that Starliner has already joined the operating roster. It has not, on NASA’s own wording.

What this update is not

It is not a cost sheet. NASA’s September 28 development-plan release, as used here, does not require a dollar figure to be understood, and none is invented.

It is not a blame memo. The release describes investigation findings, thermal modifications, a remaining valve redesign, and a certification path. It does not hand the reader a courtroom.

It is not a window into astronauts’ private feelings. Those are not in the record provided, and they are not invented here.

It is not a claim that 2028 is locked. Plans slip. Plans also hold. The honest status is the one NASA used: current plan.

It is not proof that 61 recommendations remain open. It is proof NASA counted 61 things it wanted done or examined after the 2024 crew flight.

The shape of the bet

Commercial Crew was sold, from the beginning, as a two-track American system. One track has been carrying people. The other track flew a crew test in 2024, ran into propulsion trouble tied to thrusters operating outside qualification, and is now being asked to come back the long way: empty, instrumented, then — if the data and the remaining design work support it — crewed again.

Isaacman’s larger frame was demand. “We are living through the most exciting era of space exploration since Apollo,” he said. As the domain opens, he argued, there will be growing demand for launch vehicles, transfer stages, landers, and spacecraft that carry astronauts.

You can accept that forecast or argue with it. Either way, the Starliner update is a narrower claim: NASA still wants more than one U.S. crew taxi in the set, and it is willing to put an uncrewed flight and a multi-year certification grind between that desire and the next astronaut boarding.

The takeaway that should survive the headline cycle:

Redundancy is not a slogan. It is a vehicle that can be certified, a rocket that can be human-rated, and a propulsion system that stays inside its qualification when the thermal environment gets ugly.

Until those three things are demonstrated, 2028 is a plan written in pencil.

The plot from here

Watch three checkpoints, not one date.

First, whether Starliner-1 actually flies in the December 2026–January 2027 window NASA called possible. “Potential to fly” is not “will fly.”

Second, what the flight data say about thermal performance and thruster behavior after Boeing’s service-module thermal modifications. That is the evaluation NASA named.

Third, whether the remaining certification work — including the additional valve redesign and the rest of the investigation team’s corrective actions — closes in time for a crewed Starliner-2 by 2028, on a launcher path that has to outlive Atlas V.

If those checkpoints go well, the empty-seat mission becomes the dullest important flight of the decade: a cargo-and-data run that makes later crew flights look routine. If they do not, the plan moves, because that is what “current plan” is built to do.

Spaceflight rewards impatience in speeches and patience in hardware. This update chose hardware.

Question for you

If you were setting NASA’s rule, would you keep two American crew vehicles in the portfolio even when one is years from certification — or would you put every dollar and every launch slot into the system that is already flying people, and accept a single U.S. path until the second one is truly ready?
