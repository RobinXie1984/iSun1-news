3. Counter-intuitive:
"NASA Just Paid Boeing $359 Million More for the Spacecraft That Almost Killed Its Astronauts."

The Second Chance

In June 2024, two astronauts climbed into a spacecraft called Starliner, waved goodbye to the cameras, and rocketed toward the International Space Station for what was supposed to be an eight-to-fourteen-day mission.

They came home nine months later — on a different spacecraft.

Butch Wilmore and Suni Williams didn't do anything wrong. The vehicle did. Five of Starliner's reaction control thrusters failed during approach. Helium leaked from seven of eight service module manifolds. The crew briefly lost six-degree-of-freedom control before docking. NASA later classified the mission as a Type A mishap — the same severity category as the Challenger and Columbia shuttle disasters-

That was 2024. This is 2026.

On September 28, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stood at Kennedy Space Center and announced that NASA is not walking away from Starliner. It's doubling down — with a $359 million investment, two additional station flights, a new rocket to certify, and a 2028 target to put astronauts back on board-

What Actually Went Wrong

The February 2026 investigation report — over 300 pages, conducted by NASA employees and outside experts — didn't just blame Boeing. It blamed NASA too.

The core technical finding was almost clinical in its understatement: Starliner's service module reaction control thrusters operated outside their engineering qualification-
21
. Translation: the hardware was pushed into conditions it was never certified to handle. Ground testing and analysis traced the failures to a combination of the thermal environment around the thruster housings — called "doghouses" — and features inherent in the thruster valve design itself-
26
.

But the report went further. It flagged what Isaacman called "the most troubling failure" — not hardware, but decision-making. Program advocacy, he wrote, "exceeded reasonable bounds and placed the mission, the crew, and America's space program at risk"-
14
.

Investigators issued 61 formal recommendations spanning technical, organizational, and cultural changes-
. These are not 61 proven unresolved defects. They are a roadmap — sometimes blunt, sometimes bureaucratic — for how to fix a program that lost its way.

Dana Weigel, who manages NASA's Low Earth Orbit Program, put it plainly: "With the safety of our space station crew and the public as our highest priority, we will test Starliner's propulsion system through targeted demonstration objectives and disciplined operational controls"-
21
.

The Road Back: Uncrewed First, Crewed Later

NASA's plan is sequential, cautious, and deliberately unglamorous:

Starliner-1 — an uncrewed mission to the ISS, targeting December 2026 or January 2027. It's not a test flight in the traditional sense. NASA calls it an "engineering evaluation mission" — designed to verify improved thermal environments on the service module, gather qualification data, and identify residual risk before anyone climbs aboard-
21
.

Starliner-2 — the first crewed return, no earlier than 2028. NASA astronaut Woody Hoburg has been assigned to command it. Hoburg, a veteran of the Crew-6 mission in 2023, said he is "deeply humbled to follow in the footsteps" of Wilmore and Williams-
.

Fifth and sixth station flights — NASA intends to exercise contract options for additional Starliner missions, though these are options, not flown flights-
21
.

Vulcan certification — Starliner currently launches on Atlas V, which is nearly out of production. NASA will work with Boeing and United Launch Alliance to certify Vulcan for crew transportation after Atlas V's final flight-
26
.

Isaacman framed the decision in terms of redundancy: "NASA has been committed to having multiple crew transportation options since the beginning of the Commercial Crew Program"-
21
.

That redundancy matters more than ever. SpaceX has signaled it intends to retire Crew Dragon by 2030, if not sooner-
. NASA has awarded SpaceX a $946 million contract extension to keep Dragon flying through the end of the decade, but the writing is on the wall-
. And according to NASA's FY2027 budget materials, the agency's current operational crew transportation capabilities rest on just two vehicles: Crew Dragon and Russia's Soyuz-

.

That's not redundancy. That's a single point of failure with a backup that requires a geopolitical relationship most of Washington would rather not depend on.

The Money Question

$359 million. That's what NASA is adding to its existing contract with Boeing to fix Starliner's design flaws and certify Vulcan-
.

It's a lot of money for a spacecraft that has never successfully completed a crewed round trip. But here's the counter-intuitive math: according to a 2026 audit, Starliner — despite its problems — is cheaper for NASA per seat than switching entirely to SpaceX-
4
.

Boeing's original Commercial Crew contract was $4.2 billion for six operational missions. That contract has since been dialed back to three crewed flights-
4
. The question isn't whether Starliner is expensive. It's whether the alternative — a single-provider future — is riskier.

Isaacman's answer, essentially: yes.

"Given our extensive history with Starliner and the taxpayer investment already made," he said, "we believe it represents the fastest path to preserving reliable crewed access to low Earth orbit"-

.

What Boeing Has to Prove

The technical to-do list is substantial but specific. Boeing has already made thermal modifications to the service module, which Starliner-1 will evaluate in flight. After that mission, NASA and Boeing will implement an additional thruster valve design modification, focused on a known issue called "poppet seal extrusion" — a problem that has appeared before in human spaceflight-
26
. Boeing will also install new crew module thrusters, new batteries, and make minor parachute system modifications before anyone flies-
26
.

None of this is exotic. It's the unglamorous work of certification: testing, retesting, documenting, and proving — not just claiming — that the vehicle is safe.

What cannot be engineered as easily is trust. The 2024 mission shattered NASA's confidence in Starliner and, arguably, the public's confidence in Boeing's ability to deliver. The report documented a culture where "disagreement over crew return options deteriorated into unprofessional conduct while the crew remained on-orbit"-
11
.

That's the part that doesn't show up on a test stand.

The 2028 Question

Let's be precise about what 2028 means. It is a plan. Not a certified flight. Not a guarantee. Isaacman himself used the words "our current plan"-
21
.

Between now and then, Starliner-1 has to fly. Its thermal modifications have to work. The valve redesign has to be certified. Vulcan has to be certified for crew. The 61 recommendations have to be — not just addressed — but institutionalized, as the report put it, "to ensure that safety is never compromised in pursuit of schedule or cost"-
11
.

Any one of those steps could slip. Any one of them could fail. The history of this program is a history of schedules that didn't hold.

But the alternative — letting Starliner die — would leave NASA with a single operational U.S. crew vehicle and a retiring fleet. For an agency that spent the last decade building redundancy into its commercial crew strategy, that's not a future anyone wants to bet on.

So Boeing gets another chance. Wilmore and Williams — now both retired — won't be on the flight. Woody Hoburg will be. And somewhere in a clean room in Florida, engineers are already working on the valves.

The Takeaway

Starliner's story is not a redemption arc yet. It's a repair job — messy, expensive, and years overdue. But it's also a case study in what it takes to fail safely in human spaceflight: not the absence of failure, but the willingness to name it, investigate it, and try again.

Sixty-one recommendations aren't a verdict. They're a checklist.

The question is whether Boeing and NASA can actually check every box.
