The Weight of 61: Redefining "Recommendations"

When NASA concluded its investigation into Starliner’s 2024 crew flight, the agency’s deep dive yielded exactly 61 recommendations.

In the breathless arena of public opinion and social media, a number like 61 can easily be misunderstood. To a layperson, 61 recommendations might sound like an indictment—a laundry list of insurmountable flaws. But in the cathedral of orbital mechanics and spacecraft design, 61 is not a tally of proven unresolved defects. It is a blueprint.

Every time a spacecraft leaves the Earth’s atmosphere, it becomes a data-gathering leviathan. It records temperatures, pressures, and performance metrics that cannot be perfectly simulated in a laboratory. The 2024 crew flight did exactly what test flights are designed to do: it pushed the vehicle, stress-tested its systems, and brought back a mountain of telemetry.

Out of that data came the 61 recommendations. NASA’s investigation highlighted specific areas where the spacecraft's reality diverged from its simulations. Chief among these were the service-module thrusters, which operated outside their original engineering qualification limits. The vacuum of space is an environment of wild extremes, swinging from blistering solar radiation to the absolute freezing void in a matter of minutes. The investigation revealed that the thermal conditions and specific design features of the spacecraft contributed directly to these thruster performance anomalies.

This is where the actual engineering takes over from the theory. Following the data, Boeing has already implemented thermal modifications to address how the spacecraft handles the extreme heat dynamics of orbital flight. But spacecraft systems are deeply intertwined; changing how heat is managed often requires looking at how fluids and gases move through the vehicle. As a result, an additional valve redesign, along with a suite of other intricate changes, remains a critical part of the ongoing certification work.

The Timeline Gambit: Building the Bridge to 2028

Spaceflight operates on a currency of confidence, and confidence is bought with testing. Because the Starliner architecture is undergoing these thermal modifications and valve redesigns, those changes must be validated. NASA is not in the business of hoping a redesign works; they are in the business of proving it.

Administrator Isaacman’s announcement outlined the stepping stones required to reach the 2028 crew-return target. Before humans ever strap back into the Starliner capsule, the modified hardware will face the ultimate arbiter: space itself.

The current plan envisions flying the uncrewed Starliner-1 mission in either December 2026 or January 2027.

This uncrewed flight is the linchpin of the entire architecture. It is designed to be a rigorous evaluation of the engineering changes. Will the newly modified thermal systems maintain the proper equilibrium? Will the redesigned valves perform flawlessly under the extreme stress of launch, orbital maneuvering, and reentry? The uncrewed Starliner-1 mission will answer these questions without risking human life. It is a ghost ship built to chart a safe passage for the crew that will follow.

It is vital to understand the boundaries of NASA's announcement. The 2028 date for Starliner-2 is a plan, not a certified flight or an ironclad guarantee. Aerospace timelines are inherently fluid because they are dictated by data, not by PR schedules. If the uncrewed flight in late 2026 or early 2027 reveals that further tweaking is required, the 2028 plan will shift. In spaceflight, the schedule is always subordinate to safety and engineering readiness.

The Broader Chessboard: Why Redundancy is the Holy Grail

To fully grasp why NASA is committing this level of time, resources, and engineering bandwidth to the Starliner program, one must look at the agency’s FY2027 budget materials and the broader philosophy of modern space exploration.

Currently, the United States relies on a carefully balanced ecosystem for crew transportation to the International Space Station. The primary workhorse for American astronauts has been SpaceX’s Crew Dragon, flying alongside the venerable Russian Soyuz spacecraft. Both vehicles have proven their immense value, providing reliable access to low-Earth orbit.

So, why push forward with Starliner? Why not simply rely on the systems that are already flying?

Because in space exploration, relying on a single point of failure—or even just two—is a risk that mission planners refuse to take. Redundancy is the gravitational force that keeps a space program grounded in long-term viability.

If an anomaly were to ground one type of spacecraft, a lack of alternatives could cripple humanity's presence in space, halting research and jeopardizing the maintenance of orbital outposts. NASA’s overarching strategy has always been to cultivate a robust, multi-provider commercial space economy. Starliner is not just a backup; it is intended to be a foundational pillar of a diversified fleet. By working toward Starliner's certification, NASA is actively buying down systemic risk for the next decade of spaceflight.

The Rocket Equation: From Atlas to Vulcan

The capsule, of course, is only half the equation. A spacecraft is useless without a rocket to tear it away from Earth’s gravity. The September 2026 update also shed light on the future of the launch vehicles that will carry Starliner into the black.

NASA intends to exercise options for a fifth and sixth Starliner station flight in the future. Again, these are intended options, not evidence of completed missions, but they signal NASA's long-term faith in the architecture's potential. To execute these future missions, the launch infrastructure must also evolve.

Historically, Starliner has been paired with the United Launch Alliance (ULA) Atlas V rocket—a legendary launch vehicle with a storied history of reliability. But the Atlas V is approaching its twilight. As the aerospace industry marches forward, older platforms are retired to make way for more advanced, capable systems.

Isaacman confirmed that NASA is actively working hand-in-hand with Boeing and ULA to certify the new Vulcan rocket for Starliner missions, a transition that will occur after the Atlas V completes its final flight. Certifying a new rocket for human spaceflight is a monumental task of its own, requiring rigorous documentation, structural analysis, and flawless test flights. The Vulcan rocket represents the next generation of heavy-lift capabilities for ULA, and marrying it to the Starliner capsule ensures that the program has a modern, sustainable ride to orbit for years to come.

This transition highlights a beautiful, complex truth about aerospace engineering: nothing exists in a vacuum. Every modification to a spacecraft’s thermal system, every valve redesign, and every launch vehicle transition must be perfectly synchronized. It is an intricate ballet of thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, and orbital physics.
