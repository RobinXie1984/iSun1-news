The Gravity of a Second Chance
In the unforgiving vacuum of low Earth orbit, there are no participation trophies. Physics does not care about your launch schedule, your stock price, or your national pride. It only cares about thermodynamics, orbital mechanics, and the structural integrity of your valves.
On September 28, 2026, the reality of this ruthless environment was laid bare in a highly anticipated update regarding Boeing’s CST-100 Starliner. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman outlined a path forward that is as ambitious as it is cautious: a plan to return astronauts to the spacecraft on the Starliner-2 mission by 2028.
But before any human being straps into that command module again, the spacecraft must face a gauntlet of fire, data, and deep-space isolation. The road to 2028 is not a victory lap; it is a grueling engineering crucible. It requires an uncrewed test flight in the dead of winter, a complete transition of launch vehicles, and the digestion of a massive safety audit.
To understand where Starliner is going, we first have to understand the ghosts it is leaving behind.
The Anatomy of 61 Recommendations
When news breaks about an aerospace investigation yielding "61 recommendations," the public imagination often leaps to a catastrophic image: a spacecraft held together by duct tape and 61 unresolved defects.
It is vital to separate internet hyperbole from aerospace reality. Sixty-one recommendations are not 61 proven unresolved defects. In the rigorous, heavily regulated world of human spaceflight, a "recommendation" is the currency of survival. It is a formalized mandate to improve, document, or redesign. A recommendation can range from a physical re-machining of a titanium valve to a simple update in a flight controller’s procedural checklist. It is a holistic safety review designed to close every conceivable gap between a successful flight and a guaranteed one.
NASA’s investigation into the 2024 crew flight test—the mission that ultimately saw Starliner return to Earth uncrewed while its astronauts returned via SpaceX—uncovered hard truths about the spacecraft's service module. The data showed that certain service-module thrusters operated outside their original engineering qualification limits.
Why does this happen? Space is a paradoxical environment. It is freezing cold in the shadows and blisteringly hot in the sun, but because it is a vacuum, it lacks the air required for convective cooling. When a spacecraft fires its thrusters to perform an orbital insertion or a deorbit burn, those engines generate immense heat. On Earth, that heat is carried away by the atmosphere. In space, it can only radiate away slowly, or be absorbed by heat sinks. If the thermal conditions and design features of the service module aren't perfectly calibrated, that heat pools. It soaks into the surrounding plumbing, degrading the performance of critical valves and causing the thrusters to behave unpredictably.
Boeing has already moved to address this. Thermal modifications have been made to the spacecraft to better manage the brutal thermodynamics of orbital maneuvers. Furthermore, an additional valve redesign and other systemic changes remain a core part of the ongoing certification work.

The Uncrewed Crucible: Starliner-1
Because the stakes are human lives, NASA and Boeing are not rushing back to the launchpad with a crew. Before Starliner-2 can fly, Starliner-1 must prove itself in the void alone.
Targeted for December 2026 or January 2027, Starliner-1 will be an uncrewed mission to the International Space Station. This timing is highly strategic. Launching in the winter months often aligns with specific orbital mechanics and ISS visiting vehicle schedules, but more importantly, it sets a necessary cadence for data collection.
Starliner-1 is not merely a delivery run; it is a flying laboratory. Its primary mission is to evaluate the engineering changes born from those 61 recommendations. The newly implemented thermal modifications, the redesigned valves, and the updated software logic will all be pushed to their limits in the actual environment of space.
If the thrusters perform flawlessly within their qualified thermal envelopes, if the heat sinks absorb and radiate the energy as modeled, and if the service module operates with the quiet reliability required of a human-rated transport, Starliner-1 will pave the way for the future. But if the vacuum reveals new anomalies, the timeline will shift. This is why the 2028 crew-return target is strictly a plan, not a certified flight or a guarantee. In aerospace, the hardware dictates the schedule, not the calendar.
The Monopoly Myth and the Rule of Two
To the casual observer, the delays and engineering hurdles of Starliner might prompt a simple question: Why bother? SpaceX’s Crew Dragon is a proven, highly successful workhorse. Why is NASA investing the time, energy, and resources into exercising options for a fifth and sixth Starliner station flights?
The answer lies in the foundational doctrine of modern space exploration: redundancy.
According to NASA’s FY2027 budget materials, the current reality of U.S. crew transportation relies on the SpaceX Crew Dragon alongside the Russian Soyuz. While Dragon has performed admirably, relying on a single domestic provider for access to low Earth orbit is a strategic vulnerability that NASA is unwilling to accept. Spacecraft are complex machines subject to fleet-wide groundings. A single anomaly—a parachute tear, a software glitch, a trunk separation issue—can ground an entire fleet for months or years.
If the United States has only one operational domestic crew vehicle, a grounding forces NASA to rely entirely on the Russian Soyuz, an arrangement fraught with geopolitical complexities and limited seat availability. Starliner is not just a backup; it is a dissimilar system. It uses different parachutes, different docking mechanisms, and different launch abort profiles. If Dragon is grounded, Starliner can fly. If Starliner is grounded, Dragon can fly.
This is why NASA intends to exercise its options for those fifth and sixth flights. These are intended options for future missions, not evidence that they have already flown. They represent a long-term commitment to a dual-provider architecture, ensuring that the United States maintains uninterrupted, sovereign access to the orbital frontier.
The Vulcan Factor: A Rocket in Transition
Complicating the path to 2028 is not just the spacecraft, but the rocket that pushes it into orbit.
For its initial test flights, Starliner rode atop the venerable Atlas V, a rocket with a legendary record of reliability. But the Atlas V is flying its final missions. The era of this specific launch vehicle is coming to a close, and the mantle is being passed to United Launch Alliance’s (ULA) next-generation Vulcan rocket.
NASA intends to work closely with both Boeing and ULA to certify Vulcan for human spaceflight following the Atlas V's final flight. Certifying a new rocket for crew is a monumental task. It requires proving that the rocket's vibration profiles, acoustic loads, and abort-triggering telemetry are perfectly harmonized with the Starliner spacecraft.
The transition from Atlas V to Vulcan adds a layer of logistical complexity to the Starliner program. The spacecraft must be integrated with a new launch vehicle, and the combined stack must undergo rigorous safety reviews. It is a testament to the sheer scale of the orbital enterprise that NASA, Boeing, and ULA are managing the simultaneous certification of a heavily modified spacecraft and a brand-new rocket, all while keeping the International Space Station continuously crewed.
The Horizon: 2028 and Beyond
As we look toward the 2028 horizon, the narrative of Starliner shifts from one of troubleshooting to one of operational maturity.
If Starliner-1 succeeds in the winter of 2026/2027, the 61 recommendations will transition from theoretical fixes to proven flight heritage. The valve redesigns will be validated. The thermal modifications will be certified. The spacecraft will finally step out of the shadow of its 2024 crew flight and into its intended role as a vital pillar of American spaceflight.
The plan for Starliner-2 to carry astronauts by 2028 represents a belief in the engineering process. It is an acknowledgment that building machines capable of keeping humans alive in the most hostile environment known to our species is inherently difficult, painfully slow, and absolutely necessary.
There will be no shortcuts. There will be no glossing over the data. Every thruster firing, every thermal reading, and every valve actuation will be scrutinized. Because when the hatch finally closes on Starliner-2, and the countdown reaches zero, the men and women inside will be trusting their lives to the thousands of engineers who looked at 61 recommendations and refused to stop until every single one was resolved.
