Starliner's Second Chance: 61 Recommendations and a 2028 Plan

Headline options

Suspenseful:  One Empty Test Flight Stands Between Starliner and Its Next Crew — and It Could Launch as Soon as December

Emotional resonance:  The Spaceship That Carried Two Astronauts Up, Then Came Home Without Them

Counter-intuitive:  Why NASA Is Planning More Flights for the Capsule That Couldn't Bring Its Crew Home

The photo that aged strangely

There is a NASA photograph taken on July 3, 2024. Boeing's Starliner sits docked to the International Space Station's Harmony module, 256 miles above the Arabian Sea, off the coast of Mumbai. It is a long exposure. The Earth below is a blur of light.

It looks like a victory lap.

It wasn't.

That capsule had carried NASA astronauts Butch Wilmore and Suni Williams to the station on its first crewed flight. It was supposed to carry them home. Instead, Starliner came back to Earth empty. The crew stayed behind and returned later on a different spacecraft.

Spacecraft are judged on two numbers: how many people they launch, and how many they bring back. Starliner scored one out of two.

On September 28, 2026, NASA reused that same photo to illustrate a new announcement. The ship in the picture is the same. The story NASA wants to tell about it is not.

Takeaway:  In human spaceflight, getting there is the headline. Getting back is the job.

Monday's announcement

On Monday, September 28, 2026, NASA and Boeing issued a joint update on Starliner (NASA release 26-079). Strip away the press-release polish and it says four things.

First, the next Starliner won't carry people. An uncrewed mission, Starliner-1, could fly to the station in December 2026 or January 2027. NASA calls it an engineering evaluation flight.

Second, more fixes come after that flight. They include a thruster valve redesign that isn't finished yet.

Third, astronauts are expected back aboard on the flight after that. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman put it plainly: "Our current plan is to return astronauts on Starliner-2 by 2028."

Fourth, and most surprising, NASA intends to buy  more  Starliner flights. It plans to exercise options for a fifth and sixth station mission. It will also work with Boeing and United Launch Alliance to certify ULA's Vulcan rocket to launch Starliner once the Atlas V retires.

Read Isaacman's sentence twice. The key words are "current plan." That is not "scheduled." It is not "certified." It is not a promise. It is a target, stated by the person who owns the target.

Takeaway:  In a space program, "plan" is a load-bearing word. Don't read it as "date."

Sixty-one

In February 2026, NASA published the findings of its Program Investigation Team on the 2024 Crew Flight Test. The team looked at both what broke on the spacecraft and how the program was run. It produced 61 recommendations.

Sixty-one is the kind of number that goes viral for the wrong reason. It sounds like a list of 61 broken parts. It isn't.

The recommendations were addressed to NASA. Their stated purpose was to make sure future missions meet the standard human spaceflight demands. Some concern hardware. Others concern process: how decisions get made and how risk gets tracked. A recommendation is advice from investigators. It is not a confirmed defect, and it is not proof that 61 problems are still unsolved.

The better way to read 61 is as a measure of how hard the investigators looked. A short list after a flight like that one would worry me more than a long one.

What the source material does  not  tell us also matters. It doesn't say how many of the 61 are closed. It doesn't assign blame. It doesn't put a price on any of it. So I won't either.

Takeaway:  A long list of recommendations is a sign of scrutiny, not a count of failures.

Heat, a seal, and small rockets

Starliner steers with small reaction control thrusters mounted on its service module. They make the tiny corrections a capsule needs to hold its attitude and line up with the station.

According to NASA, some of those thrusters ran outside their engineering qualification during the Crew Flight Test. In plain terms: they operated in conditions they had never been proven to handle. NASA says this led to the loss of control experienced on that flight.

Why? After extensive ground testing and analysis, NASA and Boeing traced the problem to a mix of causes. One was the thermal environment around the thrusters. The other was features built into the design itself.

The valve fix still to come targets something called poppet seal extrusion. A poppet is a small valve that opens and closes to meter propellant. Its seal can deform and squeeze out of place. When that happens, NASA says, thruster performance suffers.

Here is the uncomfortable part. Nothing here was a single dramatic failure. It was an interaction: heat plus design, in a place the design hadn't been qualified to go. Interactions are the hardest bugs to find, because every part can pass its own test.

An imagined scene

A test stand, somewhere. It is late. An engineer watches a thruster fire, pause, and fire again, over and over, while heaters warm the hardware around it. She isn't waiting for an explosion. She's watching a line on a screen drift, by a fraction, pulse after pulse. Nobody will ever make a movie about that line. But whether two people can trust a ride home may depend on it.

End of fiction.

Takeaway:  The most dangerous failures aren't the loud ones. They're the ones that only show up when two "fine" things meet.

The road back, in order

The sequence NASA laid out is deliberate. Each step exists to earn the next one.

Thermal modifications, done.  Boeing has already changed the service module to improve its thermal environment.

Starliner-1, uncrewed.  Possibly December 2026 or January 2027. Its job: check whether the thermal changes worked, collect the performance data needed to qualify the system, and find whatever risk is left before people fly.

More changes after that flight.  The poppet-seal valve redesign gets finished. Boeing also plans new crew module thrusters, new batteries, and minor parachute changes to improve performance and reliability.

Testing and certification  for crewed flight.

Starliner-2, with astronauts.  Current plan: by 2028.

The order carries the logic. The first flight after the fixes has no crew. Nobody is asked to trust the capsule until it has shown, in space, that the heat problem is under control.

Dana Weigel, who manages NASA's Low Earth Orbit Program, framed Starliner-1 in similar terms. She said the safety of the station crew and the public comes first. She said the propulsion system will be tested through targeted demonstration objectives under disciplined operational controls. And she called validating thermal performance a key element of certification.

Notice what that means for the timeline. Every date after Starliner-1 depends on what Starliner-1 finds. If the data comes back clean, 2028 looks reachable. If it surfaces something new, the calendar moves. That isn't pessimism. It's how test flights work. The point of a test is that you don't know the answer yet.

Takeaway:  An uncrewed flight doesn't mean nobody's at risk. It means nobody's asked to take the risk before the data does.

Why double down?

This is the part that makes people squint. The capsule hasn't flown crew since 2024. Its next flight is empty. And NASA wants to add flights five and six?

NASA's stated reason is one word: options. The release says that having more than one way to carry crew has been a goal of the Commercial Crew Program from the start. The agency ties this to ending station operations responsibly, moving to commercial space stations, and widening commercial access to orbit.

The budget backdrop explains why this matters. NASA's FY2027 budget request says U.S. crew transportation currently relies on SpaceX's Crew Dragon, alongside Russia's Soyuz, while Starliner works toward certification. So there are ways to reach the station today. The question is how many  American  commercial options NASA wants when the next surprise hits.

Anyone who manages risk will recognize the logic. You don't build redundancy because you expect your main system to fail. You build it because you can't know when it will. A backup that only exists on paper is not a backup. It has to fly, keep flying, and stay certified.

The Vulcan piece is the quieter half of the same story. Starliner has launched on Atlas V, and Atlas V is heading toward its final flight. Without a certified replacement rocket, Starliner would be grounded no matter how good the capsule becomes. Certifying Vulcan keeps that from happening.

Two things are worth being clear about. "Intends to exercise options" is a stated intention, not flights that have happened. And none of this says anything bad about the providers already flying. It's a statement about how many providers NASA wants, not about any one of them.

Takeaway:  Redundancy looks wasteful right up until the day it's the only thing that works.

What to watch, and what to ask

If you want to follow this story without getting swept up by hype or doom, watch for three signals.

Does Starliner-1 launch in its December 2026 to January 2027 window?  A slip isn't a disaster, but it pushes everything after it.

What does NASA say about thruster thermal performance after the flight?  That is the core question the mission exists to answer.

When is the valve redesign finished and qualified?  Crewed flight can't be certified until it is.

Until those answers come in, 2028 stays exactly what NASA called it: a current plan.

The lines I'd keep from this story:

Getting there is the headline. Getting back is the job.

Sixty-one recommendations measure scrutiny, not failure.

The hardest bugs live where two "fine" systems meet.

A test flight is only useful if you're willing to believe what it tells you.

Redundancy is insurance you hope you never claim.

Starliner's comeback has an unglamorous shape. No new crew, no big moment, just an empty capsule, a lot of data, and a long list worked down line by line. That may be what a second chance really looks like.
