Suspenseful: Inside the Black Box: FTC Orders Nine Giants to Open Their Pricing Algorithms

Emotional Resonance: Who Sets the Price—and Who Gets the Cut? An Inquiry Into the Quiet Mechanics of Everyday Commerce

Counter-intuitive: The FTC Is Asking Whether Personalized Data Lowers Prices More Often Than It Raises Them

The Federal Trade Commission has opened a wide information-gathering inquiry into how nine large companies use personal data when setting prices for consumers and compensation for workers. On October 28, 2026, the agency ordered Delta, United, JetBlue, Lyft, Uber, DoorDash, Instacart, Target, and Walmart to supply detailed records on their pricing, offer-assignment, and compensation methods. Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson is the official quoted in the announcement. The orders rest on Section 6(b) of the FTC Act, which authorizes studies without a specific law-enforcement target. Recipients have 45 days from the date each order is received or served—not necessarily from October 28—to respond. The Commission voted 2–0. Nothing in the announcement establishes that any company has secretly discriminated, been found liable, or faces a fine or ban. Company responses, the resulting analysis, and any effects on consumers or workers remain unknown.

This is the story of a regulator deciding to look inside systems that already shape millions of daily transactions, without yet claiming what it will find.

What the orders actually request

The primary instrument is a 15-page special-report template. It asks each recipient for the methods used to set prices or assign offers, the data inputs that feed those methods, any override procedures, tests that have been run, disclosures made to customers or workers, and the division of customer payments between worker compensation and company revenue. General requests cover the period from January 1, 2023, through the date of compliance. Certain requests about research projects reach back to 2021. These are requests for records; they are not a released company dataset and they do not describe actual production code.

The template specifically inquires whether loyalty information, browsing history, or other personal data influences the prices or offers shown to customers. For the platform companies that use independent workers, it asks whether phone usage patterns or acceptance history influence compensation. The FTC is posing questions about possible practices. It has not reported findings that any of these practices occur at every recipient, nor that they produce the same effects everywhere.

An August proposed policy statement supplies background context. It is not an enacted rule. Earlier agency criticism of a 2024 study reflects the Commission’s own judgment at the time; the present inquiry stands on its own terms.

The companies and the markets they touch

The nine recipients span airlines, ride-hail and delivery platforms, and large retailers. Treating all nine as airlines would misstate the scope. Delta, United, and JetBlue move people by air; Lyft and Uber move people and sometimes goods by road; DoorDash and Instacart connect shoppers and delivery workers; Target and Walmart sell goods both in stores and online. What they share is scale and the routine use of digital systems that can adjust offers in real time.

Travelers already encounter fare changes that depend on remaining seats, days until departure, and competitor prices. Riders and diners see surge multipliers or delivery fees that rise when demand clusters in a neighborhood. Shoppers see loyalty prices that differ from shelf prices. Workers on platforms see acceptance rates and completion metrics that can affect which jobs appear or how much they earn. The inquiry asks how much of that variation is driven by general demand conditions and how much by data attached to a particular person or device.

Demand-based dynamic pricing and person-specific pricing are distinct. A higher fare when a flight is nearly full does not require the airline to know anything unique about the buyer. A lower price shown only to a logged-in loyalty member, or a delivery fee that changes after the app observes the user’s prior cancellations, does. The template is written to elicit evidence that can separate those mechanisms. Until the companies respond, the relative weight of each remains an open factual question.

Section 6(b) and the shape of the inquiry

Section 6(b) lets the Commission require reports from companies on a broad range of subjects. It does not require the agency to allege a violation of any existing statute. The October 28 orders are therefore an information-gathering exercise, not a complaint, a consent decree, or a finding of liability. The 2–0 vote indicates agreement among the sitting commissioners who participated; the announcement does not describe dissenting views.

The 45-day response window begins when each company is served. Practical compliance may involve internal legal review, data extraction, and, in some cases, negotiations over the scope or form of production. What the agency ultimately receives, and what it elects to publish or summarize, will determine whether the inquiry yields new public knowledge or remains largely internal.

Transparency, bargaining power, and efficiency—marked as analysis

From the standpoint of an entrepreneur who builds or invests in platforms, the inquiry highlights three practical tensions that already exist in the market, independent of any final FTC conclusion.

First, transparency. When a price or a payout changes, the person on the other side of the transaction rarely sees the full set of inputs. A traveler may know that fares rose because seats are scarce; a rider may not know whether an acceptance-rate penalty contributed to a lower payout. Companies argue that revealing every input would allow competitors or sophisticated users to game the system. Workers and consumers counter that opacity makes it harder to decide whether to accept an offer, switch platforms, or walk away. The template’s questions about disclosures and override procedures go directly to this trade-off. Greater disclosure could reduce surprise; it could also change the information advantage that some platforms currently hold.

Second, bargaining. On multi-sided platforms the company sits between the customer and the worker. Customer payments must cover worker compensation, platform costs, and profit. The orders ask how that division is calculated and whether personal data on either side shifts the split. If acceptance history or phone-use patterns systematically reduce a worker’s expected earnings, the worker’s outside options—other platforms, traditional employment, or simply declining work—become more important. If loyalty data systematically lowers a consumer’s price, the consumer’s willingness to compare across sites or brands also changes. The inquiry does not assume one outcome is better; it seeks the data that would let observers measure the size of any shift.

Third, efficiency. Personalized or segmented offers can, in principle, match supply more tightly to demand and reduce idle capacity. An airline that fills the last seats with price-sensitive travelers who would otherwise stay home may operate fuller flights. A delivery platform that steers high-acceptance workers toward peak hours may reduce wait times. The same tools can also extract more surplus from those with fewer alternatives. The template’s request for tests and research projects since 2021 is aimed at learning which effects companies themselves have measured. Until those records are produced and reviewed, claims about net efficiency gains or losses remain conjectural.

These three tensions—transparency, bargaining leverage, and efficiency—are ordinary features of any market that uses granular data. The FTC’s decision to examine them across airlines, platforms, and retailers at the same time simply places them under a common set of questions. Whether the answers differ by sector is itself an empirical question the responses may illuminate.

What remains unknown

No company responses have been released. The agency has not published interim findings. Effects on any particular consumer or worker are not documented in the announcement. The August policy statement remains a proposal. Criticism of the earlier 2024 study is the Commission’s prior characterization, not a new factual determination. The 45-day clocks are still running for at least some recipients. Any subsequent staff report, public workshop, or enforcement action would be a separate step.

Observers can watch for three signals. First, the volume and character of the material the companies produce—whether it includes actual algorithmic specifications, summary statistics, or primarily legal descriptions. Second, whether the Commission elects to release aggregated or anonymized findings. Third, whether any of the nine companies later adjust their public disclosures about pricing or compensation factors. Each of those developments would supply new facts; none is guaranteed by the October 28 announcement itself.

The practical stakes for operators and users

For companies already operating at this scale, the immediate task is compliance: locating the relevant records, determining which systems generated which offers, and preparing responses within the statutory window. Longer term, the inquiry may encourage more systematic internal documentation of how personal data enters pricing and compensation decisions. Entrepreneurs designing new platforms can treat that documentation as a design constraint rather than an after-the-fact legal exercise—recording the inputs, the tests run, and the disclosures made while the system is still being built.

For travelers, riders, shoppers, and independent workers, the inquiry does not change today’s prices or payouts. It does place on the public record a set of questions that many people have already asked informally: why did this fare appear, why did this job pay less than the last one, and how much of the difference is explained by data attached to me? Answers, if they arrive, will come from the companies’ filings and any subsequent FTC synthesis, not from the announcement alone.

The Commission has chosen to ask those questions of nine firms whose systems touch a large fraction of American commerce. The records it receives will either confirm that person-specific data plays a limited role beside ordinary demand signals, or they will show more granular influence than many outside observers currently assume. Either result would be useful. Until the responses arrive and are examined, the only firm conclusion is that the black box has been ordered open.
