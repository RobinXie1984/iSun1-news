Suspenseful: What Does the Algorithm Know Before It Names Your Price?

Emotional Resonance: Your Data, Your Price, Your Pay: Who Gets to Decide What You’re Worth?

Counter-intuitive: Personalized Prices Could Help Customers—and Still Leave Them With Less Bargaining Power

A price can tell you what something costs. A personalized price could also reveal what a company thinks you will tolerate. On October 28, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission announced an inquiry into how nine major companies use personal information in pricing and worker compensation. The central question reaches across shopping, travel, deliveries and rides: when businesses know more about the person on the other side of a transaction, how does that knowledge change the deal?

The inquiry has produced no announced findings about the named companies. It establishes neither wrongdoing nor a verdict on personalized pricing. Its importance lies in what the FTC wants to examine: the machinery connecting personal data, customer offers, worker pay and company revenue.

For entrepreneurs, that machinery deserves attention. Personalization can make a service more useful and a marketplace more efficient. It can also give one side of a transaction a much clearer picture of the other side’s choices. Understanding where those benefits and risks meet is becoming a business question as much as a regulatory one.

The price tag is acquiring a memory. We need to understand what it remembers—and what it does with that knowledge.

The nine recipients span several parts of everyday commerce: Delta, United, JetBlue, Lyft, Uber, DoorDash, Instacart, Target and Walmart. Airlines, transportation and delivery platforms, and retailers all fall within the inquiry. Describing this as an airline investigation would miss much of its scope.

The FTC issued the orders under Section 6(b) of the FTC Act, which allows the agency to conduct broad studies without a specific law-enforcement purpose. The commission’s vote was 2–0. Andrew N. Ferguson is the chairman quoted in the announcement.

Recipients have 45 days from receipt or service to respond. That clock does not necessarily begin on the October 28 announcement date.

These procedural details establish what kind of event this is. The agency is compelling information. It has not announced a lawsuit, a fine, a ban or a finding that any recipient secretly discriminated against customers or workers.

An inquiry opens a door. The evidence still has to come through it.

The first conceptual hurdle is the word “personalized.”

Prices already move for many reasons. Demand rises. Supply becomes constrained. Time matters. A business may charge different amounts at different moments because the commercial conditions have changed.

That is demand-based dynamic pricing. A changing price, by itself, tells us little about whether personal information influenced it.

Person-specific pricing raises another question: did information about the individual help determine the offer?

The FTC is asking whether loyalty information, browsing activity or other personal data affects prices. On the compensation side, it is asking whether information such as a driver’s phone usage or acceptance history affects pay.

Those are questions and possible practices under examination. They are not established descriptions of how every recipient operates.

The distinction matters because a useful debate needs to identify the mechanism. If prices rise because capacity is scarce, the discussion concerns scarcity, allocation and market power. If a price changes because a system infers something about a particular person, the discussion also concerns the use of that information.

Both mechanisms could exist within a market. Their effects require evidence.

A screenshot showing two different prices might start a conversation. Explaining the difference requires considerably more work.

The FTC’s 15-page special-report template gives a sense of that work.

It requests information about pricing methods, the assignment of offers and compensation methods. It asks about inputs, override procedures, tests and disclosures. It also seeks information about how customer payments are divided between worker compensation and company revenue.

The general requests cover January 1, 2023, through compliance, with some research-project requests reaching back to 2021.

This is a request for records, methods and explanations. The template is not a released company dataset, and it does not reveal the actual code running inside the named businesses.

Still, the categories are revealing about the inquiry’s design. The agency wants to understand more than the number that appears on a screen. It wants to know how that number was produced, what information entered the process, how the process was tested and when someone could override it.

From a business-analysis perspective, that is where the interesting questions begin.

An algorithm has an objective, whether explicitly defined or embedded in the way its success is measured. A system could be evaluated by conversion, revenue, service availability, retention or another outcome. Different objectives can produce different decisions even when the available data is identical.

Knowing that a business uses an algorithm therefore answers very little. The useful questions concern what it is designed to accomplish and what happens when it succeeds.

There is a credible argument for personalization, and it deserves a fair hearing.

As an economic possibility, a targeted discount could make a purchase affordable to someone who would otherwise walk away. More relevant offers could reduce the effort required to find a suitable product. Better matching could help available capacity reach people who value it.

These are potential benefits, not findings from this inquiry.

A business that understands demand more accurately might serve more customers, waste less capacity or improve its economics. Some of that gain could reach customers or workers. How much reaches them depends on the market, the design and the alternatives available.

For an entrepreneur, dismissing all personalization would mean overlooking useful opportunities. There is value in remembering preferences and reducing friction.

The harder question is what happens when the same information supports an estimate of how far someone can be pushed.

A system that learns what a customer likes may also learn something about how readily that customer will accept an offer. Information that improves relevance can potentially improve the seller’s bargaining position.

One dataset can support several commercial intentions. A friendly interface does not tell us which intention governs the transaction.

That brings us to bargaining power.

In my analysis, the most consequential issue may be the uneven distribution of knowledge. A company may have extensive information about patterns of behavior, while the customer sees a single offer and has limited insight into its construction.

The person can accept or decline. Yet the quality of that choice depends partly on what they can compare, how much time they have and whether alternatives are practical.

Consent to a transaction does not, by itself, explain the conditions under which the offer was accepted.

This does not establish that any named company exploited such an imbalance. It explains why the inquiry’s questions matter.

A market can become more precise at estimating preferences without making its participants equally well informed. Precision and bargaining power are different achievements.

Entrepreneurs should recognize the tension. Better information can create value. It can also help a business capture a larger share of value that already exists. A revenue increase alone cannot tell us which occurred.

The worker-compensation side makes that distinction especially important.

A customer’s payment and a worker’s compensation are connected, but they need not move together. The FTC’s request for information about the division between worker pay and company revenue puts that connection under examination.

The customer-facing price offers only a partial view of a platform transaction.

As an analytical matter, a higher customer charge could coincide with higher worker compensation, higher company revenue or some combination. A lower customer price could be funded in different ways. The actual allocation requires records.

That is why examining both sides is useful. A service may look more affordable to the buyer while changes elsewhere alter its overall effect. Conversely, a higher price may support improvements whose distribution needs to be understood.

The inquiry does not yet tell us which patterns occurred.

Its questions about driver phone usage and acceptance history raise a further issue: what should a compensation system learn from a worker’s behavior?

Information might help organize work or improve matching. It might also enable inferences about a worker’s willingness to accept particular terms. Establishing whether either occurs, and with what consequences, requires evidence about the inputs, methods and results.

Worker history becomes economically significant when it helps shape the next offer. The critical question is how that history is interpreted.

Transparency sounds like the obvious remedy. It is also harder than it sounds.

A general disclosure that prices “may vary” provides limited insight into whether personal information influenced a specific offer. A detailed technical explanation could overwhelm the very people it is intended to inform.

My view is that useful transparency should help someone make a decision or challenge an error.

That could mean explaining the categories of information that materially affect an offer, clarifying whether individual behavior influences compensation, or providing a workable route to review an unexpected outcome. These are possible design approaches, rather than requirements established by this inquiry.

The FTC’s request for disclosures and override procedures could help illuminate what arrangements exist. Their effectiveness remains unknown.

Override procedures are particularly interesting because automated systems inevitably operate within organizational choices. Someone decides which exceptions matter, who can intervene and what evidence justifies changing an outcome.

A review process has practical value only if it can reach the decision that needs reviewing.

For a founder, this is product design with consequences. A customer-support channel that cannot explain or correct an offer may leave the core source of confusion untouched.

The inquiry also invites a better definition of efficiency.

Businesses often measure efficiency through revenue, conversion, utilization or cost. Those measures are useful, yet each captures a particular perspective.

A system might improve a company’s financial results while leaving customer outcomes unchanged. It might increase total activity while changing the division of gains between workers and the platform. It might produce benefits across several groups.

These are possibilities to test, not conclusions to assume.

An informative analysis would ask what changed relative to a meaningful comparison: prices, availability, completed transactions, worker earnings, company revenue, or the effort required to find an acceptable deal.

It would also look beyond averages. An overall improvement can coexist with uneven outcomes. Understanding that distribution is part of assessing the system.

The scientific habit is straightforward: define the claimed benefit, choose a relevant comparison and inspect the result. “The algorithm improved performance” remains an incomplete statement until performance has a clear meaning.

For entrepreneurs, this creates an opportunity to compete on understandable terms.

A business could choose to explain how personalization benefits its customers and show evidence supporting that explanation. It could design internal reviews that examine customer and worker outcomes alongside revenue. It could make disputed decisions easier to revisit.

Whether those choices earn trust or improve commercial performance is itself testable. They should not be treated as automatic marketing magic.

But they offer a useful strategic question: can a company make its use of data an advantage that customers can recognize?

Personalization is easier to defend when the benefit is visible. An offer that feels relevant and can be understood has a different relationship with trust from an unexplained number that changes without an intelligible reason.

Trust deserves measurement too. Complaints, repeat use and willingness to share information can help a business investigate whether its system is building confidence or spending it.

For now, the public record described in the brief stops at the inquiry.

The August proposed policy statement remains background; it is not a newly enacted rule. Any agency criticism of its 2024 study should be understood as the agency’s judgment, rather than an independently established conclusion.

Company responses, substantive results and effects on consumers and workers remain unknown.

Those limits leave plenty worth exploring. The FTC has framed a consequential set of questions about how information travels into prices and pay. The answers could help distinguish beneficial matching, ordinary responses to demand and uses of personal data that deserve closer scrutiny.

The entrepreneurial lesson begins with a simple discipline: understand what your system is learning about people, and examine what you reward it for doing with that knowledge.

A more intelligent marketplace can create better matches and more useful choices. Its success should be judged partly by the people accepting those choices and doing the work behind them.

If a company uses your personal data to shape your price or your pay, what would you want in return: a measurable benefit, a clear explanation, a meaningful choice—or all three?
