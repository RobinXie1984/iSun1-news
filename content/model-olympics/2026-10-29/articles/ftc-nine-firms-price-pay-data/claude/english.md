Nine Companies, One Question: Does the Price Know Who You Are?
Three headline options

Suspenseful: The FTC Has 45 Days of Questions for Nine Companies, and the Answers Could Redefine What “The Price” Means

Emotional Resonance: Is the Price on Your Screen Meant for You Alone? Regulators Are Asking Uber, Walmart, Delta and Six Others

Counter-intuitive: The Most Important Price Case of the Year Isn’t a Case. It’s a Questionnaire.

The bottom line first

On October 28, 2026, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission ordered nine companies to explain whether, and how, personal data shapes the prices customers pay and the money workers earn. The companies are three airlines (Delta, United and JetBlue), four platform and delivery businesses (Lyft, Uber, DoorDash and Instacart) and two big retailers (Target and Walmart).

This is not a lawsuit, a fine, a ban or a finding that any of these companies did anything wrong. It is a study. Under Section 6(b) of the FTC Act, the agency can require companies to hand over information for a broad inquiry without a specific law-enforcement purpose. The Commission voted 2–0 to issue the orders. Each recipient has 45 days from the day it is served to respond, so the deadline is not simply 45 days after October 28.

The inquiry matters because of what it is trying to see: the logic behind prices and pay. For decades, the shared price has been one of the quiet foundations of commerce. One shelf tag, one fare, one rate card. The FTC is now asking whether that foundation is still intact for companies that know a great deal about each customer and each worker. Nobody outside these companies knows the answer yet, and the regulator doesn’t either. That is the point of asking.

What actually happened

The facts are short.

The FTC announced the inquiry on October 28 and published the template of a 15-page special report. Companies will have to fill it in. Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson is the commissioner quoted in the announcement.

The questions run in two directions:

Toward customers: whether loyalty-program information, browsing behavior or other personal data affects the prices or offers a person sees.
Toward workers: whether data such as a driver’s phone usage or history of accepting jobs affects how much that driver is paid.

These are questions about practices that may exist. They are not established findings about any of the nine recipients, and nothing in the announcement shows that every company, or any particular one, uses personal data in the ways described.

The time window is wide. General requests cover January 1, 2023 through the date of compliance. Some requests about research projects reach back to 2021.

Background matters too. In August, the FTC issued a proposed policy statement related to this area. It is a proposal, not a newly enacted rule. The agency has also judged that its own 2024 study of the topic fell short, and that is the agency’s own assessment of its earlier work. This new round of orders is, in part, an attempt to collect better evidence than it had before.

What we do not know is long: what the companies will say, what the data will show, and whether consumers or workers have been helped, harmed or neither. Every one of those questions is open.

Two prices that move for very different reasons

The single most useful idea for reading this story is that prices can move for two very different reasons, and the brief is careful not to blur them.

Demand-based dynamic pricing moves with the market. A flight gets more expensive as seats sell out. A ride costs more when everyone leaves a stadium at once. The price responds to conditions, and anyone in the same situation at the same moment sees roughly the same number. It may be frustrating, but it is legible. You can wait, book earlier, or walk a few blocks.

Person-specific pricing moves with you. The input is not “how many seats are left” but “what do we know about this customer.” That could be purchase history, loyalty status, how often you browse without buying, or other signals tied to your identity or device.

Both look the same from the outside. You see a number on a screen. You cannot tell why it is that number. That is why a price difference by itself proves nothing. Two people seeing different fares may simply be searching at different times, for different seats, under different inventory conditions. Assuming every price gap comes from personal data would be as wrong as assuming none of them do.

The FTC’s inquiry is, in effect, an attempt to look under the hood and separate the two.

The other side of the ledger: pay

The worker half of the inquiry is the part I’d watch most closely, and it gets less attention than it deserves.

On a platform, the same algorithmic machinery can work in both directions at once. It sets what the rider pays and what the driver earns. The FTC’s template asks companies to describe how the money a customer pays is divided between worker compensation and company revenue.

That one request goes to the heart of how platforms work. A platform sits between two sides of a market. If it has detailed data on both sides, it can in principle shape the price for one and the pay for the other separately. The gap between them is the platform’s take.

The FTC asks specifically whether things like a driver’s phone usage or job-acceptance history affect compensation. To be clear, that is a question. It is not evidence that any company does it. But the very fact that regulators thought to ask shows how far the conversation has moved. “Personalized pricing” used to mean shoppers. It now plausibly includes paychecks.

Reading the questionnaire like a product spec

I find the 15-page template more revealing than the press release. Read it the way an engineer reads a spec, and it sketches the architecture the FTC suspects might exist.

According to the template, companies are asked to describe:

Methods: how prices are set, how offers are assigned to people, and how compensation is determined.
Inputs: what data feeds those methods.
Override procedures: when and how humans step in to change what the system produces.
Tests: what experiments the company has run.
Disclosures: what customers and workers are told.
The split: how a customer’s payment divides between worker pay and company revenue.

Any builder will recognize this list. It is the anatomy of a pricing system: model, features, human-in-the-loop controls, A/B tests, user-facing messaging, unit economics. The FTC is asking for the full stack.

A caution is needed here. These are requested records. The template is not a released dataset and it is not an account of any company’s actual code. It tells us what the FTC wants to know. It tells us nothing yet about what any company does.

The request for testing records, some reaching back to 2021, stands out to me. Experiments are where companies learn what customers will tolerate and what workers will accept. If personalization exists anywhere, the test logs are where its fingerprints would be. If it does not exist, those same logs could help show that.

What this is not

It is worth stating plainly, because headlines will be tempted to blur it:

Not a lawsuit. No complaint has been filed against these nine companies over these questions.
Not a conviction or finding. No company has been found to discriminate, secretly or otherwise.
Not a ban or a fine. Nothing is prohibited by these orders and no penalty has been imposed.
Not a new rule. The August document is a proposed policy statement, and this inquiry is information-gathering.
Not a verdict on all nine as one type of business. These are airlines, ride and delivery platforms, and retailers. Their pricing problems differ a great deal.

Section 6(b) studies exist precisely so a regulator can understand an industry before deciding whether anything needs doing. Sometimes that leads to enforcement or rules. Sometimes it leads to a report and nothing more. Right now, nobody knows which way this one will go.

Analysis: three lenses for thinking about it

Everything in this section is my own analysis, not a finding from the FTC or a claim about any named company.

1. Transparency: the price as a message

A price is information. A public price tells everyone the same thing at once. That is part of why markets work: you can compare, shop around and learn what something costs.

Person-specific pricing, if it exists at scale, changes what a price means. The number stops telling you about the product and the market. It starts telling you about the seller’s view of you. The big question then is not whether personalization happens. It is whether people can tell when it does.

That’s why “disclosures” in the template matters. A world where people know their loyalty status affects their offers is very different from one where they can’t tell. The first is a negotiation. The second is a one-way mirror.

2. Bargaining: who knows more

Every price is a quiet negotiation, and negotiations turn on information. Historically, sellers knew their costs and buyers knew their own budget. That rough balance held prices in check.

Detailed personal data can tilt that balance. If a seller can estimate how badly you need something, or how unlikely you are to look elsewhere, the seller holds more cards. The same reasoning applies to workers, maybe more strongly. A gig worker usually sees one offer at a time and has limited visibility into what others are paid or how offers are chosen. If pay were tuned to personal signals, the worker would be bargaining against a counterparty that can see much more of the board.

Again, whether this actually happens at any of these companies is unknown. The structure is what makes the question worth asking.

3. Efficiency: the case on the other side

Fairness requires taking the opposing view seriously, and economists have one.

Price discrimination in the textbook sense is not automatically harmful. Charging different prices to different people can expand access. Student discounts, senior fares and targeted coupons put goods in reach of people who would otherwise skip them. Personalized offers can also mean fewer irrelevant promotions and more useful ones. On the worker side, matching jobs to people more precisely could, in principle, cut idle time and raise earnings for some.

So the honest question is not whether personalization is good or bad. It is narrower and harder:

Who captures the gains?
Do people know it’s happening?
Can they opt out, compare or walk away?

A discount you can see and choose differs fundamentally from a markup you can’t detect. The FTC’s request for inputs, tests and the payment split looks designed to tell those cases apart.

Why an entrepreneur should care

If you build anything with a pricing engine, a marketplace or a pay model, read the template as a preview.

Its six categories (methods, inputs, overrides, tests, disclosures, revenue split) are a reasonable checklist for any data-driven pricing system, whatever happens with this inquiry. Could you explain your inputs in plain language? Do you log when humans override the model? Do your experiments have a written rationale? Could a customer or worker understand, at a high level, why they saw the number they saw?

None of this is legal advice, and the inquiry creates no new obligations for anyone outside the nine recipients. But regulators tend to ask the same questions again once they have learned to ask them. Builders who can already answer them clearly will spend less time scrambling later.

There’s also an opportunity in it. If trust in prices becomes a competitive issue, then clear, explainable pricing could become a selling point instead of a compliance cost.

What to watch next
The 45-day clocks. Each runs from when a company is served, so deadlines may fall on different dates. Extensions or negotiations over scope are possible but not known.
Whether the FTC publishes findings. A 6(b) study often ends in a public report. When, and how detailed, is unknown.
The August proposed policy statement. Watch whether it is finalized, revised or left as is, and whether this inquiry’s data shapes it.
Company responses. None are included in the brief. What the nine companies say publicly, if anything, is still to come.
The worker side. If the payment-split data is ever summarized publicly, it could be one of the most informative views yet of platform economics.
The question underneath

Strip away the agency process and one simple question is left: when a company sets a price or a wage, is it pricing the situation or pricing the person?

For most of commercial history, the answer was obviously the situation. The tools to price the person at scale didn’t exist. Now they plausibly do, and the FTC is asking nine large companies to show their work.

We don’t yet know what they’ll show. The answer might be reassuring, troubling or more mundane than either side expects. But the act of asking already changes something. It signals that the logic behind a number on a screen, the inputs, the tests, the overrides and the split, is no longer viewed as purely private. In 45 days, give or take, at least part of that logic begins moving from inside these companies toward a regulator.

That’s the real story. Not a verdict, but a door starting to open.
