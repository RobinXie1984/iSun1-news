# Behind the EU's 19% Housing Average, a 36% Share Comes Into View

On 26 November 2026, Eurostat, the statistical office of the European Union, released *Housing in Europe – 2026 edition*. The interactive publication describes how people across the EU's member states lived in 2025. Eurostat acts as a central bookkeeper for European statistics. National statistical offices collect figures in their own countries, and Eurostat applies shared definitions so that a household in Lisbon can be compared with one in Tallinn. The publication's headline figure sounds calm. On average, households in the EU spent 19% of their disposable income on housing in 2025. Further into the publication, a second figure appears. People in a lower relative-income group, as Eurostat defines it, spent an average of 36% of their disposable income on housing. That share is almost double the overall figure.

Both numbers are accurate at the same time, because they describe different populations. The 19% figure summarises everyone in the private households covered by Eurostat's survey, a scope explained below. The 36% figure summarises one defined group within that population. The story lies in the space between them: who sits inside the average, where they live, and how much of their money their homes absorb.

Housing is a bill that returns every month. It competes with food, transport, heating and savings for the same pool of money, so the share it takes shapes what remains for everything else. That is why statisticians measure it as a proportion of income alongside its size in euros.

Even before any breakdown by income, the EU picture covers a wide range. Eurostat reports country averages from 11% of disposable income in Cyprus to 35% in Greece. Denmark recorded 28%, and Germany and Sweden each recorded 24%. The EU's 19% is an average of housing cost shares across households. It differs from a median, which is the value for the household in the exact middle of the line. It is also a description of spending, separate from any recommended budget or official limit.

## What the figures count

Two terms carry most of the weight in these statistics. The first is disposable income. This is the money a household has available to spend or save after taxes and social contributions, including earnings, pensions and social benefits. The second is housing costs, and Eurostat defines these with care.

For tenants, housing costs include the rent they actually pay. For owners, they include mortgage interest, after any tax relief on that interest. Both groups can also have utilities, structural insurance, service charges, regular maintenance and repairs, and housing taxes where these apply. One item stays outside the measure. When an owner repays the borrowed amount itself, which lenders call the principal, that part of the monthly mortgage payment falls beyond this definition. A home's purchase price and a first-time buyer's deposit also fall beyond it. So these figures describe the ongoing cost of living in a home, and the separate question of what it costs to buy one belongs to other statistics.

Some households receive housing allowances, which are public payments that help with rent or other housing costs. For its main measure of heavy housing burden, Eurostat takes these allowances into account on both sides of the calculation. It subtracts them from housing costs and also from disposable income. The list of cost components and this allowance adjustment are two separate steps in the method, and the final indicator reflects both.

The 2025 figures on housing cost shares and living conditions come from a survey framework called EU-SILC, short for EU statistics on income and living conditions. Every year, member states collect information from people in private households using harmonised definitions. Eurostat then combines these national results into EU figures that reflect each country's population. People living in institutions or other collective households generally sit outside this frame. Homelessness and unsheltered housing need therefore appear in these indicators only partially. The survey also has its own timing. The income a household reports can refer to an earlier period than its current housing costs. The 2025 survey figures are best read as a careful annual portrait assembled from survey answers. They differ from a live reading taken on a single day. The publication also sets them beside separately dated historical comparisons and price-index series, each with its own measure, which appear later in this article.

One more figure helps set the scene. In 2025, 68% of the EU population lived in owner-occupied homes. The counting unit here is people. The figure refers to residents, as distinct from the number of households, the number of dwellings, the number of recent buyers or the number of individuals named on a property deed. National shares differ sharply: Eurostat reports 47% for Germany and 94% for Slovakia.

Owner occupancy tells us how a home is held. Owners include people still paying a mortgage and people who have paid theirs off. Both groups still face utilities, insurance, repairs and, for the first group, interest. Housing cost share asks a different question: how much of disposable income those ongoing costs consume. The 68% figure answers the first question and leaves the second for other measures.

## The income divide

The 36% figure rests on a dividing line that Eurostat uses throughout its social statistics. The line sits at 60% of the national median equivalised disposable income. Each part of that phrase has a plain meaning.

The median is the middle value: if everyone's income were lined up from lowest to highest, the median would belong to the person in the middle. "Equivalised" means incomes are adjusted for household size and composition. A single adult and a family of four with the same total income have very different amounts per person. The adjustment places them on a common scale so they can be compared fairly. "National" means each country uses its own median. The line therefore falls at a different euro amount in each country. A person counts as below the line when their household's adjusted income falls under 60% of the middle income in their own country. Eurostat uses this same line as its at-risk-of-poverty threshold.

In 2025, people below this line spent an average of 36% of their disposable income on housing. People at or above it spent an average of 16%. The 36% describes how much of their income went to housing. The size of the group below the line is a separate statistic.

An everyday picture shows how such a gap can arise. This is an illustration of arithmetic, separate from Eurostat's findings. Imagine the same grocery receipt for 50 euros set beside two different weekly budgets. Against a budget of 500 euros, the receipt takes 10%. Against a budget of 250 euros, the same receipt takes 20%. The receipt stays identical; the slice doubles because the budget is smaller. Housing cost shares work the same way. A share can rise because the cost rises, because income falls, or through both at once.

Eurostat's 2025 figures show the size of the gap between the two income groups. They leave open how much of it comes from lower incomes and how much from the cost of the homes these groups occupy. Because the figures form a cross-section, a snapshot of one year, they describe the pattern and leave its causes for further study.

This returns us to the 19% average. An overall figure blends the experience of both groups and lands between them. The people spending around a third of their income on housing are folded into that blend. The average remains a fair summary of the surveyed population living in private households. Residents of institutions and other collective households generally sit outside that frame, and unsheltered housing need appears in it only partially. Within the surveyed population, the lower-income group's experience appears only when the population is divided.

## The map within the average

Income is one way to divide the population. Place is another, and for that Eurostat uses a measure called the housing cost overburden rate. It counts the percentage of people living in households whose housing costs exceed 40% of disposable income. Housing allowances are accounted for on both sides, as described above. "Exceed" means strictly more than 40%. A household spending exactly 40% belongs to the group with cost shares of 40% or less.

The 40% threshold is a statistical convention. It marks a point at which Eurostat counts a housing burden as heavy, giving every country a common yardstick. It works as a measuring marker. It is separate from any rule for personal budgeting, and it makes no forecast about eviction for any particular household.

In 2025, almost 10% of people living in EU cities were in housing-cost-overburdened households. In rural areas, the figure was 5%. The city rate was higher in 24 EU countries and lower in three. National examples show how differently this pattern can play out. In Greece, the rates were 27% in cities and 26% in rural areas, high in both settings. In Denmark, they were 20% in cities and 10% in rural areas, a gap similar in proportion to the EU-wide one.

These percentages count people living in affected households. They differ from the share of city income spent on housing, and they differ from the share of homes or tenants facing difficulty. They describe populations living in different kinds of places.

The pattern raises questions about how housing costs, incomes and location relate to each other. It leaves one practical question for case-by-case judgement: what any particular household might gain by moving. That calculation would depend on wages, jobs, transport, the homes available, their quality and personal preferences. The city–rural comparison describes the populations as they stood in 2025, and the savings from a move remain outside what it measures.

## A wider view of housing conditions

A single ratio captures one kind of pressure. Eurostat's publication adds several other indicators, each measuring something different.

The first concerns arrears, meaning payments that fall overdue. In 2025, 9% of people in the EU lived in households with arrears on a mortgage, rent or utility bills. The word "or" matters here. A household counts if it fell behind on any one of these payments, so the 9% describes people affected by at least one overdue housing-related bill. In 2010, the comparable share was 12%, so the EU figure has fallen over fifteen years. Greece stood far above the average, at 42% in 2025.

The publication also reports on space and warmth for 2025. Overcrowded housing, meaning homes with too few rooms for the people living there under Eurostat's criteria, accounted for 17% of the EU population. Underoccupied housing, meaning homes with more rooms than the household needs under those criteria, accounted for 33%. Separately, 9% of people lived in households that reported an inability to keep their home adequately warm. Each of these figures answers its own question with its own criteria. They stand side by side as separate measures. Adding them together would mix different questions.

Price series supply further context. Between 2010 and 2025, EU house prices rose 61%, rents rose 29% and consumer prices, the general cost of goods and services, rose 43%. These are nominal index changes. An index tracks how a typical level of prices moves over time compared with a starting year. "Nominal" means the figures are in current money, before any adjustment for inflation. Each series follows its own basket and method.

Read side by side, the indices suggest that house prices outpaced general prices over the period, while the rent series grew more slowly than general prices. Each series describes an average movement. A specific family's rent, a specific home's value and a new buyer's monthly payment are separate quantities that require their own data. The indices also leave the 2026 outlook open.

The arrears decline and the rise in house prices occurred over the same years. The publication records both movements and leaves their causes open. Taken together, the indicators show that housing difficulty has several dimensions. A household can keep up with every payment and still live in cramped rooms. A household can have ample space and still struggle to heat it.

## Where the next look begins

The publication offers a careful description of 2025, and its design sets the reach of that description. EU-SILC's harmonised definitions make countries comparable. Its private-household frame and survey timing define the population and period that the survey indicators describe. The 2025 cost-share and living-condition figures in this article are survey estimates for that population in that year. The 2010 arrears comparison and the 2010–2025 house-price, rent and consumer-price indices carry their own dates and measures. The patterns all these figures reveal still await a causal explanation.

That same design suggests where a closer look would start. Each comparison in this article divides the population along one line at a time: income in one, location in another, tenure in a third. A focused investigation would bring those lines together. It would compare housing cost shares for lower-income and higher-income people as tenants, as owners with mortgages and as outright owners, in cities and in rural areas, and set arrears, warmth and space indicators beside each group. Eurostat's detailed tables and metadata are the natural place to begin, and that investigation would confirm which combinations and margins of error they provide.

Public debate about housing often turns to a familiar set of levers: building more homes, improving energy efficiency so homes cost less to heat, and supporting household incomes. Each carries trade-offs in cost, timing and who benefits. The 26 November publication is a statistical report. It describes conditions and leaves policy choices to governments and their own evidence. Any claim that a particular law, construction programme or subsidy relieved housing pressure would need separate evidence: its design, its implementation dates and its measured results.

What the 2025 figures establish is a clear pattern. Across EU private households, housing took an average of 19% of disposable income. For people below their country's relative income line, it took 36%. Heavy burdens were roughly twice as common among city residents as among rural residents. Arrears have declined since 2010, while cramped homes and cold homes remain part of the picture. The average gives the starting point. The groups inside it raise the question that the next layer of evidence will have to answer: for which combinations of income, tenure and place do housing costs take the largest share of income, and which other signs of housing difficulty travel alongside that burden?
