# EU Housing Costs Average 19% of Income. Who Pays Nearly Twice That Share?

A home takes a share of the money available for everything else: food, travel, clothing and the ordinary surprises of life. On 26 November 2026, Eurostat, the European Union’s statistical office, published its *Housing in Europe – 2026 edition*, bringing together evidence on housing costs, ownership and living conditions. Its headline figures describe 2025, when EU households spent an average of 19% of disposable income on housing. Behind that broad figure lay a much heavier claim on the budgets of people with lower incomes.

Across the EU, people below their own country’s at-risk-of-poverty income threshold had an average housing cost share of 36%. For people at or above that threshold, the average was 16%. The lower-income group therefore devoted more than twice as large a share of its available income to the costs covered by Eurostat’s measure. Both groups belong within the same European housing picture. Their distance from each other gives the publication its central question: whose circumstances become harder to see when millions of households are brought together into one average?

The larger setting is a housing system with several overlapping experiences. Tenants pay rent. Owners face ongoing expenses and, in some cases, mortgage payments. People seeking their first home encounter purchase prices and the need to assemble a deposit. Established residents may occupy homes acquired under very different financial conditions. Eurostat’s publication offers ways to distinguish these experiences, beginning with a careful account of what each number measures.

An everyday comparison helps. Imagine the average journey time for a group of commuters. A single figure can describe the group while some members spend much longer travelling than others. Separating commuters by where they live brings those longer journeys into view. Housing cost shares work similarly, with an extra complication: the share depends on both the housing bill and the income available to pay it.

That arithmetic matters immediately to the 36% and 16% comparison. A household’s cost share can rise when its housing bill increases. It can also rise when its disposable income falls while the bill stays constant. Conversely, higher income can reduce the share even as housing expenses remain unchanged. These are properties of the calculation. The published comparison describes the position of income groups in 2025; the relative contributions of earnings, housing expenses and household composition remain open.

Disposable income includes relevant cash benefits and is the income available after taxes and other compulsory deductions. Eurostat also adjusts household income for household size and composition when assigning people to the income groups used here. This adjustment, called “equivalisation”, recognises that several people living together can share expenses, while additional household members still bring additional needs. It allows more meaningful comparisons between, for example, an adult living alone and adults sharing a home with children.

The at-risk-of-poverty threshold is 60% of the national median of that adjusted disposable income. The median is the middle value when incomes are arranged in order. Each country supplies its own reference point, so the classification places people within their national income distribution. The euro amount associated with the threshold therefore varies across countries. The 36% figure then describes the average housing cost share among people below their respective national thresholds.

This distinction gives the number its human significance. Housing absorbed an average of more than a third of available income in that group, leaving a smaller share for other needs. Moreover, a smaller remaining share of a low income can represent a particularly tight spending allowance. The measure identifies pressure on household resources, while the detailed choices people made within those budgets remain beyond the comparison.

The EU average of 19% also spans considerable differences between countries. In 2025, national averages ranged from 11% in Cyprus to 35% in Greece. These figures describe average cost shares within the covered populations. Their usefulness lies in establishing scale and variation. A typical household’s experience, the spread around each average and the reasons for the differences require closer examination.

Ownership adds another layer. Eurostat reported that 68% of EU residents lived in owner-occupied dwellings in 2025. The figure counts people living in those homes, including household members who may hold no personal legal title. It also encompasses owners with different borrowing arrangements and financial circumstances. Ownership describes the relationship between a household and its dwelling; affordability depends on the expenses and resources attached to that relationship.

For tenants, Eurostat’s housing cost concept includes actual rent. For owners, it includes mortgage interest after relevant tax relief. Depending on the household, the measure also covers utilities, structural insurance, services and charges, regular maintenance and repairs, and taxes. A home owned outright still carries applicable running costs. A tenant’s rent likewise forms only one part of the expenses associated with keeping a home in use.

Mortgage principal—the part of a repayment that reduces the amount borrowed—sits outside this statistical cost measure. Interest is the charge for borrowing; principal repays the loan itself. A borrower’s full monthly payment can consequently exceed the mortgage amount represented in Eurostat’s housing cost calculation. The purchase price and the deposit belong to separate questions about acquiring a home.

That boundary is especially important for someone reading the 19% average while trying to enter the housing market. The figure offers evidence about measured housing costs relative to income among covered households. The money needed to secure a purchase, and the full cash commitment of a particular mortgage, require their own assessment. Existing ownership and access to ownership are connected subjects with different financial hurdles.

Eurostat uses a second measure to show how widely especially high ongoing cost shares extend. The “housing cost overburden rate” counts the percentage of people living in households whose housing costs exceed 40% of disposable income. The threshold is strictly more than 40%. In this calculation, housing allowances are netted out of both housing costs and disposable income, keeping the treatment of that assistance consistent on both sides.

The distinction between this rate and the average cost share is fundamental. An average cost share describes how much income housing absorbs. An overburden rate describes how many people live in households above a specified cost-share threshold. The 36% lower-income average and a geographical overburden rate answer different questions, even though both concern pressure on the household budget.

The 40% line gives statisticians a common reference for comparison. Its role is to identify one form of financial strain. A household with a cost share of 40% or less can still struggle with bills, occupy cramped rooms or experience inadequate warmth. A household with a cost share strictly greater than 40% has crossed the indicator’s defined boundary; its future housing security depends on circumstances that the threshold alone leaves unresolved.

Geography makes the difference between the measures particularly clear. In 2025, almost 10% of people living in EU cities belonged to households above the overburden threshold, compared with 5% of people in rural areas. These figures count residents in each type of area. They cover people across housing tenures, with the city population showing roughly twice the prevalence of this particular form of pressure.

The city rate was higher than the rural rate in 24 EU countries and lower in three. That pattern gives the EU comparison considerable breadth, while national results retain their own character. Greece illustrates the importance of looking closely: its overburden rate reached 27% among city residents and 26% among rural residents. In both settings, roughly a quarter of people lived in households whose measured housing costs exceeded 40% of disposable income.

For a household considering a move, these figures establish a geographical pattern worth investigating. The household’s own outcome would depend on the homes available, the income it could earn, travel expenses and the suitability of the dwelling. A rural average describes existing rural residents and their circumstances. A move changes several parts of a household budget at once, so the savings associated with a particular relocation remain a separate practical question.

The longer history adds context to these differences. Between 2010 and 2025, EU house prices rose by 61%, rents by 29% and consumer prices by 43%, according to the publication’s respective indices. These are cumulative changes in nominal prices—prices expressed in the money of the period, before adjusting for inflation. Each series follows a different set of prices, and each describes change across a broad market or basket.

The figures show that house purchase prices rose faster over those years than either rents or the wider consumer price measure. Their implications for a specific person depend on when that person entered the market, their income and their housing arrangements. A purchase price index tracks one aspect of access to ownership. A rent index tracks another stream of housing expense. Translating either into an individual household’s payment requires details about that household.

Payment difficulties offer a further view. In 2025, 9% of the EU population lived in households with arrears on mortgage, rent or utility bills, compared with 12% in 2010. Arrears mean payments had fallen behind. This combined indicator captures difficulty with one or more of those obligations, so the figure embraces several routes into financial strain.

The decline records an improvement in this particular measure across the stated period. It sits alongside the increases in the price indices because payment performance and market prices describe different outcomes. Incomes, household arrangements and other circumstances can influence the ability to keep up with bills. Establishing what drove the change in arrears requires evidence following those relationships through time.

Living conditions broaden the picture further. Eurostat reported that 9% of the EU population was unable to keep its home adequately warm in 2025. This measure brings attention to the experience inside the dwelling: whether the household could achieve an essential level of comfort. Its numerical equality with the arrears rate is coincidental in the presentation; the two indicators describe different conditions, and the people covered can overlap.

Warmth also helps explain why a cost share alone gives an incomplete account of housing welfare. Spending and the service obtained from that spending both matter. Assessing a home’s affordability becomes more informative when the financial measure is read alongside the conditions residents experience. The publication supplies separate views of those conditions; the connection between any particular household’s bills, income and warmth requires more detailed evidence.

The household housing cost-share, tenure and living-condition indicators discussed here draw on EU-SILC, the EU statistics on income and living conditions. National statistical collection supplies data under a harmonised annual framework, allowing Eurostat to compare covered populations using common concepts. Its focus is people living in private households. Residents of institutional or collective accommodation generally fall outside that frame, and unsheltered homelessness requires additional ways of gathering evidence. The publication’s separate house price, rent and consumer price indices retain their own measures and periods, including the 2010–2025 changes discussed here; historical household comparisons likewise retain their stated observation years.

The timing also deserves care. The publication appeared in November 2026, while the principal figures discussed here describe 2025. Income reference periods and the timing of housing cost observations can differ. The results provide a structured account of living conditions over defined periods; a household’s circumstances today may have changed.

Taken together, the measures suggest where a closer investigation would be most useful. The income comparison identifies a large difference in the share of resources absorbed by housing. The geographical comparison locates a higher prevalence of overburden in cities across most member states. Tenure helps explain which expenses enter the calculation. Examining these dimensions together could show which residents in a particular place face the greatest pressure and how that pressure develops.

That next step would also sharpen any assessment of policy. A proposal affecting housing supply, household income or energy use would need to be examined against the people it reaches and the outcomes it changes. The 2025 comparisons establish the distribution of several observed conditions. The causes of the gaps, and the effects of particular interventions, remain questions for further evidence.

The 19% average is therefore a useful entrance to Europe’s housing story. Following it into the income groups reveals a 36% share for people below their national threshold; following it across places reveals distinct patterns of financial strain. The next meaningful question is how those circumstances are changing: for which people, in which homes and places, is housing taking a larger or smaller share of the resources available for everyday life?
