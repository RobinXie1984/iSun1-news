# The Wild Strawberry That Grew Twice as Many Berries, and Why NASA Kept Its Favourite

On 25 November 2026, NASA published a report on three plants it is studying as possible fresh foods for astronauts who may one day live on the Moon or Mars: strawberries, cucumbers and lemon basil. The work comes from scientists at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida, who test how crops behave in controlled chambers that imitate selected conditions of spaceflight. For the strawberry comparison, they worked with university partners in Australia. Each crop has its own record of completed steps and its own next step. The most surprising result belongs to the strawberry. In one comparison, a wild strawberry type grown from seed produced twice as many fruits as NASA's favourite variety, and NASA kept the favourite at the top of its list. The reasons behind that choice show what a plant has to offer before it earns a place on a spacecraft.

## Why grow food in space at all

Astronauts today eat food prepared on Earth, sealed in pouches and packets, and sent up on cargo flights. That system serves the International Space Station (ISS) well because supply ships arrive regularly. A journey to Mars would last far longer, and fresh deliveries would be rare or impossible. NASA's background material on space plant research explains that some vitamins in stored food slowly break down over long storage. Fresh produce offers a way to add nutrients, flavour and variety to a menu built around packages.

Plants also do quiet chemical work. Their leaves take in carbon dioxide, the gas people breathe out, and release oxygen. Plants draw water up through their roots and release some of it into the air as vapour, which a well-designed habitat could collect and reuse. NASA mentions these abilities as part of the long-range reason for crop research. The current studies are small plant trials, though. How much of a crew's food, air or water a future space farm could supply remains an open question, and packaged food stays the base of every mission menu NASA has described.

That shifts the real question. On Earth, almost anyone can grow a strawberry in a sunny garden. In space, the question becomes which plant earns its keep inside a small box where light, water, electricity, room and crew time are all limited.

## A garden the size of a suitcase

NASA already has plant-growing equipment on the space station, and it shows how tight those limits are. The Vegetable Production System, nicknamed Veggie, is roughly the size of a carry-on suitcase and typically holds six plants. Rows of LED lights stand in for the Sun. The plants grow from "plant pillows," small bags filled with a clay-based growing material and fertilizer.

The pillows solve a problem most gardeners rarely think about. Roots need water, and they also need air, because root cells use oxygen to stay alive. On Earth, gravity pulls water down through soil, and air fills the spaces it leaves behind. On the space station, everything floats, because the station and everyone inside it are falling around Earth together. Scientists call this condition microgravity. In microgravity, water tends to cling to surfaces and gather into blobs, and bubbles drift wherever they like. Around a plant's roots, that behaviour can leave the root zone too wet, crowding out the air the roots need. The pillows in Veggie are designed to help balance water, nutrients and air around the roots. Watering a plant in orbit is a plumbing problem as much as a gardening task.

Light carries its own engineering cost. LEDs use electricity and give off heat, and a spacecraft has to supply that power and carry the heat away. Every extra plant adds to those demands.

NASA also runs a larger and more automated chamber on the station called the Advanced Plant Habitat. It controls the water, nutrients and oxygen reaching the roots, and it carries many sensors and cameras so that a team on the ground can watch the plants and adjust conditions. That automation reduces routine work for astronauts. Crews have grown and studied a range of plants on the station over the years, and samples have come back to Earth for analysis. This history gives NASA real experience with growing plants in orbit. The strawberry, cucumber and lemon basil in the new report will each need their own evidence from flight hardware.

## The strawberry that did too much

The strawberry study, according to NASA's account of a paper published in September 2026, compared two types of strawberry under conditions set up on Earth to imitate parts of a space habitat. Researchers from Kennedy worked with collaborators at the University of Melbourne and the University of Western Australia.

One was 'Delizz', NASA's leading strawberry candidate, which bears large red fruit. The other was a wild strawberry with small yellow fruit. NASA's account links the yellow colour to a recessive genetic mutation.

Grown from seed, the wild strawberry produced twice as many buds, twice as many flowers and twice as many fruits as 'Delizz', according to NASA. On first reading, that sounds like a clear winner.

The important word is "many." These figures count objects. They measure how many berries appeared, and the wild berries were small. Twice as many small berries could add up to more food, less food or about the same amount as half as many large ones. For a crew, the useful quantity is the amount of edible fruit, which depends on both the number of berries and their size. NASA's report gives the counts and describes the sizes, and those two facts together frame the comparison.

Then there are the runners. A runner is a long stem that a strawberry plant sends sideways along the ground. Where a runner touches soil, it can root and start a new plant, which is how strawberries spread across a garden bed. NASA reports that the wild strawberry produced too many runners for a compact space-growing setup. Those stems would take up valuable room in a cabin where every handful of space has a job. In a space garden, the room a plant fills counts as part of its cost, alongside the light and water it uses.

A plant has a limited budget of energy and building material. It can spend that budget on fruit, on leaves, on roots or on runners. A plant that pours effort into spreading stems can push beyond the edges of its tray, and a crew might then need to cut it back. NASA's account presents the yellow fruit and the heavy runner growth as separate observations about the wild strawberry. One trait would explain the other only if future research showed a link.

So 'Delizz' remained NASA's leading strawberry candidate. The finding is a real step forward, because it shows a trade-off that a simple fruit count would hide. A higher number of berries counts for less when the plant also crowds its neighbours and may call for extra care.

An everyday comparison helps here, as an illustration of the choice. Imagine choosing a plant for a small kitchen window box. One tomato variety gives you lots of tiny tomatoes but sends long vines across the counter. Another gives fewer, bigger tomatoes and stays neatly in its box. Counting tomatoes alone would favour the first plant. A sensible gardener would also weigh how much the tomatoes actually add up to, how much counter space the vines steal, and how often the plant needs trimming and watering. A spacecraft garden asks the same kind of questions, with stricter limits on space, power and safety, plus the strange behaviour of water in orbit.

## A cucumber that keeps on giving

NASA reports that a study published in August 2026 in the journal *HortScience* examined cucumber growth, harvest and nutrition under spaceflight-like conditions. "Spaceflight-like" means that researchers on Earth recreated selected features of a space environment inside a chamber. According to NASA's account, some plants grew enlarged leaves, and the plants produced a steady stream of edible fruit.

That phrase is promising, and it describes the harvest in general terms. For a crew, the timing of a harvest matters alongside its size. A plant that ripens fruit over many weeks gives a kitchen a regular supply of something fresh, while a single large harvest arrives all at once and has to be eaten or stored quickly. NASA's description of a steady stream points toward the first pattern, in qualitative words. At one point NASA's article uses the wording "spaceflight conditions," which could sound like an orbital experiment. The study NASA describes took place in chambers designed to imitate flight conditions, so the cucumber results come from ground testing.

Both the strawberry and cucumber studies reach this article through NASA's account. The two papers returned access errors when checked on 27 November 2026, so the findings described here follow NASA's summaries.

Cucumbers belong to the gourd family, along with pumpkins, melons and squash. NASA presents the cucumber as possibly becoming the first gourd-family crop grown in space. That remains a future possibility.

A wider menu has practical value. Different crops bring different flavours and nutrients, which helps keep meals appealing over months. Variety can also add some resilience, because one crop's poor performance leaves others in place. That protection has limits: plants sharing one chamber also share its equipment, so a broken pump or a plant disease could harm several crops at once.

## A herb that has passed its first checks

Lemon basil has moved through a sequence of preparation steps. NASA reports that it has completed preliminary testing and seed sanitisation. Sanitisation is a preparatory step that reduces microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi on seeds before they travel. NASA requires this step before flight because microbes can spread easily in a closed cabin. It lowers the number of microbes and forms one part of a longer safety process.

The next step, according to NASA, is to see how well lemon basil grows in plant pillows, the same kind of root container used in Veggie. That test will show whether the herb takes to the growing system engineers have built.

The case for a herb is about flavour. Astronauts can tire of eating similar packaged meals day after day, a problem sometimes called menu fatigue. A bright, lemony herb could make familiar dishes taste fresher. The report presents this as the herb's potential appeal. The basil's route shows how a candidate advances step by step: preliminary testing, then seed sanitisation, with growth in plant pillows as its next named step.

## Three places, three kinds of test

The strawberry and cucumber findings NASA reports come from chambers on Earth, and the lemon basil work so far consists of ground preparation. That matters, because each setting answers a different question.

A ground chamber on Earth can control light, temperature, humidity and nutrients very precisely, and it operates under full Earth gravity. On the space station, plants face microgravity, where water and air move around roots in unfamiliar ways. The Moon and Mars each have partial gravity, weaker than Earth's and stronger than the near-weightlessness of orbit. A plant that thrives in a ground chamber gives researchers a good reason to test it in orbit. Each new setting then provides its own evidence.

NASA's planned Ohalo III investigation aims at exactly this gap. NASA describes it as a prototype system intended to confirm reliable water delivery to plant roots in microgravity and to make better use of limited growing space. Its stated goals also include productivity, nutrition, food safety and dependable day-to-day operation. NASA's 25 November report describes Ohalo III as planned for the space station, and the investigation's results lie in the future. NASA also suggests that lessons from the project could help indoor farms on Earth, which also try to grow more food in less space with careful use of water. That benefit remains a hope for now.

Ohalo III connects directly to the strawberry puzzle. A crop's value depends on the system around it. If water reaches every root reliably, and if the space inside the growing chamber is used well, then a compact plant with large fruit can deliver its full potential. If the system struggles, even a fruitful plant may disappoint.

## The question at the heart of the space garden

Taken together, NASA's three candidates mark progress of a careful, practical kind. The strawberry comparison revealed a trade-off that a single impressive number would conceal. The cucumber study, in NASA's account, produced a steady stream of edible fruit under spaceflight-like conditions. Lemon basil has completed preliminary testing and seed sanitisation, with growth in plant pillows as its next step. The reported findings come from ground chambers and preparation work, and each points toward a further test.

The underlying question is easy to state. A crop for space works a bit like a member of the crew. It has to fit in a cramped room, use shared supplies carefully, stay healthy, and give back more than it takes. One useful way to think about it, offered here as an explanation rather than a NASA formula, is to ask how much good food a plant provides for the space it occupies, the time it takes to grow, the electricity its lights consume, and the attention it demands from busy astronauts. The wild strawberry scored high on one count, berries, and raised doubts on space and care. 'Delizz' kept its lead because it looked like the better overall fit for the room available.

Later tests in orbit could examine some of those qualities directly, such as the weight of the harvest, its nutrients, the water and power a crop consumes, the crew time it requires and how reliably the equipment runs. Those measurements would bring the choice into sharper focus. Until then, the strawberry leaves a question for every candidate that follows it. When every handful of space aboard a spacecraft matters, which plant will turn its share of light, water and care into the most useful food?
