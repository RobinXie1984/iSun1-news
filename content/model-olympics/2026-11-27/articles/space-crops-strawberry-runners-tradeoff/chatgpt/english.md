# The Strawberry Produced Twice as Many Fruits. Why Did NASA Keep Its Rival Ahead?

Feeding astronauts begins with dependable meals that can travel with them and remain useful through a mission. Fresh plants could add flavour, texture and nutrients to that stored food, giving future Moon and Mars crews a more varied menu. In a report published on 25 November 2026, NASA described research into three possibilities: strawberries, cucumbers and lemon basil. Researchers are assessing how these crops grow, what they produce and how they might fit inside equipment built for space. Among the findings was a strawberry comparison with an intriguing outcome: the plants that produced twice as many fruits still left another variety leading NASA’s candidate list.

A spacecraft garden has to earn its room. Before a plant supplies anything edible, it occupies growing space and needs light, water and care. Its roots require oxygen as well as moisture. Its leaves and stems expand as it grows. A useful harvest therefore involves a relationship between the food collected and the resources committed over time. For a crew living inside a constrained habitat, the shape and behaviour of a plant can matter alongside the appeal of its fruit.

NASA’s three candidates are following distinct paths through that assessment. Strawberries have supplied a comparison between varieties under simulated space conditions. Cucumbers have produced encouraging results in tests described as spaceflight-like. Lemon basil has undergone preliminary testing and seed sanitisation, with growth in specialised root containers as a next assessment. Each offers a possible contribution to future meals. Their performance in flight and in future surface habitats remains an open research question.

The strawberry result makes the challenge unusually easy to see. According to NASA’s account of a September 2026 paper, researchers at Kennedy Space Center, the University of Melbourne and the University of Western Australia compared seed-grown wild strawberries with a variety called ‘Delizz’. Under the reported simulated conditions, the wild plants produced twice as many buds, flowers and fruits. Their fruit was small and yellow; ‘Delizz’ produced larger, red fruit. NASA nevertheless retained ‘Delizz’ as its leading candidate because the wild plants produced excessive spreading stems called runners.

Imagine choosing plants for a small kitchen shelf. One strawberry plant might carry many little fruits while sending stems into the space beside its pot. Another might hold fewer, larger fruits within a more manageable shape. Counting fruit would answer one useful question. Choosing what belongs on the shelf would also involve how much food each plant supplies, how long it occupies the shelf and how much tending it requires. This is an illustration of the decision facing space-crop researchers, whose growing equipment imposes much tighter demands.

A runner is a stem that extends away from the parent strawberry plant and can form another plant. On Earth, that behaviour helps strawberries spread. Inside a confined growing system, the same growth can consume valuable volume. Leaves, roots, fruit and spreading stems all become parts of the equipment’s space requirement. NASA’s assessment identifies the wild strawberry’s runners as a practical obstacle, even alongside its abundant reproductive growth.

The reported twofold result deserves careful attention because several different measures sit close together in ordinary language. Buds are developing structures, flowers are a stage toward fruit, and fruit counts describe the number produced. Those observations tell researchers something about the plants’ development under the test conditions. The amount of food available to eat also depends on the size and edible mass of those fruits. Here, NASA describes a size difference between the two candidates, making that distinction central to the story.

Twice as many small berries can represent several possible amounts of food relative to a smaller number of large berries. Weighing the harvest establishes that relationship. Relating its edible mass to occupied space and growing time helps assess how effectively a crop uses a chamber. NASA’s reported comparison establishes the count advantage and identifies the growth habit that kept ‘Delizz’ ahead in the agency’s selection; its conclusion remains a candidate assessment.

The wild berries’ yellow colour has a separate explanation. NASA attributes it to a recessive genetic mutation, an inherited change affecting their appearance. The reported evidence connects that mutation to colour. Runner growth is the distinct trait at the centre of the space constraint. Keeping these observations separate preserves the actual lesson of the comparison: a crop can have appealing characteristics while its overall growth pattern makes it harder to accommodate.

Access for this feature was limited to NASA’s summaries of the September strawberry paper and the August cucumber paper discussed below; independent checks of their full methods and results remain pending. The strawberry evidence supports continued interest in ‘Delizz’ and identifies a difficulty with the tested wild plants. Comparing edible harvests against the room each plant occupies would further clarify the practical value of that choice.

The hardware helps explain why that fuller comparison matters. NASA’s established Vegetable Production System, usually called Veggie, is roughly the size of carry-on luggage and typically holds six plants. Used for research aboard the International Space Station, it supplies light through light-emitting diodes, or LEDs. The roots grow inside plant pillows: containers holding a clay-based growing medium and fertiliser. These pillows help deliver water and nutrients while preserving access to air around the roots.

The word “pillow” makes the equipment sound simple, yet it addresses a demanding problem. A root needs moisture to obtain water and dissolved nutrients, and it also needs oxygen. Excess water can occupy spaces that would otherwise contain air, leaving roots short of oxygen. Healthy growth therefore depends on how water is distributed through the root area, as well as how much arrives there.

On the space station, that distribution occurs in microgravity, the near-weightless conditions of orbit. Water and air bubbles behave differently there, complicating the balance around roots. A growing system has to deliver moisture reliably while keeping oxygen available. The success of a plant on Earth provides useful evidence about its potential; orbital testing examines how the plant and its equipment work together under this additional physical constraint.

A future Moon habitat introduces another setting. The Moon has partial gravity, weaker than Earth’s, while the space station provides orbital microgravity. Mars would bring its own surface conditions and habitat requirements. An Earth chamber can reproduce selected features, such as controlled lighting, while each destination still calls for evidence relevant to its environment. Moving a crop through these stages gradually connects promising biology with the conditions in which a crew would actually grow it.

That distinction is especially important for the cucumber results. NASA’s November report describes an August 2026 study in *HortScience* that assessed cucumber growth, yield and nutrition under spaceflight-like conditions. In the agency’s account, researchers observed some enlarged leaves and a steady supply of edible fruit. These findings suggest a candidate worth pursuing, with the possibility of cucumbers eventually becoming a first crop from the gourd family grown in space.

The evidence described here concerns the controlled tests. An orbital cucumber harvest remains a future possibility in NASA’s account. The reported steady supply is a qualitative description of production. Harvest records that connect quantities with dates and growing space would help translate that pattern into a contribution to the menu.

Even at that stage, the timing of harvests raises a useful issue. A crew experiences fresh food as portions arriving over days and weeks. A crop producing successive edible fruits could contribute differently to meals from one gathered in a single harvest. Evaluating that contribution would involve both the amount collected and its timing. This is one reason researchers examine growth and yield together: the plant’s development determines when growing space begins to supply food and how that supply continues.

Lemon basil presents a different kind of value. A herb can add flavour to packaged meals through a comparatively small serving. Its possible role belongs within a larger menu that continues to depend on stored food. NASA reports preliminary testing and completed seed sanitisation, a preflight step intended to remove microorganisms. The next described assessment concerns how the plants grow in plant pillows.

For basil, sanitising seeds prepares them for further work; growth in the hardware and later food-safety decisions have their own questions to resolve. The herb’s culinary promise is straightforward, while its performance in space remains to be established. NASA’s account supplies a prospective ingredient for future meals, with further testing between the prepared seed and a crew’s plate.

The three crops thus illuminate different parts of selection. Strawberries expose the importance of plant shape and harvest measurement. Cucumbers bring attention to the possibility of continuing fruit production. Basil shows how flavour can be a useful contribution in its own right. Their value would depend on actual performance within the growing system and on the role each serves in the menu.

Crew attention also belongs in that assessment. Water delivery, plant management and harvests can create work, and spreading growth may add to the care required. The strawberry comparison identifies a reason to study that potential workload, whose scale remains to be measured. A candidate that looks attractive in a chamber still has to fit the practical rhythm of a mission, where tending crops shares time with other tasks.

NASA’s Advanced Plant Habitat explores a more automated approach to plant research than Veggie. It uses LEDs, controlled delivery of water and nutrients, and sensors and cameras that support monitoring by teams on Earth. Automation can reduce routine intervention and help researchers follow changes inside the chamber. Reliability remains something to demonstrate through operation, including how the system responds when conditions drift away from what the plants need.

The next hardware questions also appear in Ohalo III, a prototype investigation described by NASA. Its stated aims include validating water delivery and making effective use of growing volume in microgravity. It also addresses productivity, nutrition, safety and operational reliability. NASA’s 25 November report described the prototype as set for launch to the International Space Station, placing orbital validation ahead in the reported programme.

Those objectives connect directly to the strawberry puzzle. Growing volume includes the plant’s changing shape, the room around it and the equipment supporting its roots. Reliable water delivery determines whether that allocated space can keep producing healthy growth. Food safety and nutrition determine the usefulness of the harvest. A prototype can help test those relationships together; its intended benefits remain prospective until results establish them.

The larger ambition extends beyond an occasional fresh ingredient. Plants absorb carbon dioxide, release oxygen and take part in water recycling, giving them possible roles within future habitat systems. The small crop trials described here establish a much narrower scope of evidence: candidate selection and steps toward workable cultivation. A self-sufficient supply of food, air and water would require a demonstrated balance between what a whole growing system delivers and what a crew consumes. Packaged food continues to underpin the menu being considered.

For now, the most revealing result is a choice shaped by a plant’s excess growth. The wild strawberry supplied twice as many buds, flowers and fruits under the reported conditions, while its runners used space that researchers valued. ‘Delizz’ remained ahead. As crop tests move into hardware and flight, the question becomes more tangible: how much useful food can each plant reliably bring to the table, within the room, resources and attention a crew can spare?
