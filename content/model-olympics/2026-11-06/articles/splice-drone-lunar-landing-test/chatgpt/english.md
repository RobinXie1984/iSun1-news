# NASA’s Drone Changed Its Landing Target. Could the Same Decision Carry a Spacecraft to the Moon?

A spacecraft approaching the Moon has two closely connected jobs: reach a place worth exploring and find ground suitable for touchdown. NASA, the United States space and aeronautics agency, is developing sensors and software to help vehicles make those decisions during descent. On August 27, 2026, an Alta-X drone carrying NASA’s SPLICE navigation payload rehearsed part of that task over Rogers Dry Lake in California. According to NASA’s November 4 account, the payload assessed the terrain and redirected the drone after judging its intended landing area unsafe. The Earth flight connected an observation of the ground to a change in destination.

That connection matters because scientific interest and landing convenience can pull a mission toward different places. NASA describes future access to terrain near boulders, slopes and shadowed craters as part of SPLICE’s purpose. A spacecraft landing close to a feature of interest could put instruments or explorers nearer their work. Reaching such terrain requires a chain of decisions: locating the vehicle, measuring its movement, inspecting the surface and determining which suitable patch remains within reach.

SPLICE stands for Safe and Precise Landing–Integrated Capabilities Evolution. It brings together instruments, onboard computing and software for guidance and navigation. Navigation establishes where a vehicle is and how it is moving. Guidance calculates the path it should follow. Hazard assessment adds another essential piece: whether the ground at the end of that path appears suitable for landing. NASA’s California test provides a reported example of those functions working together far enough to change a flying vehicle’s target.

The question that follows is practical. How much does an Earth drone’s successful change of target tell us about the decisions a spacecraft could make during a lunar descent?

A familiar parking situation offers an illustrative analogy. A driver uses a road map to reach the correct street, looks along the curb for a suitable space, then judges whether the car can turn into it. Each step uses different information. The street address establishes the general destination. A fresh view reveals conditions at the parking space. The car’s position and turning ability determine whether that space is reachable. A lander faces a related division of work during descent, with its available time and maneuvering capacity governed by flight conditions.

NASA’s broader SPLICE architecture begins one part of this process with Terrain Relative Navigation. A camera takes pictures of the surface below, and software compares visible features with stored images collected from orbit. Recognizing the same features in both sets of images helps establish the vehicle’s location. In everyday terms, the system matches landmarks outside its window to landmarks on its map. That information can help guide a spacecraft toward a desired scientific region and around known geographic features.

Knowing the region still leaves a finer question about the particular patch beneath the vehicle. A local scan can reveal rocks and slopes relevant to touchdown. SPLICE’s Hazard Detection Lidar addresses that task. Lidar uses laser light reflected from a surface to measure it. This instrument scans the ground and builds a three-dimensional representation of its shape. The resulting digital elevation map is a computer model showing how the height of the ground varies from place to place.

Hazard software examines that height model to identify risky areas and candidate landing sites. The distinction between location and local suitability is central to the design. A vehicle could recognize that it has reached the intended region while discovering that its selected touchdown point contains a hazard. The stored map and the fresh scan therefore answer different questions about the same destination. SPLICE also includes an approach called Active Terrain Relative Navigation, which extracts recognizable three-dimensional features from elevation maps to help establish position and build a continuing picture of the terrain.

Movement requires its own measurements. NASA’s Navigation Doppler Lidar sends laser beams toward the surface and analyzes the returning light to estimate velocity and altitude: how fast the vehicle is moving and how high it is above the ground. Those estimates help guidance software understand the vehicle’s present situation. A promising landing patch has practical value only when the descending vehicle can reach it within its operating limits.

NASA calls its trajectory-planning approach Dual Quaternion Guidance. The name refers to the mathematical method; its relevant purpose here is calculating a path toward the landing site while respecting vehicle and environmental constraints. The program describes minimizing fuel use as a design goal. The calculation also accounts for where sensors must point so they can continue observing the terrain. Redirecting a vehicle therefore involves both choosing a destination and preserving the ability to gather the information needed along the way.

The descent and landing computer processes sensor information and runs navigation, guidance and hazard-assessment calculations. Taken together, these functions describe a system in which measurements can influence the next action. NASA’s program page explains this broader architecture; the precise combination of components carried on August 27 remains unspecified in the flight account. Its description supports an understanding of how SPLICE technologies are intended to cooperate, while the drone report establishes the particular action NASA says occurred.

The program’s illustrative lunar sequence gives a sense of how those tasks could unfold during descent. It describes the motion-measuring lidar activating at about 10 kilometres above the surface. At about 500 metres, the hazard-detection lidar scans the landing area and generates a terrain model. The computer then considers candidate touchdown locations within a circle approximately 100 metres across. Those distances belong to NASA’s example of lunar operation. The 100-metre figure describes the diameter of the search area; landing accuracy measures a different quantity, the distance between an intended touchdown point and the actual one.

For the California drone flight, altitude, duration and landing error remain unspecified in NASA’s published account. Keeping the illustrative sequence separate from the flight result preserves the meaning of both. The lunar example explains a proposed arrangement of sensing and decision-making. The August 27 report describes a terrain assessment that led to a diversion during simulated lunar descent and landing maneuvers on Earth.

The work that enabled that flight began with integration between teams and equipment. NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston developed the payload. Specialists at Armstrong Flight Research Center in Edwards, California, worked with the Johnson team to integrate, test and fly it aboard the Alta-X during June and July 2026. NASA says those activities checked communications, data handling, telemetry and command and control. Telemetry is information sent from the flying vehicle to the team monitoring it.

Those checks concern the connections through which a navigation system becomes useful. Sensor information must reach the computing system, and the resulting commands must reach the vehicle in a usable form. The July 15 photographs accompanying NASA’s article show earlier hover and communications work. They document preparation within the campaign. August 27 is the date NASA assigns to the Rogers Dry Lake descent and landing maneuvers.

During that reported test, the payload generated terrain maps from light-detection and other sensor data, assessed possible landing areas and redirected the drone when its target was judged unsafe. The useful result lies in the connection between sensing and action. A terrain map can describe a hazard; a functioning guidance chain can use that description to change where a vehicle goes. NASA reports that the drone demonstration exercised this connected behavior.

The scale and consistency of that performance remain open. The account supplies a description of the achieved action, with quantitative reliability still undetermined. A success rate would require both a count of successful outcomes and the total number of attempts. Assessing hazard detection would also require a record of the actual hazards and the system’s responses to them. Landing error would show how closely the vehicle reached its selected destination. NASA’s article leaves those measures unspecified, so its evidence supports a demonstrated capability within the reported test rather than a numerical estimate of dependable performance.

That distinction becomes especially useful when considering what a system means by a suitable landing area. The software makes its assessment from sensor observations and the rules used to evaluate them. A physical patch of ground also has properties that matter to the particular vehicle arriving there. As an engineering inference, judging the quality of a diversion requires examining the observation, the site-selection decision and the resulting flight together. Each part helps explain whether the system chose a reachable place that met the vehicle’s landing requirements.

The August result sits within a longer development history. NASA says SPLICE was introduced in October 2017, with related technologies developed through earlier projects. Its program page lists precursor sensor testing aboard the Morpheus rocket in 2014, further testing involving the Xodiac rocket in 2017, and navigation work in 2019. In March 2025, a helicopter carried hazard-related components over a hazard field at Kennedy Space Center. These entries show technologies being exercised through several kinds of flight and test platform over time. They describe a development lineage whose hardware and software configurations evolved.

NASA project manager Davis Adams characterizes the drone flight as the culmination of nearly a decade of work and says it increased readiness for integration into future robotic and crewed lunar flights. That is the project’s assessment of progress. The November article leaves the numerical technology-readiness level and the criteria behind that assessment unspecified. Both public accounts come from NASA, so the evidence presented here carries agency attribution; independent replication remains unestablished in these sources.

The published chronology also contains an unresolved junction. NASA’s November 4 article describes the June and July preparations as leading toward a full-scale Voyager Technologies test scheduled for later in the publication month. Separately, the SPLICE program page lists an August 2026 drone test at Voyager’s Lunar Surface Proving Grounds in Mojave, followed by project closeout in September 2026. It describes that August entry as a closed-loop test, meaning measurements fed back into the system’s guidance of the vehicle.

The August 27 Rogers Dry Lake event and the August Voyager milestone therefore remain separate entries in the public record. How the November future-tense reference aligns with the August test and September closeout is unresolved. The status of any further full-scale test at the time of publication is unknown. NASA’s clarification would establish the sequence and help readers understand how the reported flight fits into the project’s concluding work.

The path toward wider use also carries distinct stages. NASA says it has worked with commercial lunar lander providers on versions of SPLICE technologies and on data gathered during development. Adams reports industry interest in navigation applications and possible uses for autonomous movement across a surface. The November 4 account describes guidance software availability through NASA’s software catalog as planned for fall 2026. Release status and licensing remain unverified in the two public accounts considered here. Collaboration, evaluation and a planned software release each indicate activity at a different point between research and operational use.

Moving from the California test toward a lunar mission would bring a different vehicle into a different physical setting. Earth’s atmosphere and gravity shape drone flight; a lunar spacecraft descends under lunar conditions using its own propulsion and control systems. Mars introduces another environment again. These differences define the next engineering questions. Mission-specific evidence would show how sensors, computing and guidance perform together aboard the vehicle that must actually land.

The reported achievement is concrete: NASA says a flying test system used terrain information to redirect itself from a landing area it assessed as unsafe. Its significance comes from joining observation to a consequential action during descent. The remaining suspense concerns how reliably that chain will hold as the vehicle, terrain and environment change. When a future spacecraft identifies a better patch of ground below, what measured evidence will show that it can turn that choice into a dependable touchdown?
