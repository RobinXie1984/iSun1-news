# A Drone Rejected Its Landing Spot Over a California Dry Lake. What Would It Take to Trust That Choice on the Moon?

On August 27, 2026, a large multi-rotor drone called an Alta-X flew over Rogers Dry Lake, a wide, flat desert basin beside NASA's Armstrong Flight Research Center in Edwards, California, and acted out the final stage of a Moon landing. It carried a guidance, navigation and control package from a NASA program called SPLICE, short for Safe and Precise Landing – Integrated Capabilities Evolution. Engineers at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston built the package. Flight specialists at Armstrong, the agency's flight-research center in the Mojave Desert, mounted it on the drone and flew it. NASA's account, published by Armstrong on November 4, says the package mapped the ground below, judged possible landing areas, and steered the drone to a different spot when its intended landing area was judged unsafe. The maneuvers were simulated descents and landings on Earth. The flight still touched on a question that every lunar lander faces in its last moments. How does a machine recognize that its chosen patch of ground is a poor place to touch down, and how does it reach a better one while it still has time and fuel to act?

## Why a lander needs to change its mind

Landing on the Moon has always involved a tug-of-war between where scientists want to go and where a spacecraft can safely settle. The most interesting ground often sits beside the most awkward ground: fields of boulders, steep crater walls, slopes that could tip a lander over. During the Apollo missions, astronauts trained to recognize particular lunar features, so they could work out their position by looking out the window and matching what they saw to what they had studied. NASA's SPLICE program page describes an effort to hand that job, and a close inspection of the ground beneath the lander, to sensors and software aboard the spacecraft.

According to that page, future missions could use SPLICE tools to aim for places Apollo crews had to avoid. Its examples are regions with hazardous boulders and areas near shadowed craters. The page also says NASA will consider SPLICE technologies for Artemis crewed landings and for future Mars sample return missions, and that they could help land humans on Mars as early as the 2030s. Each of these is a stated prospect. The page describes intended benefits, and the August flight sits at one step along that path.

The program has a long history. The SPLICE page says it was introduced in October 2017, and it builds on older NASA work, including an earlier project called ALHAT, the Autonomous Precision Landing and Hazard Avoidance Technology project. Davis Adams, the SPLICE project manager, called the August flight the culmination of almost ten years of work in NASA's November 4 account.

## Four questions on the way down

A landing spacecraft has to answer several separate questions, quickly and in order, as it descends. The first is *Where am I?* The second is *How fast am I moving, and how high am I?* The third is *Is the patch of ground I am aiming for actually safe?* The fourth, if the answer to the third is discouraging, is *Can I steer somewhere better within the limits of my fuel, my engine and my sensors?* A wrong answer to any one of these can undo correct answers to the others.

**An everyday comparison, offered purely as illustration:** imagine driving to an unfamiliar address at night. A street map tells you which block you are on. Your speedometer and the passing streetlights tell you how fast you are closing in. When you reach the address, your headlights reveal a deep pothole in the parking space you had planned to use. Now you need enough room and enough time to swing into the next space over. The map, the speedometer, your eyes and your steering each handle a different part of the problem, and the arrival goes well only when all four work together. A Moon landing is far harder than parking a car. The comparison simply shows why knowing your location, sensing your motion, seeing the ground up close and steering to a new spot are separate tasks.

NASA's program page links specific pieces of SPLICE equipment to these questions. Two terms come up repeatedly. **Lidar** works like radar with light: an instrument fires laser pulses at a surface and times the reflections to measure distance. A **digital elevation map** is a grid of height measurements, so that each small square of ground has a number saying how high it sits. Picture a sheet of graph paper with an altitude written in every box.

For *Where am I?*, the page describes Terrain Relative Navigation. A camera photographs the surface during descent, and software compares those live pictures with maps assembled earlier from images taken by spacecraft orbiting the Moon. By tracking craters and other features in the photos and finding them on the stored map, the onboard computer works out the spacecraft's position. This is the automated version of the Apollo window-gazing. The page says it lets a lander steer around large known features such as hills and craters, and land close to scientifically interesting sites instead of far away, which would leave a rover a long drive.

An orbital map has a natural boundary, though. It shows the landscape as it looked from high above, at the detail that orbiting cameras captured. A rock big enough to damage a lander, or a short steep slope, can be too small to appear clearly on that map. This is where the third question comes in, and it needs fresh measurements taken during the descent.

For *How fast and how high?*, the page describes Navigation Doppler Lidar. The instrument sends laser beams to the surface and measures the returning light. A small shift in the returning light reveals speed, much as the pitch of a passing siren reveals the speed of an ambulance. NASA says this instrument gives considerably more precise measurements than the radar sensors used on earlier landers, in a smaller, lighter, lower-power package.

For *Is this spot safe?*, the page describes Hazard Detection Lidar, a laser-based three-dimensional imager that scans the landing field and builds a detailed terrain model from very large numbers of laser pulses. Hazard Detection Software then reads the resulting elevation map, marks regions to avoid, such as large rocks and steep slopes, and picks out safer candidates. A companion system, Active Terrain Relative Navigation, uses the same elevation maps in a second way. It picks out distinctive three-dimensional features to help fix the spacecraft's position, and it stitches successive scans into one continuous high-resolution map to support the choice of a landing site.

For *Can I get there?*, the page describes Dual Quaternion Guidance. A quaternion is a mathematical tool for describing rotation and orientation. In plain terms, this guidance system plans a flight path that uses as little fuel as possible while obeying the vehicle's physical limits and keeping the right sensors pointed at the landing area. According to the page, it can also redirect the vehicle to a newly chosen site once the hazard software selects one. Saving fuel is a stated design goal. NASA's account of the August drone flight reports the diversion and leaves fuel figures out.

Coordinating all of this is the Descent and Landing Computer, a multicore processor that NASA's page describes as the system's brain. It runs the sensors and processes their data for navigation, guidance and hazard detection. The page adds that NASA plans to replace its commercial processor chip with a more advanced one called the High-Performance Space Computing processor, which the agency is developing.

## A sample landing, on paper

The program page walks through how these parts would work together during an example lunar landing. As the spacecraft descends from orbit and fires its engine, the camera-based navigation tracks surface features. At about 10 kilometers above the surface, roughly six miles, the Doppler lidar switches on and begins measuring speed and altitude. Near the end, the lander turns upright for touchdown. At about 500 meters, around 1,640 feet, the hazard lidar scans the landing field, and the computer looks for the safest touchdown point within a circle about 100 meters across, about 330 feet.

These figures describe an example architecture for a Moon landing. They are planning numbers that show the scale of the problem: in NASA's illustrative lunar sequence, the final safe-landing search begins about 500 meters up and covers an area roughly the size of a large sports field. They come from NASA's description of how the system is meant to operate. They are separate from the August drone flight, whose altitudes NASA's account leaves unstated, and they make up a design example as opposed to a demonstrated accuracy.

There is a second distinction to keep in view. The program page lists the full family of SPLICE components. The November 4 account describes the drone's payload in terms of what it did: it mapped terrain, built high-resolution elevation maps from light-detection and other sensor data, assessed candidate areas and diverted. It leaves out a complete list of the hardware and software aboard the Alta-X that day. Which of the named components flew on August 27 therefore remains open in the public record.

## How the test came together

NASA's account describes a buildup over the summer. In June and July 2026, Armstrong specialists integrated the Johnson-built payload with the Alta-X and flew it, checking communications, data handling, telemetry (the stream of measurements a vehicle sends back to the ground) and command-and-control links between the payload and the drone. Photographs dated July 15 show Adams and two pilots, Justin Link and chief pilot Justin Hall, working with the system. NASA's captions say the payload communicated successfully with the remotely piloted drone during hover maneuvers that day. That July session was earlier preparation. The simulated descent and diversion came on August 27.

That flight joins a long line of tests. NASA's program page lists precursor SPLICE sensors from the ALHAT project flying aboard NASA's Project Morpheus rocket in 2014, and a precursor Doppler lidar flying on Masten Space Systems' Xodiac suborbital rocket in 2017. In June 2019, rocket-sled runs in Kern County, California, put the Doppler lidar through high-speed testing, and NASA says those runs demonstrated accurate high-velocity measurements. In September 2019, the camera-based Terrain Relative Navigation system, developed by the engineering firm Draper, flew on an Xodiac launch and landing. The November 4 account adds that NASA's Flight Opportunities project supported early testing, including a 2019 flight that followed a descent altitude and landing path similar to a lunar mission's, and a 2020 demonstration of two NASA-developed precision-landing sensor systems. In March 2025, according to the program page, a helicopter carried the hazard lidar, the landing computer and the hazard software over a test hazard field at NASA's Kennedy Space Center.

The record holds one puzzle worth stating plainly. The November 4 account says the June and July checks prepared the payload for a full-scale test at Voyager Technologies in Mojave, California, set for later in the same month, timing that reads oddly in an article dated November 4. The program page, meanwhile, lists an August 2026 closed-loop drone flight test at Voyager Technologies' Lunar Surface Proving Grounds in Mojave to exercise the guidance and active terrain-navigation systems, followed by project closeout in September 2026. "Closed-loop" means the system's own decisions steered the vehicle, as opposed to simply recording data. The two NASA pages give differently dated references, and how they line up is currently unresolved. The August 27 Rogers Dry Lake flight and the August Voyager milestone appear as separate entries in the record, and the identity of the full-scale test the November article anticipated remains an open question for NASA.

## What the flight shows, and where it stops

The achievement NASA reports is concrete. A payload sensed the terrain below a moving vehicle, judged that the planned landing area was unsafe, and guided the vehicle to an alternative. That links the third and fourth questions, seeing the hazard and acting on it, in a single flight. The connection matters, because a lander that spots a boulder benefits from the sighting only when it also has a workable path away from it.

Adams said the flight raised the technology's readiness level, NASA's scale for how mature a technology is, and prepared it for integration into future robotic and crewed lunar flights. He added that the validated guidance and navigation systems apply to landings on Earth, the Moon and Mars. The account gives his assessment and leaves the specific readiness number unstated. NASA also says it has worked with several commercial lunar lander companies to build versions of SPLICE into their vehicles, and that some landers planned for upcoming Moon Base missions draw on data NASA gathered while developing systems like SPLICE. Adams described coordinating with industry on using the guidance system once it appears in NASA's software catalog, which he said was planned for fall 2026, and said some companies were evaluating its navigation for precision landing and possible use in self-driving surface vehicles. These are plans and evaluations as NASA described them on November 4.

The evidence also has a defined boundary. Both accounts come from NASA itself. The August flight appears in them as a qualitative result: the system sensed the ground, judged its target unsafe and diverted. The published pages present that result in descriptive form, alongside a sample landing sequence and a list of milestones. Measuring how reliably the system makes such choices calls for a detailed test report with counted outcomes and landing accuracy, and that report is the next document to seek from NASA.

The setting matters as well. A drone over a California dry lake flies through air, under Earth's gravity, with spinning rotors that can hover and drift at will. A lunar lander descends through vacuum, under surface gravity that NASA's Moon facts page puts at one-sixth of Earth's, on a rocket engine with a fixed fuel supply, over gray dust that reflects sunlight in its own way. These are differences in the physical environment. They describe why the Rogers Dry Lake result is one rung on a ladder, a real demonstration of the logic of sensing and diverting, with the full lunar conditions still ahead.

So the story returns to its opening question, now sharper. NASA has shown a drone choosing a different landing spot when its first choice looked dangerous. For a particular lander heading to a particular stretch of the Moon, with its own engine, fuel margin and lighting, what testing would show that its sensors catch the hazards that matter at that site, and that its guidance can carry it to a safe alternative in the time that remains?
