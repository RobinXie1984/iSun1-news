# Four Rings, One Open Question: Can an Ebola Outbreak Trial Prove a Vaccine Protects Against Bundibugyo?

Since May 2026, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) has faced an outbreak of Ebola disease caused by a virus called Bundibugyo. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) is the European Union's public health agency and follows outbreaks beyond Europe's borders. On its page updated 5 November, ECDC reported that the DRC had recorded 10,668 confirmed cases, including 5,198 related deaths, as of 4 November, using data up to 3 November. On 4 November, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported a recommendation to test two Bundibugyo-specific vaccines in a trial inside the outbreak. Those two reports frame a hard problem. Families and health workers need protection now, and the evidence about whether a vaccine actually guards against this particular virus is still being gathered in the middle of the emergency.

Understanding why that evidence matters so much starts with the name itself.

## One name, several viruses

In everyday conversation, "Ebola" sounds like a single illness. Scientists use the word for a group. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) explains that Ebola disease comes from a family of related viruses called orthoebolaviruses. According to CDC, these viruses were discovered in 1976 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and are found mainly in sub-Saharan Africa. CDC lists four types that make people sick. The best known is Ebola virus itself, which belongs to a species scientists call Zaire. The other three are Sudan virus, Taï Forest virus and Bundibugyo virus. Each causes its own named disease, so the illness spreading in the DRC this year is Bundibugyo virus disease.

The distinction becomes very practical once vaccines and medicines enter the picture. CDC's background page, last updated 2 June 2026, says the US Food and Drug Administration approved a vaccine called Ervebo for preventing disease caused by the Zaire species only. The same page lists two approved treatments, Inmazeb and Ebanga, whose approvals also cover the Zaire species only. Those approvals rest on evidence gathered against Zaire. Whether the same tools help against Bundibugyo is a separate question, and it requires its own evidence.

An everyday comparison can make that logic clearer; it is an illustration of how evidence works. Imagine a key that has been tested many times and reliably opens the front door of one house. Down the street stands a house built by the same company, with a door that looks very similar. The working key gives you a good reason to walk over and try the second door. The real test happens at that door: turning the key in that lock is what tells you whether it opens. Vaccine evidence behaves in a comparable way. A vaccine proven against one virus earns a serious trial against a related virus, and the answer comes from studying people exposed to that related virus. The picture describes how proof carries from one situation to another. The molecular details of how these vaccines train the body's defences are a separate scientific subject.

## What the illness does and how it travels

CDC's general background helps explain why the outbreak is so demanding to control. Symptoms usually appear between 2 and 21 days after exposure, with an average of 8 to 10 days. That long, variable window means a person can feel well for more than a week after contact with the virus and then become ill. At first, people tend to have fever, aches, pains and tiredness. As the illness worsens, it typically brings vomiting and diarrhoea, and sometimes unexplained bleeding. Bleeding appears in some cases and is one possible sign among several.

The virus spreads through direct contact with the body fluids of an infected person who is sick or who has died, or through objects contaminated by those fluids, such as bedding, clothing or medical equipment. CDC says a person becomes contagious once symptoms begin. CDC identifies health workers and family members who care for patients in the absence of proper infection control as the people at highest risk. Its prevention guidance also lists contact with the body of someone who died of the disease, for instance during funeral or burial practices. In other words, the virus moves along the most caring and intimate human connections: nursing a sick relative, treating a patient, preparing a loved one for burial.

Testing and care follow from that biology. CDC explains that doctors diagnose current Ebola disease with a PCR blood test, a laboratory method that detects the virus's genetic material, and that a separate antibody blood test can show whether someone recently had the disease. CDC recommends separating a person being tested from other people in a health facility until results are confirmed. For treatment, CDC describes supportive care as fluids and electrolytes, which are body salts, given by mouth or into a vein; medicine to support blood pressure, reduce vomiting and diarrhoea and manage fever and pain; and treatment for other infections if they appear. CDC says patients have a much better chance of surviving when they receive this care. Supportive care is available now, while specialised vaccines and drugs for Bundibugyo remain under study.

## Counting an outbreak

ECDC's figures are reported surveillance totals: counts of confirmed cases and related deaths that health authorities have recorded, each tied to a date. The DRC numbers carry a precise cutoff, data up to 3 November, reported as of 4 November. ECDC says community transmission is ongoing in the DRC, meaning the virus is passing between people within communities there. Since the outbreak began, eight provinces have been affected: Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu, Tshopo, Haut-Uélé, Bas-Uélé, Sud-Ubangi and Nord-Ubangi.

Dividing the reported deaths by the reported confirmed cases gives about 49 related deaths for every 100 confirmed cases. That arithmetic describes the totals on the page. It covers confirmed cases, so any infections that escaped testing sit outside it. It also differs from any single patient's outlook, which depends on how early care begins and many other circumstances. Vaccine performance is yet another question, and these totals carry no measurement of it. Even with those limits in view, the scale is stark: in data up to 3 November, the DRC had reported 5,198 related deaths since the outbreak began in May.

The outbreak has also crossed borders, and each country's figures carry their own date. ECDC reports that Kenya had nine confirmed cases, including two deaths, as of 26 October, all linked to Kenya's first case. Kenya's most recent case was reported on 20 October and its second death on 23 October. Nigeria reported a case on 20 October in a person with travel history to Uganda; that patient was later medically evacuated to Switzerland. As of 3 November, Nigerian authorities had identified 62 contacts of the case and were following them. ECDC also notes that cases occurred in Uganda, where that outbreak has since ended. These snapshots come from different days, so adding them into one regional total would mix measurements taken at different moments.

The 62 Nigerian contacts deserve a plain explanation. A contact is someone who may have been exposed to a confirmed patient, for example by caring for them or sharing a home. Following up means checking on each person's health over the period when symptoms could appear. Contacts are people being watched; they become cases only if testing confirms infection. This work is called contact tracing: identifying who may have been exposed and following each link in the chain so that any new illness is spotted early and the person can be isolated and cared for. ECDC reports that since 26 October, three of its experts have been deployed to Nigeria to advise and support the EU Delegation in Abuja, and that support has been offered to Nigerian authorities for surveillance, contact tracing, infection prevention and control, and preparedness. These are reported actions; ECDC's page gives them as activities, with their effects still to be measured.

Europe has seen a different side of the outbreak. ECDC says that since May, four cases linked to the DRC outbreak have been medically evacuated or imported into the EU and European Economic Area (EU/EEA). Three patients were evacuated for specialised care, two to Germany and one to the Netherlands, and a fourth case was reported in France in July. All four involved people working in healthcare or humanitarian roles in affected areas of the DRC. The Nigerian patient's transfer to Switzerland falls outside that count, because Switzerland lies outside the EU/EEA.

ECDC's risk assessment addresses specific groups of people. It estimates the likelihood of infection as low for EU/EEA residents living in or travelling to affected areas, and as very low for people living in the EU/EEA, given the very low likelihood of the virus being imported and then spreading onward. ECDC states that the assessment reflects uncertainties about the outbreak and will be reviewed as new information arrives. A severe local burden in the DRC and a very low risk for people living in Europe can both be accurate at once. A patient medically transferred for specialist care is a distinct event from the virus moving freely through a community. The European cases also underline who carries the danger: the people standing closest to patients.

## Inside the trial

That brings the story back to the research. WHO reported on 4 November that an expert group recommended adding two candidates to Tumaini, an ongoing DRC trial: ChAdOx1, from Oxford and the Serum Institute of India, and Moderna's mRNA-1469. Their entry depends on national approvals. WHO says they would be the first Bundibugyo-designed vaccines evaluated during an outbreak, judging earlier laboratory and human data sufficient. The DRC's National Institute for Biomedical Research (INRB) leads Tumaini with the University of Antwerp and partners, and the trial was already testing Ervebo against Bundibugyo. It enrolls contacts of confirmed cases in rings; four rings were in place on 4 November. WHO also describes PARTNERS, a survival-focused treatment trial launched in July, and a post-exposure drug study. It forecasts treatment findings within months and vaccine results in early 2027.

So far the record shows a recommendation. When participants receive the new candidates remains to be announced, and protection against Bundibugyo is the question the trial exists to answer.

The ring idea connects directly to the contact tracing described above. The people closest to a confirmed patient are the people most likely to meet the virus next. The tracing work that keeps an outbreak in check therefore also shows researchers where an experimental vaccine can be offered and studied.

Four rings describe how the trial is organised at one moment. They are a count of groups, which is different from a count of participants, cases or protected people. Measuring protection requires human outcome data: researchers have to compare how often people who received a vaccine fall ill against an appropriate comparison and check carefully for side effects. The public material released so far outlines the ring design in broad terms. Participant numbers, the comparison method, dosing and safety findings belong to the trial's protocol and its eventual reports.

The research effort also divides into distinct questions, and keeping them apart helps the whole picture make sense. A vaccine aims to prevent illness before it starts. A drug given after a known exposure aims to stop infection from turning into disease. A treatment given to a sick patient aims to improve survival. Contact tracing, infection control, isolation and supportive care address the outbreak today, using tools that are already in hand. Each question has its own evidence, timeline and possible answer, and progress on one leaves the others to be settled on their own terms.

This is where the key and the door return. Ervebo's record against Zaire gave researchers a strong reason to try it against Bundibugyo, and the newer candidates carry a different kind of promise. In every case, the answer depends on what happens to real people exposed to this virus, recorded carefully enough that the result can be trusted.

The outbreak keeps its own schedule meanwhile. With more than ten thousand confirmed cases in the DRC by early November, new exposure chains keep forming while trial evidence accumulates on its forecast timeline. Responders have to trace contacts, protect health workers and treat patients today, and researchers have to collect evidence clean enough to guide the next outbreak as well as this one. Both tasks run on the same networks of people, the same clinics and the same limited time.

Can a trial carried out inside a fast-moving outbreak gather evidence strong enough to establish whether a vaccine truly protects against Bundibugyo, while the response is still racing to reach today's patients and the people around them?
